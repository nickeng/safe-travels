Three Daysto the Real Sahara

الصحراء الكبرى

A thousand kilometres of rammed earth and mountain pass, ending on a 150-metre dune with the Milky Way directly overhead. November gets it exactly right.

Before you turn the key

The honest shape of this trip

Leaving Marrakech at seven in the morning, the Haouz plain is still grey and the orange sellers are hosing down the square behind you. Four hours later you are at 2,260 metres on the tizi, watching honey sellers wave jars at the traffic while the High Atlas falls away in stripes of rust, ochre and white. By dusk the earth has turned to something else entirely: ksour the colour of the ground they stand on, palm groves running along dry riverbeds, and a horizon that keeps flattening. On the second evening you climb a dune barefoot, and the only sound is the sand moving under someone else's feet forty metres away.

That is the trip. It is also, unavoidably, a driving trip — roughly 1,000 km round-trip and something like 23–25 hours behind glass over three days. Anyone who tells you the Sahara is a comfortable long-weekend hop from Marrakech is selling you the Zagora dunes, which are a shallow, scrubby warm-up an hour off the main road. The dunes in the photographs — Erg Chebbi, 150–180 m of apricot sand near Merzouga — are 560 km away, and three days is the honest minimum to reach them and get back.

The good news is that November may be the best month of the year to do it. The summer furnace has gone: Erg Chebbi runs 23–25 °C by day, warm enough to climb a dune in a t-shirt, while nights drop to 7–10 °C. Crowds are a fraction of the spring peak, riads run 15–25% below their April rates, the light is low and golden rather than bleached, and the date harvest has just finished so the roadside stalls from Erfoud south are piled with fresh tamr. The one thing November asks of you is a fleece.

Shared minibus or private car?

This is the decision that shapes everything else. A shared group tour (8–17 people) runs €85–160 per person and follows a fixed clock — 20 minutes at Todra whether or not the light is good. A private tour for two is €200–260 per person and buys you the thing that actually matters here: the ability to stop. Aït Benhaddou at 9:30 am before the coaches, an extra hour at the Dadès switchbacks, a detour to Fint Oasis.

Self-driving is entirely feasible — the N9 and N10 are paved the whole way and no 4×4 is needed — but you take on the camp booking, the camel arrangements and two long days at the wheel. For a couple on three days, a private driver is the sweet spot: you keep the flexibility and get to look out of the window.

Whatever you book, book it weeks ahead for November. Moroccan domestic tourism fills the south around the two national holidays (see below), and the best-positioned camps go first.

Must-knows

  • Cash, in dirhams. Card is rarely accepted once you leave Marrakech. ATMs in Merzouga are unreliable — draw what you need in Marrakech, Ouarzazate or Erfoud.
  • Tips are part of the price. Budget ~€5–10 per person for your driver-guide for the trip, and the same again for the camel handler.
  • Fill up in Marrakech. Fuel stops on the Tizi n'Tichka are sparse and sometimes closed.
  • Download offline maps. Signal vanishes inside the gorges and across the dunes.
  • Speed traps. Limits drop to 60 km/h through mountain villages and are enforced with hidden cameras.
  • Day 3 is brutal — 9–10 hours, arriving Marrakech 8–10 pm. Do not book a flight for the following morning.

Timing the month

Two flags, one film festival

If your dates are still loose, three things in November are worth steering around — or towards. None of them close the desert, but two of them fill the hotels and one of them turns Jemaa el-Fna into an open-air cinema.

Green March Day المسيرة الخضراء

6 November. Morocco's most politically charged holiday, marking the 1975 march of 350,000 unarmed civilians south into the Western Sahara. The King addresses the nation from Rabat; flags go up across the medina, and in a regional capital like Ouarzazate there is a modest civic parade along Avenue Mohammed V. It is genuinely felt rather than performed for visitors.

Independence Day عيد الاستقلال

18 November. Quieter and more reflective — flag-flying, school ceremonies and family gatherings, marking Mohammed V's return from exile and independence from France in 1956. In southern market towns like Boumalne Dadès and Tinghir you may catch a small ceremony at the local government building in the morning.

What it means for you: banks and government offices close on both dates; petrol stations, guesthouses, restaurants, the kasbahs and the dune camps all stay open. The real impact is demand — Moroccan domestic tourists fill Marrakech and the south around both dates, and some businesses take the whole intervening week as a de facto break. If your trip lands in that stretch, book your riad, driver and camp several weeks out.

Also worth timing

  • Marrakech International Film Festival — typically the last week of November into early December, with free screenings on a giant screen in Jemaa el-Fna. The square under an open-air cinema, food stalls blazing, is a remarkable last night. Dates shift annually: verify at festivalmarrakech.info.
  • Rissani souk — Tuesday, Thursday and Sunday. The most unfiltered market on the route; time your Day 3 departure through it if the day lands right.
  • Tinghir souk — Monday (large), Sunday (vegetables), Saturday (livestock). A working Amazigh market 2.5 km west of town, on your way to Todra.
  • Friday is couscous day nationwide, and the biggest mechoui day in Marrakech.
  • Olive harvest peaks across the Atlas in November; pressed argan and olive oil are at their freshest.

No major religious holiday falls in the November window in either 2025 or 2026 — Mawlid an-Nabi lands in late August/early September in both years.

The plan

One road south, and back

There is essentially one sensible way to do this in three days: south over the Tizi n'Tichka to Aït Benhaddou, east along the old Route of a Thousand Kasbahs to sleep near the Dadès, then east again through Todra to reach the dunes in time for a sunset camel trek. Day 3 undoes all of it in one long push. Sleep in the sand on night two — that is the night you came for.

Which direction, and why not a loop

You will drive out and back on broadly the same road, and that is genuinely the right call on three days — the alternative southern loop via the Drâa Valley and Zagora adds hours without adding dunes worth the trade. If you want a true circuit rather than an out-and-back, that is an argument for adding a day and going to Erg Chigaga instead (see Add a day).

Leg 1 · the night before & the morning after

Marrakech, bookends

Marrakech is not the point of this trip, but it is the frame around it — and half a day at each end is the right dose. Arrive the evening before departure and give yourself exactly one thing: Jemaa el-Fna at dusk. Climb to a café terrace with a mint tea around 5:30 pm, watch the day market turn into the night market below you, then go down and eat in the smoke. It is the best two-hour orientation to Morocco there is. Save the palaces for the afternoon you get back, when you will be too tired to drive anywhere and grateful for a courtyard.

The half-day that works

On your return afternoon, the southern medina makes a tight, unhurried circuit: Bahia Palace at opening for the painted cedar ceilings before the groups arrive, the Saadian Tombs ten minutes' walk away, and the ruined vastness of El Badi in the raking late light — 135 m by 110 m of empty terracotta with storks nesting on the walls. Then a hammam: November's cool evenings make the steam genuinely restorative rather than punishing, and after two days in a car it is the correct decision. A neighbourhood public hammam runs 15–25 MAD; a heritage one with a kessa scrub, 100–350 MAD. Ask your riad which nearby option takes couples.

Courtyards, carving and one blue garden

If you have longer — a spare morning at either end — Marrakech's other register is architectural, and it is worth an hour each. All four of these are indoor or walled, which makes them the right call in the middle of a bright day when the souks are at their most punishing.

Where to eat in the medina

Marrakech's own dish is tanjia, and it exists nowhere else in the same form. Lamb or beef shank goes into an unglazed clay urn with preserved lemon, saffron, cumin, garlic and smen, the neck is sealed with parchment, and the urn is buried in the ash of the furnace that heats the neighbourhood hammam's water. Four hours later the parchment comes off and a peppery citrus steam rushes out. It was bachelor food — the artisans of the souks (tanners, dyers, cobblers) needed no kitchen and no cook, just a few coins for the furnachi. Look for the clay urns lined up at the front of any serious mechoui shop; that is the signal to order.

Get the timing right

Lunch is the main meal in Morocco — 12:30 to 2:30, and it is when the good stuff is cooked. Mechoui Alley sells whole pit-roasted lamb by weight from about noon and is gone by early afternoon: arrive by 12:30. Dinner runs late — 8 pm is normal, 9 pm unremarkable — and the Jemaa el-Fna stalls set up around 6 pm and peak from 8 to 10. Confirm a price before you sit down at a stall; a grilled plate should not be 150 MAD.

Where to stay, and why it matters here

A riad is the whole point of sleeping in Marrakech: blank windowless walls on the alley, then a door, and inside a tiled courtyard with citrus trees and a rooftop where the dawn call to prayer arrives from three directions at once. Practically, your host is also your fixer — they will recommend a desert operator, arrange the 7 am taxi to your pickup point, and walk you in from the nearest gate on the first night, because no taxi enters the derbs and no address is signposted. For November, a riad with a fireplace earns its keep. The northern medina near Jemaa el-Fna puts you in the thick of it; the quieter Kasbah and Mellah quarter to the south runs 15–25% cheaper and makes an early departure less painful.

196km · 4 h

South out of the Haouz plain, the N9 begins to climb almost immediately. Cedar and scrub give way to bare geology — red, ochre and white striated rock — and then you are on the Tizi n'Tichka, the highest major pass in North Africa. Over the top, everything changes: the descent runs down toward the pre-Saharan plain, and the buildings stop being pink and start being the exact colour of the dirt around them.

Leg 2 · over the High Atlas

The pass, and the great ksar

This is the most cinematic driving day in Morocco, and the one place on the route where the journey genuinely outranks the destination — though the destination happens to be a UNESCO World Heritage site you have already seen in Gladiator, Lawrence of Arabia and Game of Thrones.

The pass tops out at 2,260 m. There is a wide parking area at the summit sign where honey sellers set up — allow twenty minutes, because it is the best panorama on the road. In November the upper slopes may carry early snow; the road itself is paved and maintained, and the Gendarmerie only closes the col when ice makes it genuinely dangerous, which is uncommon before December. Locals overtake aggressively on the switchbacks: keep your distance and use the pull-outs when trucks need past.

The detour that transforms the day

About 6 km south of the summit, a signposted turn onto the RP1506 runs 21 km to Telouet Kasbah — the abandoned palace of the Glaoui, the Berber dynasty that ran the whole south and helped depose a sultan. Craftsmen from Fes tiled its reception halls floor-to-ceiling; when independence came in 1956 the wealth was confiscated and the place was simply left. Two rooms still hold their zellij, carved plaster and gilded cedar ceilings; the rest is collapsing into mud in real time, and nobody is restoring it. The guardian charges 20–30 MAD and shows you round. It adds about 90 minutes and it is the most atmospheric stop on the route. Note the 2023 earthquake did further damage — some areas may be closed.

💡 Aït Benhaddou, timed properly. The coaches arrive from mid-morning and leave by about 4 pm. If your driver can get you there by 9:30 am, take it — otherwise stay into the late afternoon, when the mud-brick goes amber and the ksar empties out. Cross on the big footbridge (free); the small bridge routes you through a paid museum gate. Climb all the way to the agadir at the top, 20–30 minutes, for the valley view. Sturdy shoes: the lanes are clay, low-arched and slippery.

115km · 2 h

Past Ouarzazate the road becomes the old Route of a Thousand Kasbahs, running east along the N10 through Skoura's palm groves and the rose town of Kalaat M'Gouna. The Atlas is behind you now; the country ahead is flat-bottomed valleys, oasis green in a channel of bare rust rock, and a fortified earthen village every few kilometres.

Leg 3 · the valley of a thousand kasbahs

Two gorges, one night

The Dadès and the Todra are complementary rather than competing, and the classic itinerary gives you one of each: sleep near the Dadès, drive the Todra next morning. Dadès is wider and more layered — rust sandstone, irrigated valley floor, ruined ksour on the ridges, and the most photographed hairpin road in Morocco. Todra does the opposite: it concentrates, until 300-metre limestone walls close to a ten-metre gap shared by the road and a cold snowmelt river.

Along the Dadès

The gorge road peels north off the N10 at Boumalne Dadès and is paved and driveable in any car. Eighteen kilometres in, the eroded rock towers called the Monkey Fingers (Doigts de Singe) rise straight out of the valley floor near Tamellalt village — a roadside stop, and the trailhead for the best short hike in the gorge. Another twelve kilometres and you reach the switchbacks; the terrace of the Café-Restaurant Timzillite sits at the top of them, and the view back down the spiralling road is the shot. Order a mint tea rather than lunch.

Into the Todra

Enter from Tinghir, 15 km north on the R703, through the Palmeraie de Tinghir — 14 km of date palms, figs and pomegranates threaded by ancient khettara channels, worth a walk in its own right. The narrowest 600-metre section of the gorge is roughly 12 km up. Go very early or after 5 pm to have it to yourself; in November the lower sun angle actually puts more light on the canyon floor for longer than in summer. The walls hold over 400 bolted climbing routes and you can watch climbers as specks from the road without leaving the tarmac.

A navigation trap

Searching "Todra Gorge" in Google Maps drops you in Tinghir town, not the gorge. Search "Gorges du Todgha" instead. And do not attempt the 40 km mountain piste linking M'Semrir at the top of the Dadès across to Todra — it is 4×4-only and flash-flood-prone. Backtrack to the N10.

Where to sleep on night one

You have a genuine choice here, and it is a trade of drama against logistics. Skoura, in a dense palmeraie right on the N10, costs you no detour at all and has the most atmospheric kasbah guesthouses in the corridor — fireside lounges, garden-to-table food, total silence. The Dadès Gorge means waking up inside a canyon with pink walls above you and a trail from the door, but it is a dead-end 45–60 minute round-trip detour off the main road, and you will be driving out before sunrise to make Merzouga by afternoon.

Eating on the road

South of the Atlas the tagine changes. The Marrakchi version leans sweet-savoury — lamb with prunes and almonds, or preserved lemon and olives. The Berber tagine of the mountains and the south is plainer and better for it: no dried fruit, no preserved lemon, just meat with seasonal vegetables built into a careful cone around it, cumin, paprika, olive oil, and time. In the villages along this stretch there are almost no independent restaurants — dinner is at your guesthouse, cooked by the family, and that is the authentic version rather than a compromise.

250km · 4–5 h

East from Tinghir the road runs out through Tinjdad and the date-palm oases of the Ziz Valley, then Erfoud, then Rissani — the last real town, once the terminus of the caravan route from Timbuktu. After that the tarmac keeps going but the country empties: stone hammada, low black hills, and then, impossibly, a wall of orange sand on the left horizon that keeps getting taller.

Leg 4 · Erg Chebbi, Merzouga

The night in the sand

Everything else on this route is a preamble. Erg Chebbi is a 50-kilometre arc of wind-built sand rising to 150–180 metres — the highest dunes in Morocco — piled against the Algerian border, and you reach the edge of it from the main street of Merzouga village. The standard arrival is by dromedary at dusk: 60 to 90 minutes swaying at four kilometres an hour, with the sand going from gold to deep ochre to red as the sun drops behind you.

At camp there is a communal tent, harira and tagine eaten by candlelight, and then drumming around a fire — the real thing, played by staff who grew up with the music rather than a nightly floor show. Afterwards someone kills the lights. In November, with almost no light pollution for tens of kilometres, the Milky Way comes up plainly overhead and Orion climbs over the dune crests. One traveller camping here in October watched a jerboa come right up to the dinner table.

Getting the camp choice right

This is where the trip is won or lost, and photographs are almost useless — a large share of "authentic" camps online are identical mid-tier operations with the same khaki tents and the same decorative pom-poms. Two things actually predict a good night: the camp is Berber-owned and run by people who grew up in or near the dunes (which also keeps the money in Merzouga and Hassi Labied), and it has been operating for years rather than a season.

For a couple whose stated goal is the real desert, a well-reviewed standard or mid-range camp is the better buy — the setting does all the heavy lifting, and the communal tent is where the evening actually happens. Spend the money on position, not plumbing.

Booking, honestly

  • All legitimate camps now sit at the dune perimeter. The government cleared the interior camps around 2019. Any operator claiming a camp "deep in the dunes" is stretching it — the camel ride is the "deep in" part.
  • Book direct. Most camps take WhatsApp bookings from their Booking.com listing; it is usually cheaper and the money goes to the family rather than an aggregator.
  • Don't book at the gate. Turning up in Merzouga to negotiate invites a pushy scrum. Message ahead and confirm whether the camel ride is included.
  • Ask which side. Camps on the western approach face east — better for sunrise. Eastern-fringe camps face west — better for sunset. It genuinely changes your morning.
  • Camel or 4×4? The camel ride is the experience, but it is 60–90 minutes each way on an animal at walking pace. With any back or knee trouble, ask for the 4×4 transfer — 20 minutes to the same spot.

Beyond the dunes themselves

Most people treat Merzouga as one night and a camel. If your schedule gives you a morning, two things nearby are worth more than another hour on the sand — a Gnawa music village with roots in sub-Saharan Africa, and a seasonal salt lake that in November has just started to fill.

Sleeping out, and the cold

Most camps will lay a mattress on the open sand on request — the most cinematic option, and you will be cold by 2 am unless your kit is good. The compromise most couples land on is a tent with the flap open facing the dunes. Nights run 7–10 °C, and late November can touch 7 °C by 3 am; standard camps hand out wool blankets, and travellers consistently report they are not quite enough. This is what the fleece and the liner are for.

Shooting the dunes

The light is only genuinely good twice a day, and both windows are short. Twenty to forty minutes after sunrise, and forty to twenty minutes before sunset, the sun sits 10–20° above the horizon and rakes deep shadow into the dune valleys — that is when the ridgelines read as lines rather than mush. Climb higher than your subject: to silhouette a camel caravan, get onto a dune above the ridge the caravan walks along, and you can fill the frame with nothing but sand and shadow, no sky at all. A telephoto compresses the stacked ridges into abstraction. For the Milky Way, check the moon phase before you fix your date — it matters more than anything else — and reckon on f/1.8–2.8, a wide lens, and a tripod anchored with your bag in the sand.

Day 3 · the long way home

What to see on the way out

Day 3 is nine to ten hours of driving, so the honest advice is to be selective: pick one stop, not three. Wake for sunrise on the dunes — genuinely worth the 5:30 alarm, and the better light of the two — then camel or drive back out, and put your one stop in early while you still have the appetite for it.

If your departure lands on a Tuesday, Thursday or Sunday, make it Rissani: a working market with rahbas for livestock, dates, spices and metalwork, where prices are in dirhams and nobody is expecting to bargain in French. Otherwise make it Erfoud, where the plateaus are 375-million-year-old Devonian seabed and the workshops cut ammonites, trilobites and orthoceras out of black marble on diamond saws — you can buy a polished specimen from the family that found it for a fraction of Marrakech prices. Either way, eat medfouna before you go: the local stuffed flatbread, spiced meat and onion sealed in dough and baked in a fire pit, served in wedges on a wooden board. Order it the moment you sit down; it takes 30–60 minutes.

If you can find a fourth day

Three ways to stretch it

None of these fit inside three days. All three are worth knowing about, because the difference a single extra day makes on this route is disproportionate — and because the first one is a genuinely different Sahara.

The wilder alternative — swap, don't add

Erg Chigaga & M'Hamid El Ghizlane

4×4 only · 8–9 h drive + 1.5–4 h piste · needs 4 days minimum

If Erg Chebbi is the Sahara you imagine, Erg Chigaga is the one you feel. It is Morocco's largest dune field — roughly 40 km by 15 — and it has no road at all: the last stretch from M'Hamid El Ghizlane, where the tarmac simply ends, is one and a half to four hours of 4×4 piste across stony hammada, dry riverbeds and the cracked white pan of Lake Iriki. That single fact changes everything. Where Chebbi may have a dozen tour groups riding camels at once in November, Chigaga has perhaps twenty or thirty camps in total, set inside the dunes rather than along the edge, and you may see one other group all day. One operator rates the sky here Bortle Class 2 — dark enough that the galactic core shows colour to the naked eye.

The approach is better too: beyond Ouarzazate the road drops into the Drâa Valley, 200 km of date palms, irrigated fields and earthen villages, with the date harvest just finished. Along the way, Tamegroute holds a 17th-century Sufi library of some 4,000 manuscripts — Koranic commentaries, astronomical tables, one written on a dried leaf — next door to the workshops making the emerald-green pottery found nowhere else in Morocco.

The catch is time. Marrakech to M'Hamid is a comparable 8–9 hours of driving, plus the off-road leg — so this is a 4-day trip, and sources are consistent that squeezing it into three spends the extra remoteness on vehicle hours instead of dunes. Doing both ergs would need about six days. If you can add a day, take Chigaga instead of Chebbi. If you can add two, the strong play is the Foum Zguid loop: in via M'Hamid, out via the 90 km northwest piste across the full Iriki pan, so you never retrace the Ouarzazate road.

💡 Two things to verify, and one to ignore. The Taragalte Festival — Tuareg desert blues, Gnawa and Malian groups at a dune camp near M'Hamid, deliberately small at a few thousand people — usually falls right at the turn of the month (2025 ran 30 Oct–2 Nov). If your dates catch it, it is a strong argument for coming here, but 2026 dates were still only projected at research time and one site contradicts them, so confirm on the official channels and book early. And ignore any operator claiming 300-metre dunes at Chigaga: that figure is marketing inflation, repeated across at least seven tour sites. Chigaga's crests run 60–100 m. It is the bigger erg, but Erg Chebbi has the taller dunes. Sell yourself on the emptiness, not the height.

Never attempt the piste in a rental car — standard agreements forbid it, there is no signage, and vehicles have been stranded out there. Licensed local driver with a proper 4×4, no exceptions. No phone signal past M'Hamid; ATMs are in Zagora, not M'Hamid.

Half a day, on the route you're already driving

Fint Oasis & the Ounila Valley back road

Ouarzazate area · 40 min each way · half day

Two detours near Ouarzazate that almost nobody on a three-day tour sees. Fint Oasis is a palm-fringed river cutting through bare rock hills 40 minutes south of town on a gravel track — four mud villages strung along the water, women working the fields, children on donkeys, turtles sunbathing on the far rocks. It is doable slowly in a 2WD, and there is a terrace serving mint tea over the palms.

The other is the P1506 through the Ounila Valley — the old caravan route, paved the whole way and fine in a normal car, running north from Aït Benhaddou toward Telouet past small kasbahs, ochre-red settlements and palm groves, with almost no traffic. Ten minutes up it, Kasbah Tamdaght has storks nesting on its towers and effectively no visitors — a Glaoui ruin since the 2023 earthquake, so plan on exteriors. Taken as your approach to Aït Benhaddou instead of the N9, it adds roughly half a day and turns the transit into the best drive of the trip. With a 4×4 you can instead take the unsurfaced 70 km valley track and arrive at the back of the ksar, past the almost unvisited ksar of Anmiter — which is what Aït Benhaddou looks like without the visitors.

A morning, if you sleep at the gorge

The traverse above the Todra walls

Tinghir · 3 h loop · guide recommended

Almost everyone drives into the Todra, looks up, and drives out. Above the gorge there is a high traverse that looks straight down the canyon — a three-hour loop from the northern entrance up to a pass and down to the palm village of Tizgui, finishing along a very thin stone walkway. One traveller who hiked it in November called it perfect weather for it, and you will likely have the whole thing to yourself. The honest caveat from the same account: there isn't really a marked trail, and she wouldn't do it again without a guide — your kasbah can arrange one. Summer is too hot for this and December through February can be snowy, which makes November close to the last comfortable window.
