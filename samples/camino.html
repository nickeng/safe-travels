Sarriato Santiago

Six days through wet Galician granite, chestnut lanes and eucalyptus fog — the shortest walk that still earns you a Compostela, taken at the season's most beautiful and most sodden.

Before you start

The shortest legitimate Camino

Sarria is where the Camino stops being a rumour and becomes a queue. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago will only issue a Compostela to someone who has walked at least 100 continuous kilometres on a recognised route — and this small Lugo market town sits just far enough out to clear the bar. In 2024, over 150,000 people began here: nearly 30% of everyone who claimed the certificate. You will not be walking alone.

What saves it is Galicia itself, and October. The trail out of Sarria drops almost immediately into corredoiras — farm tracks running between mossy stone walls, roofed over by oak and chestnut turning copper. Cows are herded down the path in front of you. Roadside donativo tables sell jam and painted shells beside an honesty box. Then the forest changes: eucalyptus plantations, silver-barked and overwhelming after rain, all the way to the airport fence and the city beyond.

October is the month this landscape looks most like itself and treats you worst. Galicia's autumn rains arrive: reckon on rain on roughly half the days, mud on every descent, and the smell of wet eucalyptus in your clothes for a week. In exchange you get thinned crowds, chestnuts roasting on street corners from mid-month, low amber light through the canopy, and — at Portomarín — a reservoir dropped low enough that the drowned medieval town begins to surface.

The one rule that matters

From Sarria onward you must collect at least two sellos (stamps) per day in your credencial — not one. Bars, churches, albergues, honesty stalls and the odd vending machine all stamp. Miss the quota and the Compostela can be refused. Buy the credencial in Sarria for about €2 at the Mosteiro da Madalena, most albergues, or a church on Rúa Maior.

The Pilgrim's Office itself publishes no per-day number — only that the credencial must be "duly stamped along the route travelled." Two a day is the universal practice on the last 100 km, and the safe assumption.

The shape of the walk

Nothing here is mountainous — the hardest climb is the long pull out of Portomarín up onto the Sierra Ligonde, about 580 m of ascent spread over 25 km. What tires people is the surface: after rain these tracks turn to slick clay, and the two big descents (into Portomarín, and down to the Río Iso at Ribadiso) are where poles earn their weight.

Six days, and why they break here

The classic guidebook split does this route in five long stages. Six is better, and not because you're slow: it lets you cut the brutal 29 km Palas de Rei→Arzúa day in half, take lunch in Melide's pulperías without racing a clock, and sleep at Ribadiso, the loveliest stop on the route, which the five-stage version walks straight past.

October tightens around you

Two things change measurably over the month, and both matter more to a walker than to a tourist. Daylight collapses by almost an hour and a half, and on 25 October the clocks go back — overnight, the evening you'd been using to reach a bed disappears. And the odds of a wet day climb steadily from roughly a third to well over two-fifths.

The practical upshot: early October is genuinely pleasant walking with long evenings; late October means leaving O Pedrouzo in full darkness and finishing your day under lights. A headtorch is not optional in either half of the month — most pilgrims leave for Santiago between 06:00 and 07:00.

Four dates worth planning around

When in October to go

If your dates aren't fixed yet, October's calendar gives you a real reason to choose one week over another. The biggest event on the whole month isn't in Santiago at all — it's in Lugo, 40 minutes from your starting line, and it happens to bracket the days most people arrive in Sarria.

San Froilán, Lugo · 4–12 OCT

Galicia's largest public celebration, running since 1754 and classed a Festivity of National Tourist Interest. For nine days the entire walled Roman city becomes festival ground: cabezudos lurching through the lanes, wandering charangas striking up at all hours, hundreds of thousands of visitors, and octopus and empanada stalls set up inside the 2,117-metre circuit of Roman wall — the only city on earth still completely encircled by its original one.

It is 35–45 minutes by bus from Sarria (~€4–6), which makes it a genuine option on the day you arrive to collect your credencial rather than a diversion. Two days stand out: O Domingo das Mozas, the Sunday after the 5th, when traditional Galician dress and folk culture take over the old city and is the most visually spectacular day for a visitor; and the 12 October Medieval Fair, when the cathedral square and the wall fill with troubadours, falconry, crafts and a banquet, closing with fireworks.

How to use it

Arrive in Sarria a day early, take the morning bus to Lugo, walk the full circuit of the Roman wall, eat free festival octopus, come back on an evening bus and start walking the next morning. It costs you one day and gives you the Galicia that exists outside the Camino.

Spain's tourism board lists 2026 dates as "to be confirmed" — the festival has run 4–12 October for decades. Check the official Lugo programme at lugo.gal before booking. Lugo fills completely; do not plan to sleep there without a reservation.

Día de la Hispanidad

Mon 12 October · national holiday

Spain's national day, and the date that will actually reshape your walking day. Pharmacies, banks and most village shops close; albergues and pilgrim bars stay open. If you'll be between Arzúa and O Pedrouzo on the 12th, buy medication, snacks and cash the day before. ATMs still work. The trail itself is quieter — fewer day-trippers.

Magosto

Late October → mid-November

The Galician chestnut festival — fires lit in village squares and albergue patios, castañas asadas shared out with viño novo from the just-finished harvest, and sometimes a queimada. Walk in the second half of October and you'll smell chestnuts roasting in Sarria, Melide and Arzúa in the late afternoon. Some albergues host a magosto for pilgrims — ask your host.

The Botafumeiro problem

Not scheduled in October · next: 1 November

The Cathedral swings its 53 kg thurible only on a fixed list of solemnities, and October contains none of them. The next guaranteed swing is All Saints, 1 November. It does still fly unannounced when a pilgrimage group has requested and paid for it — so attending Mass is a lottery, not a plan. If seeing it matters enormously, let your trip run a few days into November.

Also worth knowing: Sarria's own Campo da Feira traditional fair falls on the 6th, 20th and 27th of every month, with pulpo á feira boiled outdoors in copper cauldrons — the most authentic version of the dish you'll eat all week, and two of those dates land squarely in a typical arrival window.

Day 0 · Sarria · the start line

An afternoon on Rúa Maior

Sarria is two towns stacked on a hill. Down by the station it is workaday Lugo province — supermarkets, a pharmacy, a bank. Climb the steps and you are on Rúa Maior, a few hundred metres of cobbles and stone houses that contains, in sequence, every albergue, every pilgrim menu, an antique dealer or two, and the churches that will sell you a credencial for €2. The whole circuit — monastery, castle tower, San Salvador, the river — is an unhurried half-day. Do it, sort your logistics, eat properly, and sleep early.

Arrival-day checklist

1. Buy or present your credencial at the Mosteiro da Madalena or an albergue. 2. Get your first stamp there and a second at the Tourist Office in the old prison on Rúa da Mercé — the two-a-day count starts here. 3. Buy tomorrow's breakfast: bakeries open 07:00–07:30 and the first bar out of town may not. 4. If you're using Correos to move your bag, arrange it tonight.

Bed & board in Sarria

Rúa Maior is lined with albergues; the choice is really between the government-run Xunta bunk (cheap, no reservations, opens 13:00) and a private room to shake off the journey before day one. Because Sarria is the single most common starting point on the entire Camino, this is one of the two nights worth booking from home.

If you arrive a day early

Two excursions justify an extra night, and both are more than filler. Neither requires your pack.

Day 1 · Sarria → Portomarín · 22.2 km · ↑480 m · 5–7 hrs

Into the corredoiras

You leave up Rúa Maior, past the giant white SARRIA letters set into the hillside — the obligatory departure photograph — and within ten minutes you cross the Ponte Áspera, a low-arched stone bridge on Roman foundations with mist coming off the stream beneath it. From there the Camino does what it will do all week: it becomes a farm track between dry-stone walls, arched over by oak and chestnut, and you share it with cattle.

Barbadelo at 4 km is the first stop that matters and the first stamp of the walk. Then a long, gentle rise through hamlets — Mercado da Serra, once a medieval market; Morgade; and about 1.6 km beyond it, at the roadside, the 100 km mojón. It is covered in graffiti and it will be surrounded by people photographing it. Stop anyway. From here the numbers start dropping alarmingly fast. After Ferreiros the track tips downhill for the last stretch, crosses the long bridge over the Miño reservoir, and delivers you to a staircase climbing into Portomarín.

Arriving in Portomarín

Portomarín: bed & board

Everyone who started in Sarria arrives here on the same afternoon, which makes Portomarín the tightest bed market on the route. Aim to be in town by 13:30 if you want a Xunta bunk. Reward: the best-looking town on the walk, a fortress church in the square, and — in an October with the reservoir low — the drowned village emerging below the bridge.

Day 2 · Portomarín → Palas de Rei · 25.7 km · ↑580 m · 6–7 hrs

Up onto the sierra

The longest day and the only one with a real climb. It begins in the dark, in a file of pilgrims descending Portomarín's stone streets with the street lamps still on and breath visible — the single best photograph of the week, if you turn around at the bottom of the hill. Then the road tips up, and stays up, for the better part of three hours: through Gonzar, past the turning for Castromaior, over Hospital da Cruz and across the open Sierra Ligonde at around 720 m, the highest ground you'll cross.

This is the stretch where Galicia's older layers show. Castromaior is an Iron Age hillfort from around 400 BCE — walls, ditches and enclosures still legible — sitting on the hill directly above the hamlet, and almost every pilgrim walks past without climbing the twenty minutes to see it. In Airexe and elsewhere you'll start noticing ceramic meigas hanging in windows, and a bar or two decorated entirely with them. The Galician saying goes: eu non creo nas meigas, pero habelas, hainas — I don't believe in witches, but they exist.

Palas de Rei itself is a functional town rather than a lovely one, named for the Visigothic king Witiza and described as a key pilgrim hub in the 12th-century Codex Calixtinus. Its roofed main square has the best restaurants of the day.

Water and food discipline

There are no services for roughly the first 8 km out of Portomarín. Fill your bottle, and buy a slice of empanada the night before — this is the one stretch of the whole route where getting it wrong actually hurts.

The only donativo on the last 100 km

At Ligonde, about 7 km before Palas de Rei, Albergue La Fuente del Peregrino is the sole pay-what-you-can albergue in the final hundred: 11 beds, Christian-run, no alcohol, April–November. Closed Tuesdays and Wednesdays — call +34 687 550 527 the day before. If the donativo experience is part of why you're walking, this is your one chance, and it means a short 18 km day from Portomarín.

Or split the stage

Eirexe / Airexe and Portos both have beds around the 15–19 km mark if 25.7 km with 580 m of climb in the wet feels like too much.

Palas de Rei: bed & board

Over 500 beds across a dozen options means you'll find something in October even walking in unannounced. The Xunta was renovated in 2022; the more interesting choice is a small albergue with a communal table, because after this day you want to be fed rather than to go looking for food.

Day 3 · Palas de Rei → Melide · 14.5 km · 3–4 hrs

A short day, on purpose

Fourteen and a half kilometres is a morning, and that is the entire point: you want to be standing at a pulpería counter in Melide at one o'clock with the whole afternoon in front of you. Walk out of Lugo province into A Coruña, through woods and small holdings, and take the two set pieces slowly.

The first is O Leboreiro, a hamlet of about six houses where a tiny 12th-century Romanesque church stands directly opposite a cabazo — a circular stone granary on legs. Church and granary in one frame is the most Galician composition on the route. Just beyond, the Camino crosses the old flat-slab bridge over the Seco stream on medieval paving. The second is Furelos, 2 km short of Melide, whose medieval bridge is widely called the most beautiful on the whole Camino Francés; the village predates the Romans, and in the Middle Ages belonged to the Knights of Saint John. Cross it, then turn and look back.

Melide is where the Camino Primitivo — the original Way, walked from Oviedo since the 9th century — comes in from the north and merges with the Francés. From here the trail is fuller, and the conversations change: you'll meet people who have been walking through Asturias for a fortnight.

Today's whole reason

Melide is the undisputed capital of pulpo á feira — and inland Galicia has been cooking octopus in copper cauldrons at its fairs since at least the 18th century. The pulperías are on and around Avenida de Lugo where the Camino meets the main road. Arrive between 13:00 and 14:00 for the freshest cook; that's also when the queue peaks, which tells you everything. Full details in Eating in Galicia.

The two pulperías

Melide: bed & board

The largest town after Sarria, with full services and — unusually — a bed surplus: the Xunta alone has 150 places and is essentially never full in October, though the dorms are cramped. There is a fine piece of Camino symmetry available here: Albergue Ezequiel is run by the same family as the pulpería, so you can eat and sleep under one roof.

Day 4 · Melide → Ribadiso · 12.5 km · 3 hrs

Ribadiso Paraíso

The gentlest day of the six, and the one most guidebooks delete. Melide to Ribadiso is twelve and a half kilometres of oak and eucalyptus with two river crossings, through Boente and Castañeda, and it ends at a hamlet of twelve inhabitants on the Río Iso where a single-arched 13th-century bridge carries the Camino across the water.

Pilgrims call it Ribadiso Paraíso. The Xunta albergue here occupies the restored Hospital de San Antón, a medieval pilgrim hospice documented on this site since at least 572 AD, with a garden running down to the river. The ritual is unchanged and unimprovable: arrive early, drop the pack, and sit on the bank with your feet in cold water. American Pilgrims on the Camino staff the place with volunteer hospitaleros through 31 October, which makes the welcome noticeably warmer than a municipal bunkhouse has any right to be.

Because the albergue takes no reservations and has 60 beds, arriving by early afternoon on a short day is exactly the right strategy — and it's why this stage is short rather than pushed on to Arzúa.

Or push on 3 km to Arzúa

Ribadiso has a riverside setting, one mesón and almost nothing else. Arzúa, three kilometres further, has supermarkets, pharmacies, a lively bar scene and the cheese. If you want services, or you couldn't get a Ribadiso bunk, walk on — it makes tomorrow 19.5 km instead of 22.5 km.

The compromise most people miss: sleep at Ribadiso, then walk into Arzúa first thing tomorrow for breakfast and to buy queixo Arzúa-Ulloa for the trail. Best of both.

Ribadiso & Arzúa: bed & board

Day 5 · Ribadiso → O Pedrouzo · 22.5 km · 5–6 hrs

The eucalyptus corridor

Cross the bridge, climb into Arzúa for coffee and cheese, and then spend the rest of the day in forest. Take the left fork out of town onto the woodland option and the Camino becomes a green tunnel — eucalyptus above all, with oak in the wetter hollows, the path deep in fallen leaves and the smell after rain so strong it's almost medicinal. This is the least "sightseeing" day of the walk and, for a lot of people, the one they remember. There's little to do but walk.

Arzúa is worth an hour rather than a night if you slept at Ribadiso: the Camino del Norte merges here, the cheese shops open early, and the town gives its name to Arzúa-Ulloa, a protected-origin cow's-milk cheese — cylindrical, ivory-pale, mildly tangy under a thin elastic rind. Buy a wedge. It travels for a day in a pack and turns a bench in the woods into lunch.

Where to break it

Salceda, 4 km short of O Pedrouzo, has three small private albergues (8–20 beds, no Xunta, no pharmacy, no supermarket) and is the low-key option if you want a shorter final push into Santiago. Do not plan to sleep at Monte do Gozo, 5 km from the Cathedral — the huge albergue there gets consistently poor reports.

O Pedrouzo: the last pilgrim night

O Pedrouzo — signed as Arca do Pino too — is not beautiful, but it is where the energy of the whole route concentrates. Everyone in town tomorrow morning is walking into Santiago, and everyone knows it. Beds fill through the afternoon: arrive by 14:00 for the Xunta. Then find the bakery, because at six tomorrow morning there will be a queue outside it in the dark.

Day 6 · O Pedrouzo → Santiago · 19.9 km · 4–5 hrs

Twenty kilometres to Compostela

Set the alarm for five. Most pilgrims leave O Pedrouzo between 06:00 and 07:00 to reach the Cathedral in time for the noon Mass, which means the first hour or more is walked by headtorch through forest — easy ground, well waymarked, and quietly extraordinary with a line of small lights moving through the trees ahead of you.

The route skirts the airport perimeter — there is a gift stall by the fence with a sello all of its own, coveted precisely because it's absurd — and drops through Lavacolla, where medieval pilgrims stopped to wash before presenting themselves at the Apostle's tomb. Then the long rise to Monte do Gozo, the Mount of Joy, from which pilgrims have caught their first sight of the Cathedral spires for the better part of a thousand years. Most people walk straight past the bronze pilgrim statues on the crest without seeing them; a short detour through the park finds them, and you rejoin the arrows through the albergue grounds without backtracking. In October cloud, the spires may simply not be there. That happens, and the hill is still the hill.

From Monte do Gozo it is 4.7 km of suburb and street, and then you come through the passageway under the Pazo de Raxoi and the square opens up in front of you.

Praza do Obradoiro · the last hundred metres

Arriving, and the paperwork of joy

You will hear it before you see it. A gaita playing under the archway of the Pazo de Raxoi, the sound funnelled and amplified by the stone tunnel, and then the passage opens and the Baroque west façade of the Cathedral fills the entire far end of an enormous granite square. People drop their packs. Some lie flat on the paving. Some cry. Nobody is embarrassed, because everyone in the square walked here.

Then the practical business begins, and it's worth knowing the order of it, because the two things that genuinely need doing today are your bed and — if you're leaving within 24 hours — your Compostela. Everything else can wait a day.

The trap at the Cathedral door

Backpacks are not allowed inside the Cathedral, for security. Bags over 40×40 cm are barred from the Museum too, along with umbrellas and — pointedly — pilgrim staffs. Walk straight from the square to the noon Mass with your pack on and you will be turned away. Paid storage sits about 100 m from the Cathedral (roughly €3 for 24 hours), and bags can also be left at the Post Office on Rúa do Franco.

The Pilgrim's Mass

Four times daily at the high altar — 07:30, 09:30, 12:00 and 19:30 — with the nationalities and starting points of newly arrived pilgrims read aloud. Seating is first come, first served, and the noon service is by far the busiest; arrive well early or expect to stand at the back. If you want your country and start point announced, the mechanism is: collect the Compostela first, then register at the Pilgrim's Office before 11:00 — which in practice means the morning after you arrive.

There is also a quieter English-language Mass at 10:30 daily except Wednesdays, in the Pilgrim's Office chapel, which many solo walkers find lands better than the crush in the nave.

Cathedral renovation has at times moved pilgrim masses to nearby churches (San Francisco, San Fiz de Solovio). Confirm the current schedule at catedraldesantiago.es a day or two out.

Give it two nights, not one

The city after the walk

Santiago is not a shrine with a town attached. It is a working university city of about 101,000 people, roughly 30,000 of them students, and in October term is in full swing — which means the granite lanes you've been walking toward for six days are full of people with no interest whatsoever in your Camino. That is precisely what makes it a good place to land. The arrival is over; now you're just somewhere excellent.

The other reason to stay two nights is weather. This is one of the wettest cities in Spain in one of its wettest months, and a genuinely rainy day here has a very good indoor answer — see the ticket note below.

The Cathedral, deeper

Almost everyone sees the nave, embraces the statue of St James behind the high altar, visits the crypt, and leaves. Two additions are worth much more than that, and one of them is free.

The single best ticket in the city

The roofs ticket (€15, or €12 with an accredited credencial — carry it) also covers the Cathedral Museum, the Gelmírez Palace, the Pórtico da Gloria crypt, MuSar and the Diocesan Museum — and it stays valid for the following seven days. Buy it once and you have covered nearly every indoor rainy-day option in Santiago. Book at visitas.catedraldesantiago.es; entry is timed and punctuality is enforced, with no refunds or exchanges.

Where to look at it from

The Cathedral is best understood from a distance, and the city obliges with a ring of hills. October's low sun and frequent cloud are, for once, an advantage: wet granite under a broken sky beats flat summer glare.

Quieter corners, and the city's own life

The antidote to 1,700 daily arrivals is close at hand — and both experienced guides to this city give the same advice about timing: go out very early, or late at night, when the day-trippers have gone and the streets belong to residents again.

Food culture · what to order · the pulpo capital

Galicia feeds pilgrims the way it feeds its own

Galicia faces the Atlantic rather than the Mediterranean, and its cooking has never quite belonged to the rest of Spain: oceanic, green, Celtic-inflected, and built out of a peasant economy that turned octopus, turnip tops, dried beans, pork fat and chestnuts into something quietly magnificent. Walking here in October puts you in it at exactly the right moment — the wine just harvested, chestnuts roasting on corners, mushrooms in the markets, woodsmoke drifting out of stone farmhouses.

Two clocks, and learning to read them

The friction of eating on the Camino is a collision of rhythms. Galicians eat late — comida from 14:00 to 16:00, cena from 21:00 onward, later than most of Spain. Pilgrims rise at six and are ravenous by noon. The towns on this route have largely adapted: bars pull coffee before 07:00, and pilgrim-facing kitchens open from 12:30 for lunch and 19:00 for dinner. Stray one street off the Camino, or arrive after siesta, and you'll find everything shut with the shutters down.

The single most useful habit to adopt: eat your big meal at lunch. The menú do día is served at comida, not dinner, and it is consistently better value and better cooked than the evening pilgrim menu — which exists mainly because pilgrims need to be asleep by ten.

  • 07:00–10:00Desayuno — five minutes standing at the bar. Café con leche and tostada. Nobody sits down.
  • 10:30–12:00Almorzo — the working second breakfast, and the pilgrim's best meal of the day: drop the pack at the first open bar a few kilometres out, order a wedge of tortilla and a coffee.
  • 14:00–16:00Comida — the main event, three courses, the social anchor of the day. Kitchens close 15:30–16:00.
  • 18:00–20:00Merienda — the useful one if you reached town at four and dinner is hours away. Coffee, a slice of tarta, and in October a paper cone of roast chestnuts from a street stall.
  • 19:00–23:00Cena — pilgrim sitting at 19:00–19:30 in Sarria, Melide and Arzúa; locals arrive at 21:00. Ordering à la carte in the evening is how you eat the real specialities rather than the set menu.

Sundays are the trap: many small-town kitchens close Sunday evening or all day. Plan Sunday dinner in a bigger town — Melide, Arzúa, Santiago — and buy an empanada on Saturday night as insurance.

October is the best month of the year for a good half of this list — grelos come into season, chestnuts ripen, the wild mushrooms arrive, and the new wine appears.

    Dunked three or four times into boiling water to "frighten" it, then cooked long, sliced with scissors onto a round wooden táboa, dressed with smoked pimentón, coarse salt and olive oil. With cachelos and a ceramic cunca of young Ribeiro. Eaten standing.

    Melide is the capital
  • Caldo galegoGalician broth

    Thick, fortifying soup of white fabas, turnip greens, potato and pork fat or chorizo. The definitive cold-weather dish of the region and exactly what a wet 25 km day is asking for.

    October–March
  • Lacón con greloscured pork shoulder with turnip tops

    Boiled front leg of pork — cured, not smoked — with grelos, potato and cachelos. Often called the Galician dish par excellence; more common on a lunch menu than a pilgrim one.

    Grelos: Oct–Feb
  • Empanada galegaflat double-crust pie

    Wheat or cornmeal pastry filled with tuna and tomato, pork loin, chorizo, salt cod or queen scallops. Sold by the slice in every bakery — the single best thing to carry on the trail. Fillings shift as you walk: lamb and chorizo in Lugo, sardine toward A Coruña.

    Buy it the night before
  • Queixo Arzúa-Ulloasoft cow's-milk cheese, PDO

    Made in and around Arzúa, which you walk through on day 5. Cylindrical, ivory paste under a thin elastic yellow rind, mild and lightly tangy. The cheese shops open early; buy a wedge for lunch in the woods.

    Day 5, on the route
  • Queixo San Simón da Costasmoked teardrop cheese, PDO

    Pear-shaped, birch-smoked to an amber rind, semi-hard and buttery-sweet. Find it in the Mercado de Abastos in Santiago; eat it with local honey.

  • Tarta de Santiagoalmond tart of Saint James

    Dense, moist ground-almond cake, dusted through a stencil of the Cross of Saint James. Protected by IGP. Slices along the route; buy a whole one in Santiago as the obvious and correct trophy.

    The reward
  • Castañas asadasroast chestnuts

    Sold in paper cones from street stalls. October is peak chestnut season in Galicia — from mid-month you'll smell them roasting in every town in the late afternoon.

    Mid-Oct → November
  • FilloasGalician crêpes

    Thin crêpes eaten as dessert, sweet or savoury. In autumn, look for them filled with chestnut cream — the version that only exists at this time of year.

    Chestnut: Oct–Dec
  • Pementos de Padrónsmall green peppers, some hot

    Blistered in very hot oil, thrown with coarse salt. Roughly one in ten is fierce, hence the saying: os pementos de Padrón, uns pican e outros non.

    Best June–Oct
  • Albariño & Ribeirothe two whites

    Albariño from Rías Baixas is dry, aromatic, saline — the famous one. But Ribeiro, lighter and rounder from an older inland DO, is the traditional partner to pulpo and the one served in a ceramic cunca. In October ask for viño novo, the new harvest.

    New vintage from Oct
  • Queimadathe fire-drink

    Orujo, sugar, lemon peel and coffee beans set alight in a clay cauldron and stirred while the Conxuro da Queimada — a spell summoning Celtic spirits away — is recited over it. The blue flames caramelise the sugar; you drink it warm from clay cups. Needs arranging ahead, and is inherently a group thing, which for a solo walker is the point.

    Around 31 Oct–1 Nov

The pulpeiras of Melide

The single most important culinary fact about this stretch of the Camino is that it runs through Melide, and Melide is inland. Nowhere near the sea. And yet it has been the unofficial capital of pulpo á feira since long before anyone walked here for fun.

The explanation is the feira — the fair. Since at least the 18th century, pulpeiras — traditionally women — travelled the inland market circuit with copper cauldrons, cooking octopus brought up from the coast for farmers who never saw it. The technique survived the fairs. So did the equipment: copper pots, wooden boards, scissors, and a specific unglamorous ritual — you stand at the bar or sit at a communal wooden table, you drink Ribeiro from a ceramic cup, and there is no menu decision to make.

Two houses are the established names, both a short walk apart on and around Avenida de Lugo where the Camino meets the main road. At A Garnacha a third-generation pulpeiro works the octopus in the front window. Ezequiel is the older institution, and the family also runs a 20-bed albergue — so eating and sleeping in the same building is genuinely on the table.

Also on this route: Sarria's own Campo da Feira cooks it outdoors in copper on the 6th, 20th and 27th of the month — arguably the most authentic version you can eat, if your dates land right.

Ordering pulpo, correctly

  • Ask for a ración (€10–14). A media ración exists and is plenty for one.
  • Say yes to cachelos — boiled potatoes under the octopus, soaking up the oil and paprika.
  • Drink Ribeiro, from the cunca. Not Albariño. Not beer.
  • Ask for pimentón picante if you want the hot paprika rather than the sweet.
  • Eat it with the wooden toothpick provided, not a fork.
  • Arrive 13:00–14:00 for the freshest cook. That's also peak queue — worth it.

Melide's pulperías appear as cards on Day 3, where they belong in the walk.

The table at the end: Santiago

Six days of pilgrim menus earn you one serious meal, and Santiago can deliver at any price point you like — from a €2 caña with a free tapa attached to a Michelin-starred Galician-Japanese tasting menu. This is also the part of the country where a drink comes with food, which surprises Spaniards from elsewhere and makes it the cheapest good dinner in the city. The tapas streets are Rúa do Franco and Rúa da Raíña, and the local warning is worth repeating: if a menu looks overpriced or has photographs of uninspiring food on it, you're in the wrong place.

Buy the whole tart, not a slice. Bakeries all over the old town sell them boxed for travel, and the nuns of the 9th-century Convento de San Paio de Antealtares, immediately east of the Cathedral, make and sell almond cakes of their own.

A local's free-tapas crawl, in order, if you'd rather graze than sit: Orella (pork ears — orella is Galician for ear) → Cabalo Branco (lentils) → Gato Negro (pementos de Padrón) → Xuntanza → A Taberna do Bispo. Reckon €2–3 a round, tapa included.

Where to sleep at the finish

The last two nights

Santiago is small enough that almost every bed is within striking distance of the Cathedral, so the real question isn't which hotel — it's which side of the walk you want to still be on. Three areas cover the decision, and for a two-night stay there's an argument for splitting them: the old town for the arrival, the newer grid for the recovery.

Booking lead time in October

October is the tail of peak Camino season. The Parador and NH Collection want 4–8 weeks; the Hospedería San Martín Pinario books out faster than its website suggests and is more reliably reached by email (info@sanmartinpinario.eu). The Last Stamp is more flexible but still worth a week or two. And note the Parador's famous cut-price pilgrim rate is a low-season thing — November to March. It does not exist in October.

Also worth knowing: laundry. You will arrive with six days of walking clothes. Lavanderías in and around the old town do same-day service for roughly €8–12 — and Pilgrim House on Rúa Nova is a daytime pilgrim centre (no beds) where you can sit, decompress and talk to people who have just done exactly what you did.

If you have another day — or three

The Camino doesn't have to end at the cathedral

For centuries, pilgrims who reached Santiago kept going — another 90 km west to the cape the Romans called finis terrae, the end of the earth, where the sun died into the Sea of Darkness each evening and Celtic tribes had worshipped at the Ara Solis long before Rome arrived. It is the only Camino route that begins at the Cathedral instead of ending there, and that reframing is the whole appeal.

The one add-on worth rearranging flights for

Fisterra & Muxía · the Costa da Morte

Bus 2–3 hrs · €7–11 · or guided day trip €30–55 · 1–3 days

The Coast of Death earns its name — it has wrecked ships for centuries, which is why the 1853 lighthouse at Cabo Fisterra, 138 m above the Atlantic and the westernmost in Europe, throws a beam 65 km out to sea. The 0.0 km marker stands at the head of the road down to it, along with a bronze pilgrim's boot on the rocks and scorched patches where walkers have burned a piece of gear to mark the end. The sunset is the event; every first-hand account insists on it.

But the better of the two villages is arguably Muxía, quieter and stranger. Its Santuario da Virxe da Barca is a Baroque chapel jammed onto bare rock at the sea's edge, waves breaking metres from the walls. The legend says St James, despairing, saw the Virgin arrive in a boat made of stone, and the boulders scattered in front of the chapel are its wreckage: the Pedra de Abalar is the hull, balanced so finely you can rock it; the kidney-shaped Pedra dos Cadrís is crawled beneath for relief from back and kidney complaints. The Christian story is a late overlay on a site that was sacred to the Celts. Beside it stands A Ferida — "the wound" — a split granite megalith honouring the volunteers who cleaned this coast after the Prestige tanker broke up offshore in 2002.

Pick your version by how many days you have

One day — take the guided tour. €30–55 for 9–10 hours, and the only realistic way to see both villages plus the coastal stops without a car. A typical run: Ponte Maceira → Muros → the Hórreo de Carnota (the longest granary in Spain, nearly 35 m, built 1760–83) → the Ézaro waterfall → Cabo Fisterra → lunch in Fisterra town → Muxía → home. What you trade away is time; you will not linger anywhere.

Two days — the best walking day on the coast. Bus to Fisterra, sleep there for the sunset, then walk the 28 km coastal link to Muxía the next day and bus back. Every source that has walked both agrees on the direction: Fisterra first, so Muxía — quieter, stranger, better — is the ending. Split it at Lires (13.5 km + 15 km) to make it easy. Carry food: there is nothing at all on the Lires→Muxía leg.

Three days — arrive on foot. Bus out to Cee (the coast's only real bus terminal) and walk the last 16 km in via Corcubión's harbour and the long sweep of Langosteira beach, with the first sight of Fisterra across the bay from the Mirador de Moína. Then Fisterra→Lires→Muxía on foot. You earn both capes under your own power, which is the point.

You cannot walk the whole Camino Finisterre in three days — it's 89.6 km to Fisterra, 4–5 stages at a humane pace.

Two October traps

1. The sunset moves. Sunset at the cape slides from 20:18 on 1 October to 18:30 on 31 October, and the clocks go back on the 25th. The lighthouse is 3.2 km from Fisterra village on an unlit road — late in the month you will be walking back in full darkness. Bring the headtorch you've been carrying all week.

2. Buses thin out. Off-season frequency drops sharply and there are far fewer Muxía services than Fisterra ones — the Muxía stop is a roadside sign, not a station. Fares and times conflict wildly between sources (€7–15, 1h45–3h). Verify at monbus.es before committing, and check 12 October separately — it's a national holiday and rural services drop to a Sunday timetable.

Small rituals here

Neither village issues a Compostela, but each has its own certificate — the Fisterrana and the Muxiana — and this route needs only one stamp a day, not two. Collect at the tourist offices; if they're shut (likely in October, certain on the 12th), try the municipal albergue.

And eat percebes, prised off the rocks by percebeiros working one of the most dangerous jobs in Spanish fishing — up to €200 a kilo, and the most Costa da Morte thing on any menu.

Two more things worth an hour each, if you're there. In Fisterra, the daily lonxa fish auction by the port runs weekdays from about 16:30 — trays of the day's catch bid over by local buyers, and the least touristic thing you can do on this coast. In Muxía, the secadoiro de congros: wooden racks for air-drying conger eel, in use since the Middle Ages. Only two working racks survive in all of Spain and both are here. It takes five minutes to look at and tells you more about the village than the sanctuary does.

The two capes

The walking, and one detour inland

Beyond these, if you have a car: Monte Pindo (A Moa, 627 m — the granite massif you've been staring at from the cape, called the Mount Olympus of Spain, a real scramble), the 7 km crescent of Praia de Carnota with its 34.76 m 18th-century hórreo, and Cabo Touriñán, which is quietly the actual westernmost point of Iberia and draws nobody at sunset.

Lower effort, still excellent

Three day trips by train and bus

All from Santiago's Intermodal Station, a 20-minute walk south of the Cathedral.

  1. Ourense — hot springs for wrecked legs

    The obvious post-Camino move: free thermal pools along the Miño, fed by springs that have been running since Roman times, plus the As Burgas springs in the city centre and a cathedral with its own polychrome Pórtico do Paraíso — Ourense's answer to Santiago's. Tapas culture where every drink comes with a free one.

    High-speed train ~40 min · €10–19 · full day · take the train, the bus is 2 hrs

  2. Pontevedra & Combarro — the underrated one

    A car-free old town of granite arcades and tapas plazas, on the Camino Portugués — the shells are set into the paving. Seek out the Igrexa da Virxe Peregrina, built on the floor plan of a scallop shell: a nice architectural coda to a pilgrimage. Then a 20-minute bus to Combarro, where stone crosses and waterfront hórreos line the shore.

    Train ~45 min · €6–8 · or Monbus ~1 hr €5.80 · full day for both

  3. A Coruña — the easiest escape

    Forty minutes and €8 gets you Galicia's second city and the Torre de Hércules, the oldest surviving Roman lighthouse still in operation, UNESCO-listed and dating to the late 1st century. Add a nearly 13 km seafront promenade, an old-town tapas scene and the fresh seafood of a working port. On a day with rain forecast, a city is a lower-risk bet than a coastline.

    Train ~40 min · €8 · or bus ~1 hr €7 · full or half day

Also within reach and worth knowing about: Cambados on the Ría de Arousa, home of Albariño, with the Pazo de Fefiñáns and nearly 50 wineries — October is just post-harvest, so confirm visits ahead (Monbus, ~1.5 hrs, €5.25). And Padrón, where the Apostle's body is said to have come ashore, and where the peppers come from.
