The
Yellowhead

Sea-Tac at 8 PM to a full-hookup site under the Perseids at Whistlers Campground — routed so nothing worth seeing is more than half an hour's walk from the parking brake.

You will pull out of the Sea-Tac rental lot in the dark, and for the first two hours the trip will feel like a mistake — six lanes of I-5, the smell of a brand-new holding tank, somebody already asking how much longer. Then Sunday happens. The Fraser Valley flattens out into hop fields and berry rows, the Coquihalla climbs 1,244 m through granite, and by mid-afternoon you are in a hot, sagebrush, ponderosa-pine desert where the rivers run the colour of weak tea and the hills are eroded into hoodoos. Monday you drive up a dead-end road into a volcanic park with almost nobody in it and stand two minutes from your parked RV while 141 metres of the Murtle River falls into a canyon. Tuesday the mountains stop pretending: the highest peak in the Canadian Rockies rises 3 km straight out of the Fraser's headwaters, right beside the road. Wednesday you cross Yellowhead Pass — the gentlest crossing of the Rockies there is — and roll into Whistlers Campground with the burnt, silvering forest of 2024 standing all around you and the Perseids about to start.

The whole route follows one thread: the Yellowhead, named for Pierre Bostonais — Tête Jaune, the fair-haired Métis-Iroquois trapper who guided fur brigades through these passes in the 1820s. Highway 5 carries his name north from Kamloops; Highway 16 carries it east over the divide. Every sign you follow for four days has his head on it.

The shape of the drive

This route was chosen over the more famous Banff–Icefields Parkway approach for one reason: it front-loads the driving and then lets up. Sunday is the long day — get it done and every day after is a half-day with time to stop. Distances and times below are planning estimates; add a third again for photo stops, wildlife jams and roadworks.

Your daughters' terms have been taken as a hard design constraint, not a preference. Anything that crosses into the red is either flagged with an escape hatch (a turn-back point that keeps it legal) or left out entirely.

What the road does to the thermometer

You climb from Puget Sound damp into interior desert heat and out again into alpine cool, all in five days. The Fraser Canyon and the Thompson Valley are the hot part — that is where 50-amp power for the air conditioning stops being a luxury. By Mount Robson you want a fleece at breakfast.

Four things to sort before you leave home

1 · Get the rental company's cross-border permission in writing. This is the single most important call. Policies are not uniform: Cruise America requires a motorhome rented in the USA to be returned to the USA (fine for a round trip, fatal for a one-way drop in Canada), while peer-to-peer platforms such as RVezy and Outdoorsy permit US↔Canada crossings under their standard insurance but ask you to notify the host in advance. Ask for four things on paper: permission to enter Canada, confirmation the insurance extends into Canada, any restrictions on routes, and the documents they want in the vehicle. A verbal yes at the pickup counter is not something a border officer can verify.

2 · Passports for all four of you. A birth certificate is not enough at a land border for this itinerary, minors included. Both parents are travelling, so no notarised consent letter is needed — that requirement applies when a child crosses without a parent.

3 · Campground reservations, now, in this order. Whistlers Campground on Aug 12 is the one that cannot fail; Parks Canada's 2026 reservation service opened on Jan 27, 2026 and August is its busiest month (reservation.pc.gc.ca, or 1-877-737-3783). BC Parks shortened its 2026 frontcountry window from four months to three, releasing at 7 AM Pacific — which means Mount Robson's Aug 11 window opened on May 11 and Wells Gray's Aug 10 window opened on May 10. Both have almost certainly gone. The plan below therefore routes you to private RV parks that book directly and hold later availability, with the provincial campgrounds listed as the upgrade if a cancellation appears.

4 · Book the Maligne Lake cruise if you want it. It sells out, by the operator's own admission, nearly every day in summer. It is an add-on rather than core here — but if it appeals, buy the ticket the moment your dates are fixed.

The two live conditions to check the morning you drive

⚠ Fraser Canyon closed · smoke in the interior

As of late July 2026, Highway 1 through the Fraser Canyon is closed for a 45 km stretch from 18 km north of Yale to Jackass Mountain Summit, because of the Brunswick Complex wildfire (Brunswick Creek plus Ainslie Creek, 20,000+ hectares, evacuation orders near Boston Bar). There is no reopening estimate. This does not break the route — the Coquihalla was always the faster way north — but it means the Coquihalla is carrying everyone, with single-lane sections between the Great Bear Snowshed and the summit and again near Larson Hill. It also means Hell's Gate and Yale are probably off the table (see the add-ons).

Second: Kamloops hit an Air Quality Health Index of 10+ ("very high risk") twice in the month before your trip, and open burning and campfires are prohibited across most of southern BC. Smoke swings from moderate to hazardous inside 24–48 hours, so check it daily rather than planning around an average.

  • DriveBC and isthefrasercanyonopen.ca — road status, the morning of Aug 9
  • BC air quality warnings and FireSmoke.ca — smoke forecast
  • Alberta wildfire status and Jasper fire updates — for the last two days
  • 511 Alberta — Yellowhead Pass and Jasper roads

If smoke is thick, the trip does not stop — it just shifts. Close-range subjects hold up in haze where distant ridgelines dissolve, and this route is almost entirely close-range: canyons, waterfalls, rivers, a wildlife park, a boat.

The headline · night of Wed 12 → Thu 13 August

The Perseids peak on your arrival night — under a new moon

This is not a near-miss. The Perseid meteor shower reaches peak activity overnight on Aug 12–13, 2026, and new moon falls exactly on Aug 12 — so there is no moonlight to wash the sky out, all week. You will be inside the world's second-largest Dark Sky Preserve, 11,000 km² of it, having just parked at Whistlers Campground.

At a genuinely dark site the rate runs to roughly 50–75 meteors an hour. The catch is latitude: at 52.9°N in mid-August, true astronomical darkness is short — it starts around midnight and ends about 3:48 AM. That is your window, and it is a school-holiday-only kind of ask of two teenagers, which is exactly why they will do it.

How to actually do it after a driving day. Set up at the campsite rather than driving anywhere — you have arrived, the sky above Whistlers is dark, and the burnt forest around the campground means fewer trees in the way than there used to be. Lie back on the picnic table, face away from any RV light, give your eyes twenty minutes to adjust, and look roughly northeast. If you want the postcard, Pyramid Lake's footbridge is ten minutes' drive and is the local astrophotographers' pick, with the bridge as a foreground line. Two caveats: smoke ruins astronomy outright — dense haze turns the sky into brown mush, so keep Aug 13 as the backup night; and Jasper is bear country at 1 AM, so stay at your site or in a lit day-use area.

Also on this week's calendar

Free entry to every Canadian national park. The Canada Strong Pass runs Jun 19 – Sep 7, 2026: free Parks Canada admission and 25% off camping and overnight stays. Your whole trip sits inside it. Practically, that means no Discovery Pass purchase at the Jasper gate and a discounted Whistlers site — confirm the discount is applied when the reservation is processed. The flip side is crowds: a national promotion in the busiest month means fuller lots than a normal August, so the "before 10 AM or after 4 PM" rule matters more than usual.

A partial solar eclipse, Wed 12 August. The great total eclipse of Aug 12, 2026 tracks across Iceland and Spain — but a partial phase is reported visible from Valemount, which is roughly where you will be that morning. It is a daytime event, not a golden-hour one, and worth ten minutes of curiosity rather than a schedule change. Verify the local timing and magnitude before you count on it, and never look without proper eclipse glasses — sunglasses do not work.

Salmon, two weeks early. Chinook returning up the Fraser peak at Rearguard Falls and Swift Creek in late August into early September. On Aug 11–12 you are at the leading edge — expect early arrivals rather than the full spectacle, and treat any leaping fish as a bonus. Wells Gray's Bailey's Chute is the same story: rapids guaranteed, salmon not.

⚠ The 10 PM problem

Washington State Parks publish a flat rule — campers may enter parks until 10 PM — with no published after-hours procedure at Larrabee, Birch Bay or Bay View. A reservation confirms your site, not your entry. An 11 PM arrival is outside the stated window, and peak-August Saturday sites at all three were almost certainly gone within minutes of their nine-month booking window opening. Treat them as a cancellation watch, not a plan.

Feeding four people at 10:45 PM

Bellingham's late-night habit is not drive-thru — it is slice windows and taprooms, and one diner that has been open through most of it. The Horseshoe Cafe has served all-day breakfast on Holly Street since 1958 and runs to 2 AM on Friday and Saturday; it is the single most load-bearing recommendation on this page, so phone it before you commit (sources disagree on whether it still keeps 24-hour nights). Downtown parking is tight for a full-size rig — park a couple of blocks out and walk.

Sunday morning, 7 to 10 AM

You have about three hours before you want to be at the border. Spend one of them on a leg-stretch and two on provisioning — this is the last major US grocery cluster on your route, and selection and prices change meaningfully once you cross. Costco on Arctic Ave doubles as the overnight lot and the shopping stop; Walmart on Meridian and two Fred Meyers have big lots and long hours.

For the walk, Whatcom Falls Park is the efficient choice — paved parking, a waterfall and a 1930s stone bridge inside fifteen minutes, no commitment. If you'd rather have water and a boardwalk, Taylor Dock runs a quarter-mile of elevated trestle over Bellingham Bay into Fairhaven's red-brick blocks, with a Woods Coffee on the water at the Boulevard Park end. And Peace Arch Park straddles the actual boundary line at Blaine: a 67-foot white arch on manicured lawns where you can walk between two countries on foot while the vehicle queue inches along beside you. It needs a $10 Washington Discover Pass to park, and RVs use the highway crossing rather than the park grounds.

Skipped on purpose: Chuckanut Drive (WA-11) and the Samish Outlook are genuinely the best scenery here — Washington's first scenic byway, San Juan Islands views from roadside pullouts — but the road is narrow and winding with limited shoulder, it runs south from Bellingham, and it costs you an hour in the wrong direction on the one day you cannot afford it. La Conner and the Skagit farmland are the same story, 40 minutes the wrong way. Both are a different trip.

The transition · Sunday morning

Crossing the line

Sunday afternoon in August is the single worst window of the week to take a large vehicle north across this border: weekend returnees stack on top of tourist and commercial traffic, and the corridor's busiest crossing has reported waits up to four hours on peak days. Everything about this morning is organised around being through the booth before 9 or 10 AM.

Which crossing, for a rig this size

Take Pacific Highway. It is the corridor's truck crossing — which is exactly the argument for it. Peace Arch officially bars commercial vehicles and routes them here, which tells you what you need to know about the two facilities: Pacific Highway's lanes and inspection bays were built for buses and tractor-trailers, while Peace Arch's historic infrastructure was not. A passenger RV is allowed at either, but only one of them gives you room to manoeuvre, and Pacific Highway also sheds some of Peace Arch's chronic congestion. It is one mile east, off I-5 Exit 275.

Do not route through Lynden–Aldergrove. It is closed — a full reconstruction of the Kenneth G. Ward port, with Lynden traffic diverted to Sumas and the replacement station reportedly pushed to at least 2029. Live border data showed it "temporarily closed" in both directions in the week before your trip. Take it off the map entirely.

No official height or length limit is published for either Blaine crossing — these are at-grade highway crossings, not tunnels, so the constraint is lane width, not clearance. If your rental is over about 40 ft, call the CBSA regional office rather than guessing.

Skip the NEXUS lane. Every occupant needs a card for the vehicle to use it; one member without NEXUS puts all four of you in the general lanes anyway.

What not to have in the RV

Four things get travellers turned back or worse on this specific border:

  • Cannabis, in any form. Legal in Washington, legal in Canada, and still a serious criminal offence to carry across without a Health Canada permit. That includes CBD products, edibles and vape cartridges. This is the rule people get wrong precisely because it is legal on both sides.
  • Firewood. Prohibited without a permit — invasive insects. Every campground and gas station near the border sells bundles, and Whistlers includes it in the fee.
  • Farm-direct or unlabelled meat and produce. Commercially packaged, US-labelled groceries are fine in generous quantities — meat, dairy, retail eggs, honey, baked goods and packaged produce all sit under 20 kg/20 L personal-use limits. It is the unlabelled Skagit farm-stand haul that gets confiscated. Eat the berries in the queue.
  • Any undeclared firearm. Declaring is mandatory, and Canada's restricted classifications differ from the US. Non-residents complete RCMP form 5589 in advance but sign it in front of the officer — $25, valid 60 days.

Propane is fine: personal quantities up to 150 kg fall under a dangerous-goods exemption. Turn the tanks and appliances off before you reach the booth. And buy bear spray in Canada — Canada regulates self-defence sprays far more tightly than the US does, and buying it in BC or Alberta sidesteps the ambiguity completely for a trip that ends in grizzly country.

Once you clear the booth, the Fraser Valley does the driving for you: an hour and a half of flat farmland — Abbotsford calls itself the berry capital of Canada — with the Cascades building on both sides until the valley pinches shut at Hope. Then the road stands up. The Coquihalla climbs to a 1,244 m summit through Coast-Cascade granite, and on the far side you drop into something that looks like Nevada: bunchgrass, ponderosa pine, silt bluffs eroded into hoodoos, and by late afternoon the confluence of two rivers at Kamloops in 28-degree heat.

Stop 1 · Hope, and the best 20 minutes on the route

Hope sits where three highways meet, which is exactly why it works as a mid-day break: it is the last real town before the climb. The obvious stop is not obvious at all. In 1911–16 the Kettle Valley Railway blasted four tunnels straight through the granite walls of the Coquihalla Canyon, linked by bridges over a turquoise river ninety metres below. The path through them is wide, flat, packed gravel, with zero elevation gain — which is to say it delivers genuine drama for no physical cost at all, and it is the single best-suited stop on this whole trip to your daughters' terms.

Right in town, Hope has quietly become Canada's chainsaw-carving capital — 80-plus sculptures on permanent public display, including a nearly seven-foot John Rambo marking where First Blood was filmed in 1982. Pick up the free carving map at the visitor centre on Water Avenue (which also has the parking lot that fits a rig), hit ten or fifteen carvings within a few downtown blocks, and go. Add the Fort Hope river viewpoint five minutes away if you want the Fraser and the Cascades in one frame.

Lunch, and the climb

Both of Hope's road-food institutions are diners with lots that fit a double-axle rig — Home Restaurant on Old Hope Princeton Way (meatloaf, schnitzel, perogies, and pie that gets named specifically in reviews, 7 AM–9 PM daily) and Rolly's on the main route through town. Fill the tank here too: Hope has five stations clustered on Old Hope-Princeton Way and 4th, and options thin out badly over the pass.

Driving the Coquihalla in a loaded RV

Long sustained grades in both directions. The real risk on a descent is brake fade rather than anything on the climb — descend in a low gear instead of riding the brakes, and watch the temperature gauges rather than waiting for a warning light on the way up. If temps climb, pull into Britton Creek or the Zopkios brake check, keep the engine running at a slightly raised idle, and let it cool before continuing. Crossing in the morning or early evening rather than mid-afternoon takes the edge off. Expect active 2026 construction and single-lane sections, made worse by all the Fraser Canyon traffic now routed here.

One request from BC Wildfire Service, worth honouring: do not stop at the summit to photograph the Dewdney Creek fire. It is visible from the highway and not threatening it, and crews need the pullouts.

Britton Creek and Zopkios are also just good rest stops — washrooms, granite peaks, an expanded EV fast-charging site as of 2026, and the highest point of the day at 1,244 m. Then Merritt at roughly halfway is your last reliable fuel, food and sani-dump before Kamloops (the Merritt sani-dump is on Voght Street beside Claybanks RV Park, open year-round, though water service closes seasonally).

Stop 2 · Kamloops, Sunday evening

Kamloops is Secwepemc territory at the confluence of the North and South Thompson, and it has been open-range cattle country since the 1800s. It is not a food destination in the way the Okanagan next door is — as one local chef put it, it is "a more working-class community" — and that is exactly what makes it good: the kitchens here buy from ranchers and orchardists down the road rather than importing a scene. Your Sunday evening has three good shapes, and they are compatible.

The default is Riverside Park downtown: a river beach with summer lifeguards, a splash park open 10 AM–9 PM through Labour Day, food trucks, and free live music at the Rotary Bandshell every night at 7 PM through Aug 31. It is the natural "let the teenagers loose and eat something" stop after five hours in a vehicle. Check the air quality index before you commit to an evening outdoors — that is the one thing that cancels it.

The scenic option costs no walking at all. Drive east on East Shuswap Road and the eroded silt bluffs and hoodoos of an ancient glacial lakebed rise beside you; Tourism Kamloops frames this explicitly as a drive-by, asking visitors to enjoy the view from the vehicle out of respect for Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc land. The Kamloops Wine Trail — Monte Creek, Sagewood, Privato — is on the same road, close enough together that one parent can do a twenty-minute tasting while everyone else stays put.

And if Monday morning wants a real anchor, the BC Wildlife Park on Dallas Drive is the one Kamloops attraction that fits the brief precisely: 106 acres, around 200 animals across 65 native BC species — cougars, wolves, bears, and the only Kermode "spirit" bear in human care anywhere — connected by flat boardwalks, plus a miniature train and birds-of-prey shows. It just turned 60. You can do it in under two hours if attention spans fail.

Dinner and Monday breakfast

Kamloops eats early and this is a Sunday — kitchens cluster into a firm 5–9 PM window, and the two best rooms in town (Brownstone in the 1904 bank building, and ATLAS Steak + Fish) are both closed Sunday and Monday. That makes The Noble Pig — open 3 to 11 PM on Sundays, kitchen until 10 — the reliable sit-down pick, and a good one: it builds its burgers and pizzas on Southlands Ranch beef from Knutsford and pepperoni from Gary's European Sausage down the road, and brews its own beer.

For Monday morning, the interesting choice and the crowd-pleaser sit a few blocks apart. Kekuli Café is Secwepemc-founded and Canada's first Indigenous food franchise: bannock served as toast, tacos or dessert, saskatoon-berry smoothies and BBQ sauce, salmon and venison tacos. It closes mid-afternoon and never serves dinner, so breakfast is the only window you get. Hello Toast is the 20-year downtown institution — benedicts, stuffed toast, open 7:30 AM.

One thing you miss: the Kamloops Regional Farmers' Market runs Saturday mornings on St. Paul Street (and a smaller Wednesday market on Victoria) — neither falls on your Sunday–Monday window. Sockeye runs peak August–September, so wild BC salmon on a menu this week is real rather than decorative.

Where to sleep in Kamloops

The honest finding first: no full-hookup RV park inside Kamloops has a pool. All four in-city parks were checked. The swim happens instead at Riverside Park's supervised river beach or a short drive to a real lake — so choose your site on power and river access rather than amenities. In August, with overnight lows of 11–14°C but daytime highs pushing into the low 30s, 50-amp service is what keeps the air conditioning running all night, and that is the deciding spec.

⚠ Phone ahead today

Neither of the two best options is bookable online — both are phone or email only, which matters for a peak-August Sunday. Kamloops Riverview was showing "almost full for the season" on its own site when it was checked: call 1-844-573-3255 immediately. Paul Lake Provincial Park 25 minutes northeast is a genuine lake-swim alternative but has no hookups at all — a dry-camp fallback or a day trip, not a substitute. Silver Sage was ruled out: 30-amp only, no sewer at site.

Useful for the RV: the Kamloops Visitor Centre at 1290 Trans-Canada Hwy W offers free dumping and potable water plus washrooms and wifi, and sits directly on your route into town — the most traveller-recommended service stop of the day.

The feature · why this route exists

Canada's waterfall park,
and nobody is in it

Wells Gray is 541,516 hectares of volcanic wilderness reached by a single dead-end road — the Clearwater Valley Road, which runs 68 km north from town and then you drive back out the same way. It has no gas, no hotels and no cell towers past the gate. It also has no entry fee at all — this is a BC provincial park, not a national one, so there is no pass to buy, no gate, nobody taking your money. And here is the thing that makes it the single best-matched destination on this trip: the three headline waterfalls are 5-, 20- and 5-minute round trips from paved parking lots, and the road is paved all 42 km to the last of them. You can see the greatest hits of a park the size of a small country without the RV ever touching gravel or anyone walking more than a quarter of an hour.

Every source agrees on the crowd situation, which is the other reason it's here: July and August are the park's busiest months and it still "isn't the kind of place that attracts huge crowds" — nothing remotely like Banff or Jasper. Arrive before 11 AM or after 4 PM at the three big falls and you may have a platform to yourselves.

The four stops, in driving order

Measured in kilometres up the Clearwater Valley Road from the Wells Gray Visitor Centre at Highway 5 — which is the wayfinding system the park itself uses, and worth putting on the dashboard since your phone will be dead the moment you turn in.

Two safety notes worth saying out loud to teenagers

BC Parks explicitly flags that there is limited safety fencing at the Helmcken rim and none along parts of the Spahats trail between the platform and the picnic area. These are 75- and 141-metre drops into hard canyons. Also: the waterfall parking lots at Spahats, Dawson and Helmcken are all described as large, which is good news for the rig — but nobody publishes a rig-length figure for the Helmcken side road, so if you're over 30 ft, look before you commit to the turn.

What August does to the water

Spring snowmelt has passed, so Helmcken and Dawson are past their most violent — but the trade is a good one. At lower flow the water reads as defined rather than a solid white wash, which is better for photographs and better for actually seeing the shape of the thing. Dawson's second viewpoint is the rainbow spot: side-on over the lip of the falls, and on a sunny morning the spray catches. Bring repellent — mosquitoes get named repeatedly by first-hand visitors in the forested sections — and know that the old-growth cedar and hemlock around Spahats stays genuinely cool on a hot day.

Water the teenagers will actually get into

The park's own lakes and rivers are glacier-fed and stay cold year-round, and BC Parks is clear that no BC Parks beach has lifeguards. Dutch Lake, right in Clearwater town, is the warm-water answer: a swimming beach ringed by resorts and RV parks, with canoe, kayak and paddleboard rentals and a restaurant on the shore. Several of the lakeside RV parks have full-hookup sites a few steps from the water, which makes an afternoon swim a matter of walking out the door.

Dinner in Clearwater — and it's better than it should be

This corridor is not a dining destination; it is the last services before Mount Robson, and the cooking reflects it — hearty, unpretentious, built around wild game, freshwater fish and a short interior summer. Which is why Hop N Hog is such a surprise: a log-cabin smokehouse doing alder- and birch-smoked 14-to-16-hour brisket it calls competition grade, served family-style on metal trays. It opens at 5 PM seven days a week and runs until it sells out, usually around 9 or 9:30, takes no reservations, and reviews from July 2026 report one-to-two-hour waits at peak dinner hours. Get there at five.

Two structural things about eating along this whole corridor, worth internalising now: dinner service runs early and ends early, and several kitchens sell out rather than close at a fixed time. And the Painted Turtle on Dutch Lake — the lakeside deck option — is closed Monday and Tuesday in season, which is exactly when you're here. Plan a camp dinner as the fallback; along this stretch cooking at the site is normal rather than a failure.

Where to sleep

The choice is inside the park (closer to the falls, no hookups, gravel road) or in Clearwater town (paved, hookups, 5–20 minutes to the gate). For one night with a rented rig and a family that wants a swim, town wins easily — and the BC Parks booking window for Aug 10 opened back on May 10, so the in-park sites are a long shot regardless.

Fuel, water, and going dark

Fill the tank in Clearwater before you turn up the park road. There is no fuel inside, and no cell service past the gate — BC Parks notes that the nearest public telephone is at Helmcken Falls Lodge, which tells you everything. Download offline maps in town. A specific Clearwater dump station could not be confirmed from a reliable source; ask at the Visitor Centre at the roundabout, or at the Canco or Shell in town, on arrival. Note also that campfires are currently prohibited across most of southern BC — do not build the evening around one.

Beyond km 42 the pavement ends and 26 km of well-graded but dusty gravel continues to Clearwater Lake. Nothing on this itinerary requires it. Bailey's Chute at km 56.8 is the one temptation out there — riverside rapids where chinook attempt the leap from late August — but you are two weeks early for the salmon and it costs you the gravel both ways.

Day 4 · Tuesday 11 August
Valemount & Mount Robson
Hwy 5 North 200 km → Hwy 16 East · ~3 h driving · no cell service in the park
↑

Today the highway follows the North Thompson north through boggy river flats — one of the best places in British Columbia to see moose, especially at dawn — and then, at Tête Jaune Cache, turns east onto Highway 16 and the mountain arrives. Mount Robson is 3,954 m / 12,972 ft, the highest peak in the Canadian Rockies, and it stands more than three vertical kilometres above the Fraser River valley floor. Its south face is visible directly from the highway, no walking involved at all. Whether you actually see it is another matter.

Mount Robson's cloud problem — and the plan for it

First-hand accounts agree that the summit hides in cloud on the majority of visits. One photographer's blog put it bluntly: despite her best efforts, that was all the mountain gave her. So build in patience instead of hope. The mountain can clear inside an hour even when it's socked in at sunrise, and the two windows locals flag as best are early morning before convective cloud builds and the last hour before sunset. The strategy: do the walks anyway — the falls, the river, the forest need no summit — then swing back past the Welcome Centre or a Highway 16 pull-off later in the day and check again. On Aug 11 sunrise is about 5:32 AM and sunset about 8:30 PM, so golden hour runs roughly 6:30–7:30 AM and 7:15–8:30 PM. That's a fifteen-hour day; you have plenty of chances.

The drive up · what to stop for

Roughly two hours on Highway 5 without stops, then another hour on Highway 16 into the corridor — budget a half-day with breaks. The road is fully paved, carries heavy commercial and RV traffic routinely, and has no unusual grades: nothing like yesterday's Coquihalla.

Pyramid Creek Falls drops out of a hanging valley and is visible from the highway itself, no trail needed. Blue River at roughly halfway is your fuel, bathroom and coffee break before services thin further — and if anybody is awake at dawn, the boggy flats along the highway here are the moose ground, best worked by driving slowly with the windows down and waiting quietly ten or fifteen minutes at a promising pull-off. In Blue River itself, Grizzly Food Shack is the only well-corroborated option and is genuinely good; after 8 PM it is essentially the only thing open in town.

Valemount is the real stop: your last full-service fuel, propane and grocery before the corridor's thin services and, effectively, before Jasper. The IGA on 5th Avenue is the main store. The Visitor Centre at 785-A Cranberry Lake Road opens 8:30 AM–4:30 PM in season and is the place to ask what the salmon and the wildlife are actually doing today.

Zero-effort viewpoints

Two stops that cost you a parking brake and nothing else. The Mount Terry Fox pull-off sits 7 km west of the park's west gate — a Ministry of Transportation viewpoint, not part of the adjacent provincial park, giving a clean look back at Robson and named for Terry Fox's run. It doubles as a way to re-check the summit's cloud status while you're driving anyway. Cranberry Marsh (also called the Starratt Wildlife Sanctuary) is a boardwalk into reeds with Canoe Mountain behind it, accessed beside the Best Western lot 2 km south of the Valemount visitor centre — the one composition on this leg that isn't a mountain.

Salmon, at the very end of the world's longest climb

Rearguard Falls is the furthest point up the 1,300 km Fraser that chinook can reach. Everything below is a salmon highway; this is the wall. A 400 m boardwalk drops from a signed Highway 16 pull-off to viewing platforms — the sign says about a thirty-minute return walk, and it is right — and in August you may watch fish throwing themselves at it. The water turns a vivid green-blue when the sun comes out. The same run can be watched from the bank at Swift Creek in George Hicks Regional Park in Valemount, where Tourism Valemount runs a dedicated viewing programme. Peak is late August into early September, so you are early — treat any fish as a bonus, and note that early-morning and evening are the viewing hours.

Dinner in Valemount

Better than a town of this size has any right to be. Caribou Grill is the proper sit-down: prime rib and wild Arctic char in tarragon cream in a log building, with house cheese bread arriving at every table, dinner only from about 4:30, and up to an hour's wait in summer. Glacier Fire runs an actual wood-fired Neapolitan oven in the Robson Valley — opens 4 PM, expect forty minutes at peak. The Valemount Swiss Bakery is the one to plan around rather than stumble into: real sourdough from a traditional starter and butter pastries, a legacy of the valley's Swiss and German settlers, cash only, famous for selling out, and reportedly open Friday to Monday only — so Tuesday is a phone call, not an assumption. Do not plan a sit-down dinner at Tête Jaune Cache: sources conflict on whether its café is operating at all, and Valemount is twenty minutes away with four confirmed options.

Where to sleep

Three clusters, in descending order of romance and ascending order of certainty. Inside the park gets you a mountain from the campsite and a genuinely unplugged night — the Welcome Centre is the only place in Mount Robson Provincial Park with any cell signal at all. Just outside the gate at Robson Shadows gets you the same view, bookable directly. Valemount, 20–30 minutes southwest, gets you a serviced pull-through and no risk.

⚠ Assume the park campgrounds are gone

BC Parks' three-month window for Aug 11 opened on May 11 at 7 AM Pacific, and peak-August Mount Robson dates typically go within minutes. Two realistic paths remain: call 1-800-689-9025 or watch camping.bcparks.ca for cancellations (which do release, especially inside the seven-day window when partial refunds kick in), or take a private site. Robson Meadows reportedly holds around 20 first-come sites — arriving before early afternoon is essential and still not a guarantee.

Two more things. No campground in the park has full hookups — Robson River has 30/50-amp electrical at sites 20–41, Lucerne has water, and everyone uses the dump station on Kinney Road just past the visitor centre. And BC Parks published no official RV length limits for these three, so every figure in circulation is crowdsourced: if your rig is over about 30 ft, call the park operator (Quartz Contracting, 1-250-964-3489) rather than trusting a review. Good news on timing — Aug 11 is a Tuesday and BC Day fell Aug 3, so the three-night statutory-holiday minimum does not apply.

Backups on both sides: Tête Jaune Lodge Campground ten minutes out at the Hwy 5/16 junction (riverside, power and water), Beaverview RV Park near McBride 45 minutes west (unverified — call first), and on the Jasper side Wapiti Campground, which has pull-throughs reportedly to 145 ft. Note that Mount Robson Lodge's cabins carry a three-night minimum; whether that extends to RV sites is unconfirmed, so ask.

Day 5 · Wednesday 12 August · arrival
Yellowhead Pass → Jasper
Hwy 16 East · 85 km · ~1 h · Whistlers Campground by evening
→

One hour and one continental divide. Yellowhead Pass is the lowest crossing of the Canadian Rockies and, by the assessment of people who catalogue mountain roads for a living, "fully paved and maintained to accommodate heavy commercial traffic… gentle slopes and wide curves" — the easiest of the major passes for an RV, nothing like the technical crossings further south. You have the whole day for eighty-five kilometres. Use it.

The morning: Robson, one more time

Give the mountain a second chance at 6:30 AM, then walk something. The Journey Through Time trail is a 1 km self-guided interpretive loop directly behind the Welcome Centre — BC Parks calls it "very popular" — which is a fifteen-minute leg-stretch before you pack up. Overlander Falls is the corridor's easiest real waterfall, a short path from near the Welcome Centre to a wide, low fall on the Fraser through lush forest, and it works in flat light, which makes it the reliable option if the summit is hidden. Then Yellowhead Lake as the mid-morning coffee stop on the way over the pass. Lucerne has a swimming beach on it — BC Parks notes accurately that it stays glacially cold.

Crossing into Alberta and Jasper National Park

Every visitor to Jasper needs a valid park pass regardless of how they arrive — but for these dates it is moot. The Canada Strong Pass gives free Parks Canada admission from June 19 to September 7, 2026, so there is nothing to buy at the gate. Camping is discounted 25% in the same window, not free. If you extend past Sept 7, buy ahead at jasper.travel/parkpass. Note also that the clock changes: Alberta is on Mountain Time, an hour ahead of BC.

What you are driving into: Jasper, two years after the fire

Be ready for this, and tell the girls before you arrive. On 22–24 July 2024 a wildfire burned roughly a third of the structures in the Jasper townsite and swept through the forest around Whistlers and Wapiti campgrounds and the Pyramid Bench. The park is officially in "transitional recovery": almost everything is open, and you will drive and camp among visibly burned, silvering, regenerating forest, with active rebuilding in town. Parks Canada's own current campground photographs show blackened trunks still standing at Whistlers among new grass. It is not a ruined place — it is a place in the middle of something, and that is its own kind of interesting.

Two closures change the plan, and they are the two things older guidebooks and blog posts will tell you to do:

⚠ Closed for the entire 2026 season

Maligne Canyon — the whole trail network, First Bridge to the Fifth Bridge junction, plus the parking lot and picnic area. This was the classic easy short walk in Jasper and it is gone this year. Mount Edith Cavell / Cavell Road — including the Path of the Glacier and Angel Glacier, arguably the park's most photographed view. Also closed: Opal Hills, Wabasso Campground, and Trail 9/9C between Old Fort Point and Valley of the Five Lakes.

Parks Canada's language — it "continues to assess conditions and plan the extensive rehabilitation work required" — carries no reopening date. The loss of trees on those slopes creates real debris-flow and landslide risk. Maligne Lake and the Spirit Island cruise are unaffected and running normally; only the canyon nearer town is shut. Re-check the what's-open map before you go, in case something changes.

The evening you actually have

You arrive with daylight until 9:24 PM, so there is room for one thing before dinner. The two lakes ten minutes north of town are the obvious pick and the least effort: Pyramid Lake with a wooden footbridge out to Pyramid Island, and quieter Patricia Lake a couple of minutes before it, both reflecting Pyramid Mountain. If the girls want a horizon instead, the Jasper SkyTram is 10 minutes from the campground and climbs 973 m in about seven minutes to a boardwalk at 2,263 m — on a clear evening you can see Mount Robson from up there, which is a satisfying way to end a drive that started at it. You do not need the summit trail above the upper station; the boardwalk is the payoff.

RV road restrictions inside the park — check before every turn

Parks Canada publishes hard limits on several Jasper side roads, and mid-August is inside the tightest of them:

  • Lake Annette Road and Lake Edith Drive — no RVs or trailers, max 6 m, seasonally June 15 – September 15. You are squarely inside that window. Park at Whistlers or in town and bike the connector trails or take Jasper Transit.
  • Pyramid Lake Road and Pyramid Beach Road — max 7.5 m. Fine for a mid-size motorhome, tight for a large Class A.
  • Miette Road — max 7.5 m, with a trailer drop-off lot at the start.
  • Sunwapta Falls — "not recommended for large RVs or trailers due to lack of parking."
  • Full-size, bus-scaled lots that are genuinely easy: Athabasca Falls, the Columbia Icefield Centre, Maligne Lake's upper lots.

Several general park roads carry a 6 m default unless posted otherwise. Where the rig doesn't fit, Jasper Transit and the SkyTram shuttle exist precisely for this.

Home: Whistlers Campground

781 sites, three kilometres south of the townsite, and — the reason it is the right choice for a rented motorhome — the only Parks Canada frontcountry campground in Jasper with full-hookup sites, and the only one with no published length restriction. Most of the others (Miette, Snaring, Wilcox, Honeymoon Lake, Kerkeslin, Jonas) cap out around 25–27 ft. Full-service sites carry 15/30/50-amp electrical plus water and sewer.

First-night dinner, and stocking the RV

Jasper is a company town for a wilderness — 5,000 people at the bottom of an 11,000 km² park, fed for a century by a rail line that did not always run on time. That isolation produced a habit of restaurants sharing rations when the freight was late, which hardened into a tradition of community dinners that survives. What is on the plate is Rocky Mountain cooking taken literally: elk, bison, wild boar and Arctic char are what is running through the valley outside the window, not a marketing conceit. The fire took at least a dozen restaurants for good — L&W after five decades, Tekarra, Syrahs, the Patricia Street Deli, Sunhouse — and the survivors cluster along Connaught Drive and Patricia Street, the commercial spine the fire largely spared.

For a Wednesday arrival: Fiddle River is the pick, upstairs on Connaught with mountain views, taking reservations, dinner 5 to 9 or 9:30 — be seated by 8:30 to order comfortably. Order the bison meatballs with fried bannock, the wild game meatloaf or the Alberta elk stroganoff. If it is full, Terra at The Crimson runs to 10:30 PM. Evil Dave's is the teenager play — a Malevolent Meatloaf of bison and wild boar, "cowboy sushi" to share. And Jasper Brewing Co., Canada's first national-park brewery, reopened on 23 May 2026 after nine months dark: the Black & Blue is a blueberry-vanilla ale layered with a stout. Confirm its hours — the schedule is still settling after the reopening.

Groceries: Robinson's IGA on Connaught and Super-A on Patricia are both on the spared strip and were among the first essentials back online after the fire; both historically run to 9 or 10 PM in peak summer. Propane tank exchange is at the Petro-Canada on Connaught. Do not buy firewood — it's in your site fee.

Post-fire hours are still stabilising for some businesses; confirm by phone once you're in town. Alba on Patricia Street was finishing a rebuild with no announced date as of mid-2026 — worth a check in case it opens in time. Coordinates for several downtown restaurants could not be verified to street level, so a few food pins are omitted rather than placed approximately; everything named sits within Jasper's compact six-block core, all within a short walk of each other.

Wildlife, and why mid-August is different

Elk walk through Whistlers Campground and the townsite routinely. Calving season is winding down and the rut starts September 1, which means bull elk are already more on edge than they were in spring — Parks Canada is specific: keep 30 m (three bus lengths) away, never park between a male and females, and keep the girls within arm's reach if elk are near. For bears the distance is 100 m. In mid-to-late August bears move to higher elevations as the berries ripen, so roadside sightings shift. Never feed or approach anything, and photograph from the vehicle.

Optional extensions

If you can steal
another day or two

The itinerary above ends the moment you park at Whistlers, which is what you asked for — but it puts you inside Jasper National Park with an RV and a park pass, and there are two full days sitting right there. Both are out-and-back from the campground, and both are built almost entirely from drive-up viewpoints. A third option only exists if a highway reopens.

Add one day · the best one

South on the Icefields Parkway to the Columbia Icefield

Own vehicle · 104 km / 67 mi each way · 6–7 h round trip, or 9–10 h with the glacier tour · no fuel, no cell service

This is the highest-value day in the region for a family that will not hike, because the road does the work. Almost every named sight between Jasper and the icefield is a paved pullout or a path under fifteen minutes: hanging glaciers, a waterfall that spills straight off a cliff beside the shoulder, and at the end, the most accessible glacier by road anywhere. Fuel the RV completely in Jasper first — Parks Canada is blunt that there are no gas stations between Jasper and Saskatchewan River Crossing, 154 km south, and your turnaround at 104 km sits well inside that gap. Bring water and snacks; the only food is the Sunwapta Falls Resort restaurant and the Icefield Centre.

The one real driving note: sustained 8–11% grades on the ascent and descent of Sunwapta Pass right at the Columbia Icefield. Downshift early. And dress for it — the glacier runs about 15°C colder than the valley, with cold rain and even wet snow possible in midsummer. Every stop involves standing around in that wind.

  1. 1

    Goats and Glaciers Viewpoint · 37 km

    A 50 m paved path to a viewpoint known for mountain goats on the cliffs above the river, with hanging glaciers in the distance. Binoculars help.

    2 min walk · free · roadside pullout
  2. 2

    Athabasca Falls · 30 km

    Only 23 m high and one of the most powerful falls in the mountain parks, because of sheer volume — the whole glacial Athabasca forced through a slot in limestone and quartzite. Paved multi-viewpoint loop with a few stairs, restrooms, bus-sized lot.

    15–20 min loop · free · best wow-for-effort on the road
  3. 3

    Sunwapta Falls · 54 km

    Athabasca Glacier meltwater through a limestone gorge — its name is the Stoney word for "turbulent water." The upper falls and footbridge are about a hundred metres from the car park. Skip the lower-falls extension: 2.8 km return, downhill then back up.

    5 min return to the main view · not recommended for large RVs — small lot
  4. 4

    Tangle Falls · 96–99 km

    Multi-tiered cascade down sculpted limestone, visible from the shoulder — one of the Parkway's most photographed stops and one you need no path for at all. Narrow pullout; in August with tour buses stopping too, be ready for a second pass.

    2–5 min · outhouse on site · 6.5 km short of the Icefield Centre
  5. 5

    Columbia Icefield Discovery Centre · 104 km — turn around here

    The largest icefield in the Canadian Rockies, visible from the highway. The centre itself is free to enter: viewing windows, the Meltdown! glacier gallery, restrooms and a restaurant — glacier views with zero walking. The Ice Explorer + Skywalk combo (no longer sold separately) runs 2.5–3 hours: a shuttle, a 56-passenger ice buggy onto the Athabasca Glacier with 15–25 minutes standing on the ice, then the glass-floored Skywalk cantilevered 280 m above the Sunwapta Valley. Departures every 15–30 minutes, May 1 – Oct 12, and book online — midday August slots go first. There's 20% off after 3:30 PM.

    Free centre · ticketed tour 2.5–3 h · 75 stairs to the centre (alternate path exists)
Do not walk onto the glacier outside a guided tour. Parks Canada is explicit: the ice at the toe is hollow and collapsing, and people have died falling into hidden crevasses. The Toe of the Athabasca Glacier trail (1.8 km return, officially about an hour) is fine as an out-and-back of ten or fifteen minutes for a closer look from solid ground — just don't step on the ice.
Add one day · the famous one

East up the Maligne Valley to Spirit Island

Own vehicle · 48 km each way · allow 90 min each way · 6–8 h with the cruise, 3–4 h without · no fuel, no cell service

The reason most families make this drive is a boat, which is exactly why it suits yours: the single most photographed view in Canada is reached by a seated, covered, 90-minute narrated cruise rather than a trail. And the road there is, by the account of people who drive it constantly, one of the most reliably productive wildlife drives in the park — elk, moose, mountain goats, bighorn sheep, deer, and both black and grizzly bears. "Almost every time we drive it, we have an amazing sighting." For two teenagers who would rather stay in the vehicle, that reframes the whole day as a safari.

The cruise sails 14 km down Maligne Lake — the largest natural lake in the Canadian Rockies — to a viewpoint over Spirit Island, with about fifteen minutes ashore. You do not walk on the island itself; that is prohibited out of respect for its significance to the Stoney Nakoda First Nation. The alternative to the boat is a 28 km return paddle.

  1. 1

    Hanging Valley Viewpoint · 6 km in

    The first pull-off on the road, and easy to miss — watch the odometer. A panorama back across the Athabasca Valley with Pyramid Mountain opposite and, on a clear day, the SkyTram cars visible on Whistlers Mountain.

    5–10 min · pure pull-off, no trail
  2. 2

    Medicine Lake · 25 km — the disappearing lake

    In summer glacier melt floods this basin into a full lake; by autumn the water drains out through sinkholes into a cave system and resurfaces 16 km downstream. Parks Canada calls it one of the largest known sinking rivers in the Western Hemisphere. It is a two-minute "wait, the whole lake vanishes?" story, and a pure drive-up view. Watch the shoreline for bears and the rocks for pikas.

    10–15 min · no walking at all · only 14 parking spaces — expect to wait at midday
  3. 3

    Maligne Lake & the Spirit Island cruise · 48 km, road's end

    Boardwalk and viewing area by the historic Curly Phillips Boathouse, a visitor centre, and three food options — the Lake House Café (hot food 11 AM–4 PM), a Waffle Hut open 8:30 AM–6:30 PM, and the restaurant. Cruises run 9 AM–6 PM daily, June 20 – Sep 6, ticket office from 8:30. Canoe, kayak and pedal-boat rentals if the cruise is sold out.

    Cruise 1.5 h (Premium 2 h, ~$30 more, later and better light) · 297-space lot but large RVs use the upper lots
The one hard failure mode of this day is the ticket. The operator states plainly that the cruise sells out; a Jasper-based writer who drives the road constantly is blunter — "nearly EVERY day in the summer." Buy online the moment the date is fixed, at banffjaspercollection.com or 1-888-900-6272. Then arrive at the lake 60 minutes before departure: seating is first-come even with a ticket, staff will not hold the boat for anyone stuck looking for parking, a missed sailing is not refunded, and re-booking costs $15 a head. Leave Whistlers by 8–8:30 AM. Not permitted on board: strollers, pets, bear spray.

Do not route around Maligne Canyon looking for a partial opening — there isn't one. It is closed for all of 2026, First Bridge to Fifth Bridge, and it used to be this day's easy walk. Plan around Medicine Lake and the lake instead. Cruise pricing isn't published as text on the operator's booking widget; check before you budget.

Only if a highway reopens · check DriveBC on Aug 9

The Fraser Canyon instead of the Coquihalla

Hwy 1 north from Hope · slower and more winding than Hwy 5 · currently CLOSED

If the Brunswick Complex fire is out and Highway 1 has reopened, there is a genuinely better version of Sunday. The Fraser Canyon route north from Hope is more scenic and far more historic than the Coquihalla, and it strings together three stops the fast road doesn't have. Yale Historic Site, 25 minutes north of Hope, is a compact gold-rush museum and heritage village from BC's 1858 rush — one first-hand visitor called it "surprisingly moving" for how quickly you can see it. Then Hell's Gate, where a cable tram drops 153 m / 500 ft into the narrowest, most violent point of the Fraser — 200 million gallons a minute through a gorge as narrow as 35 m — to a suspension bridge over the rapids, gold-rush interpretive displays, and a fudge and ice-cream shop. Forty-five to sixty minutes, essentially no walking. It is the kind of thing high-schoolers remember.

Assume this is off. As of late July 2026 the closure covers exactly the stretch containing Hell's Gate, with active evacuation orders near Boston Bar and no reopening estimate — and Hell's Gate's own operating status should be confirmed even if the road opens. Check DriveBC the morning of Aug 9; if it's still red, take the Coquihalla and lose nothing but these two stops.

Two smaller things in this same category, both requiring a detour in the wrong direction: Bridal Veil Falls off Highway 1 west of Hope (a 15–20 minute walk to a dramatic fall, no boots needed) only fits if you approach Hope via Highway 1 rather than Sumas; and the Hope Slide viewpoint 18 km southeast on Highway 3 — a read-the-sign stop at the site of one of the largest landslides in Canadian history, 47 million cubic metres in January 1965 — is the opposite direction from Jasper entirely. And if a pool is a hard requirement rather than a preference, Sun Peaks Resort 45 minutes from Kamloops is the only nearby RV option that has one; it converts an overnight into a detour.
