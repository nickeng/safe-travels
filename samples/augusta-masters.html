One week a year, a mill town on the Savannah River becomes the center of the golfing world — and the azaleas time it perfectly.

Rounds Practice Mon–Wed · Tournament Thu–Sun Weather 70–75°F day / low 50s night Bloom Azalea peak, early–mid April Badge Lottery only · $125–$160 face

Augusta smells like cut grass and pine sap in the first week of April. On Washington Road the traffic crawls four lanes deep past pawn shops and pancake houses, and then the city simply stops being a city: behind a low wall on the north side, 365 acres of manicured silence. This is the week Augusta rents out its own bedrooms, when a barbershop hangs a hand-lettered sign for parking and a $1.50 pimento cheese sandwich outsells everything in Georgia. Come Sunday evening the crowd drains back onto I-20, the azaleas start dropping, and the town exhales for another year.

You are six friends with four days and a tournament at the center of it. The good news is that Augusta is small — downtown is walkable, the course is fifteen minutes from almost anywhere, and the countryside across the river holds a horse town that most patrons never see. The bad news is that everything about this week — beds, tables, tee times, badges — is decided months before you arrive. This guide is built around that reality: what the tournament actually asks of you, and how to fill the hours it leaves free.

Why April is the whole point

April in Augusta averages 70–75°F by day and drops to the low 50s after dark — mild enough to walk a hilly course for eight hours, cool enough to want a layer for a rooftop at nine. Rain is entirely possible and part of the tournament's lore, so a packable shell earns its space. The real reason the calendar lands here, though, is botanical: azaleas peak in early-to-mid April, exactly when the tournament is played, and they do it citywide — not just behind the gates but along Walton Way and through the historic Summerville streets.

If you have no badges

Plenty of groups come for the week without ever getting inside. The city runs on tournament energy regardless — bars show every shot, restaurants build Masters menus, and the Augusta National Women's Amateur final round plays the course on Saturday April 3, the weekend before, with its own separate (and far less oversubscribed) lottery. Arriving that weekend is the sleeper move.

The reason you're here

Seven days behind
the wall

The first major of the year is played on a former plant nursery, and it shows: every hole carries the name of what grows beside it — White Dogwood, Golden Bell, Azalea. The grounds are startling in person: no litter, no visible advertising, no phones anywhere, and a hush that makes a roar from two holes away sound like weather rolling in. The 122 magnolias lining the entrance drive were planted as seeds by the Berckmans family in the 1850s, when this was the Fruitlands Nursery, and only players, caddies and members ever drive down it.

How the week is shaped

Practice rounds run Monday to Wednesday and tournament rounds Thursday to Sunday, with the field cut after Friday. Which day you hold changes the experience completely — and for a first visit, a practice round is arguably the better ticket. You can walk the entire property at your own pace, the crowds are thinner, and it is the only time cameras are permitted. Tournament days trade that freedom for the thing you came for: leaderboard pressure, and roars.

The badge problem

Tickets are lottery only, at masters.com, and the 2027 application window ran June 1–20, 2026 — it has closed. Winners get one day, up to four tickets. Resale is strictly prohibited: badges bought from third parties can be voided at the gate, and scalping within 2,700 feet of the club is a criminal matter under Georgia law. If nobody in the group drew a badge, plan the week around the city and the Saturday-before amateur final instead.

What to do once you're inside

A handful of rituals are non-negotiable, and most of them cost almost nothing. The concession pricing is deliberate institutional theater — a pimento cheese sandwich has held at $1.50 for decades, and the course is cashless, so bring a card.

The phone rule is absolute

No phones or electronic devices, any round, no exceptions — people are ejected for it every year. Leave them in the car and agree on a meeting point and time before you walk in, because you will separate. The pay phones on the grounds still work; bring a handwritten number if you need one. The one photo you'll get is the free staff-shot portrait at Founders Circle, delivered as a QR download.

Wednesday's camera policy can differ from Monday–Tuesday — confirm at masters.com before you go. Merchandise: the South Gate shop is consistently less crowded, popular items sell out early in the week, and you can ship purchases home from the course.

01 · Tea Olive Section One

The city the
tournament borrowed

 −4

Augusta was a cotton and textile town long before it was a golf town, and the good stuff downtown is all downstream of that: a canal dug in 1845 to power the mills, a brick promenade along the Savannah River, a church so ornate the city couldn't bear to demolish it, and the childhood streets of James Brown. Most of it sits within a few walkable blocks between Broad and Reynolds. This is what fills the hours the tournament leaves you.

Along the river

The Riverwalk is the city's front porch — two levels of brick walkway from 5th to 13th Street, with the upper level feeding into Broad Street and the lower running right at the waterline. The museums cluster along it, the Saturday market sets up beside it, and the pedestrian bridge at 5th Street now carries you across to South Carolina on foot. Give it an easy evening.

Mills, canal and industry

The Augusta Canal is the reason the city exists in its modern form — a National Historic Landmark that brought the Industrial Revolution to the Deep South. The best way to see it is from the water: an open-air replica Petersburg boat, narrated, gliding past Confederate Powder Works ruins and 1880s mills. Tours sell out during tournament week, so reserve.

Azaleas, swamp and soul

Two things surprise first-time visitors: how wild the edges of Augusta get, and how much of American music came from here. Phinizy Swamp is 1,100 acres of bald cypress and Spanish moss inside the city limits, with river otters, herons and — at a respectful distance — alligators. And the Godfather of Soul is everywhere once you look: a bronze statue mid-Broad Street, a mural on the boulevard that carries his name, and a museum room devoted to him.

Timing note

Pendleton King Park, the city's celebrated Azalea Walk, was closed following Hurricane Helene damage — check pendletonkingpark.com before making the trip. If it's shut, drive Walton Way and the Summerville streets instead: the streetside azalea display there is the best in the city and costs nothing.

13 · Azalea Section Two

Where to point
the camera

 −7

The cruel irony of the most photogenic week in golf is that you cannot photograph it — no cameras on tournament days, no phones ever. That makes the practice rounds precious, and it makes the rest of the city worth planning properly. Augusta's best frames are almost all at either end of the day.

Inside the gates — practice rounds only

If you hold a Monday or Tuesday badge, bring a real camera; it is the single best photographic opportunity of the week. Two spots are worth queuing for. Founders Circle, at the base of the clubhouse flagpole where Magnolia Lane ends, is where staff photographers take your group portrait against the yellow-pansy Masters logo garden and hand you a QR card to download it — expect 15–20 minutes mid-morning, longer at weekends. And Amen Corner is the reason for the long lenses: the 12th green over Rae's Creek with Hogan Bridge in frame, and the azaleas behind the 13th at their most saturated in the afternoon, when the light comes from the west. Claim a chair behind the 12th tee early — placing it holds your spot all day.

Practical

Amen Corner is a serious downhill walk from the clubhouse — roughly ten storeys of elevation drop — so go there first while your legs are fresh. The 12th green itself is off-limits to patrons; shoot from the spectator area near the tee and the 11th green.

Outside the gates — golden hour and after dark

These are the frames that don't need a badge. The Washington Road gate and its "Members Only" sign is public sidewalk and a rite of passage; go early, because it's a zoo all week. Everything else rewards either the hour before sunset or full dark.

05 · Magnolia Section Three

The table,
set for six

 −9

Augusta cooks slow: smoked pork, simmered hash, Sunday spreads that run all afternoon. Geographically it sits on a seam — close enough to South Carolina to have absorbed the Midlands tradition of mustard-sauced pork and hash-and-rice, but firmly Georgian in its insistence on Brunswick stew beside the pit, boiled peanuts from a roadside cooler, and cobbler to close. Over the last decade a genuine restaurant scene has grown up along Broad Street and in Surrey Center — oyster bars, wood fire, farm sourcing — in a city long overshadowed by Atlanta and Savannah.

Read this before you book anything

Masters week is the hardest week of the year to eat well in Augusta. The post-round rush hits between 6:30 and 7:00 PM as the crowd pours off Washington Road, waits run long, and locals famously stay home entirely. Reservations at any chef-driven room need weeks or months of notice. Two workarounds actually help: eat early (before 6) or late (after 9), and use lunch for the places that don't take bookings.

The sandwich in the green wrapper

No single food is more tied to a sporting event than pimento cheese is to this one. Cheddar and cream cheese whipped with mayonnaise and diced pimento peppers, spread thick on plain white bread, wrapped in green paper, sold for $1.50 — a price that has barely moved in decades and is kept low on purpose.

Its lineage is genuinely strange. In the 1940s local families made them in home kitchens and trucked them to the course. Later the recipe passed to a South Carolina caterer named Nick Rangos, who guarded it so fiercely that he took it to his grave when his contract ended. The club now makes its own in-house — more heavily spiced, looser with the mayo, say the loyalists — and whether it's as good as the original is Augusta's favorite argument. During tournament week the city joins in: participating restaurants run a Taste of the Tournament pass, each putting their own spin on it, with jalapeño, smoked cheddar, or pork rinds on top.

Where to get it outside the gates

Wife Saver — the original caterer's descendant business — sells tubs of it year-round, no badge required. The Taste of the Tournament participant list for 2027 is published closer to the tournament at visitaugusta.com.

When Augusta eats

The South starts early and this week starts earlier — gates open at 7:30 AM for practice rounds, so the biscuit counters and coffee bars are the first bottleneck of your day. Dinner is the social meal, though older Augustans still use "dinner" for the noon meal and "supper" for the evening one. Late night belongs to Broad Street, where bars run to 2 AM and the tournament crowd and locals finally mix.

Dinner, when you've booked ahead

The rooms worth planning around. Frog Hollow Tavern is the city's gold standard and books out first; Abel Brown earned Augusta its first James Beard nomination and runs a serious raw bar. Both need reservations made well in advance of the week.

Drinks, dessert and the late shift

Post-round decompression, in rough order of how the evening tends to go: a rooftop while the light is still good, a taproom or cocktail bar after dinner, and cake at any hour. Boll Weevil, in a historic cotton warehouse, does twenty-plus desserts a day and sells its cake in half or whole slices — order the Georgia bourbon pecan pie.

Play your
own round

You are in one of the great golf towns in the world during its biggest week — it would be strange not to play. Three courses within about 35 minutes carry real history, and all three are public. Masters-week rates are inflated and tee sheets fill early, so book at the same time you book your beds. Rates below are tournament-week; normal-season prices are a fraction of them.

Bartram Trail Golf Club and Mount Vintage Golf Club are two more strong public options within 45 minutes; their 2027 Masters-week pricing and packages hadn't been published at the time of research.

10 · Camellia Section Five · Day Trip

Aiken, and the
horses at dawn

 −13

Thirty-five minutes west across the Savannah River is the day trip almost no patron takes, and it is the best one. Aiken is a Gilded Age winter colony — Vanderbilts, Whitneys and Hitchcocks arrived by private railcar from the 1880s and brought their racehorses with them — and the horses never left. Thoroughbreds are still cooled down in neighborhood driveways on a Tuesday morning, the roads in the Horse District are left unpaved by civic choice to protect hooves, and there is a street corner where Whiskey Road meets Easy Street. It predates Augusta's tournament by about sixty years.

The morning that makes the drive worth it

Leave Augusta at 6:30 and you'll be trackside by seven, which is when the Aiken Training Track opens and the thoroughbreds come out to work on a one-mile red-clay oval. Spectators are welcome, free, from the Cot Campbell Clockers' Stand; training runs to about eleven. On a foggy April morning — common here — the mist sits on the infield and the whole thing looks like a painting. In the middle of that infield stands a single spreading live oak known as Blue Peter's Tree, planted over the grave of a 1948 champion and reckoned the most photographed tree in town.

From the track it is five minutes to South Boundary Avenue, whose live oaks arch clean over the road for blocks — it made the cover of Southern Living's "South's Best" issue in 2018. Go before the traffic builds. Then breakfast downtown, and the rest of the day is gardens, woods and a walkable main street.

Two more, if the timing lines up

Neither fits neatly into a single morning, but both are worth building a day around if the calendar cooperates.

Where to eat in Aiken

A Gilded Age resort town that never stopped hosting sets a higher table than most towns this size. The cooking is Midlands and Low Country — stone-ground grits, hushpuppies, oysters trucked from the coast — served with what locals call unhurried grace. Nearly everything worth eating opens onto The Alley.

Worth checking before you drive

Aiken's spring equestrian calendar is dense and the 2027 dates weren't published at research time. Aiken Polo Club plays Sunday afternoon matches at Whitney Field through the spring (about $10 general admission), and the Aiken Spring Steeplechase normally falls in late March or early April — possibly the weekend before you arrive. Confirm at aikenpoloclub.com and aikensteeplechase.com.

18 · Holly Section Six

Where six of you
sleep

 −15

Augusta during Masters week is one of the most extreme cases of event-driven lodging inflation in American sport. Budget chains that run $80–$150 the rest of the year list at $400–$800 and up; houses within a mile of the gate can clear $7,000 a night. Across 1,005 listings analyzed for the 2025 tournament, a five-night stay averaged $3,774 — roughly 452% more than the same rooms the week after. For a group, this arithmetic has one clear answer.

Book now, and expect minimums

Within three miles of Augusta National, book 6–12 months out — the genuinely walkable houses are often claimed the moment the previous tournament ends. Aiken is workable at 3–6 months. Most area Airbnb and VRBO hosts impose a 4–7 night minimum for the week, and premium homes near Magnolia Lane are let as full-week contracts. Confirm the minimum stay with the host before you even ask about price.

Rent a house, almost certainly

Six friends in four hotel rooms at surge pricing costs more than a four-bedroom house and gives you less: no kitchen, no shared room to watch the coverage in, no base of operations. Locals list their own homes specifically for this week. The economics scale in your favor as the group grows — a $3,500-a-night house split six ways is about $583 per person per night, against $700 for a single Hampton Inn room on Washington Road.

Four days around the tournament fills up on Augusta and Aiken alone. But two of the great cities of the American South are within driving distance, and if you can add a day at either end — or you drew a badge for only one round — these are worth the mileage. Both are better as an overnight than a round trip.

Add a day, or an overnight

Savannah — squares, moss and the Lowcountry table

Drive 2 hr 15 min · I-20 E to I-16 E · Full day, better as an overnight

Georgia's oldest city is laid out on a 1733 grid of shaded squares — 22 of the original 24 survive — and in April it is at its absolute best: azaleas run to about mid-month, then Chinese fringe trees take over looking "covered in giant cotton balls," jasmine scents the whole historic district, and resurrection fern on the live oaks turns vivid green after any rain. Start at Forsyth Park, whose 1858 cast-iron fountain at the end of an oak-lined path is the city's defining image; go by 8 AM, because this is peak season and the squares fill fast. Walk north through Lafayette, Chippewa and Monterey squares to the twin spires of the Cathedral Basilica of St. John the Baptist, then detour to Jones Street — brick-paved, flag-hung, generally argued to be the prettiest block in America.

Eat seriously here. Savannah is the unbroken heart of Gullah Geechee cooking, and the city's rice-country pantry produces things you won't find inland: Savannah red rice, a direct descendant of West African jollof; she-crab soup; and shrimp and grits made with sweet Atlantic shrimp from the tidal creeks. The Grey — Mashama Bailey's restaurant in a restored Art Deco Greyhound bus depot — is the reservation to chase; Mrs. Wilkes Dining Room is the opposite experience and just as essential, a family-style lunch at shared tables with a queue down the block.

If you overnight, the two half-day trips beyond downtown are Wormsloe Historic Site, where 400 live oaks arch over a mile-and-a-half avenue, and Bonaventure Cemetery, which is at its most photogenic in exactly this azalea window. And note the dates: the Savannah Music Festival runs April 2–17, 2027, sixteen days of jazz, classical, folk and country across downtown venues.

💡 Wormsloe rewards the 9 AM opening — mist in the avenue and no cars in your frame. A trolley system was announced to reduce vehicle traffic on the oak avenue, so confirm personal-car access with the park before building a morning around it.
The longer drive, and worth it in April

Charleston — the peninsula, and the azalea gardens

Drive ~2 hr 30 min · I-20 E to I-26 E · Long day; an overnight is much better

Charleston is the most-praised food city in the South and one of its most photogenic, a peninsula of pastel townhouses, cobbled alleys and antebellum mansions facing the harbor. The walkable core is small: Rainbow Row's thirteen candy-colored Georgian houses on East Bay Street, the mansion promenade of The Battery at the southern tip, the cobblestones of Chalmers Street, and the four-block City Market that has traded since 1804, where Gullah sweetgrass basket weavers still work.

What makes April the right month, though, is twenty minutes up Ashley River Road: Magnolia Plantation & Gardens holds one of the oldest public gardens in America and its azaleas peak from late March into mid-April — the single best-timed stop in this whole guide. Middleton Place, on the same corridor, has America's oldest landscaped gardens.

Book food before you go. FIG and The Ordinary — a raw bar in a former bank hall, famous for its shellfish towers — both release tables 28 days out, meaning early March for an April trip. The no-reservation alternatives are excellent and require queuing: 167 Raw before noon, and Charleston's two great barbecue houses, Lewis Barbecue and Rodney Scott's. April is still in South Carolina oyster season, which closes mid-May.

💡 Watch the closures if you go at the weekend: FIG and Chubby Fish shut Sunday and Monday, and Vern's is closed Tuesday and Wednesday. Magnolias, The Ordinary and High Cotton are the Sunday-safe rooms.
Half a day, and no crowds at all

Clarks Hill Lake — the decompression option

Drive ~45 min · US-221 / GA-47 · Half day

If four days of crowds is three too many, drive forty-five minutes northwest to one of the largest lakes east of the Mississippi. Clarks Hill — Lake Strom Thurmond on the South Carolina side — covers 71,000 acres across the state line, and Mistletoe State Park on the Georgia shore has a sandy beach, fishing piers, pine-forest trails and kayak rentals on site. April water is clear and the summer crowds are still two months away. Georgia state park day use runs about $5 a vehicle.
