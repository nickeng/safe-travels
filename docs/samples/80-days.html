Around the World in Eighty Days

Phileas Fogg's wager, retraced stop for stop — and the honest reckoning of which of his legs you can still travel, and which the twentieth century took away.

On the evening of Wednesday 2 October 1872, in the reading room of a stone palazzo on Pall Mall, a man who had never been seen to hurry folded his newspaper and wagered £20,000 that he could go round the world in eighty days. He left at a quarter to nine that same night with a carpet-bag and twenty thousand pounds in banknotes, and he did not once, in eleven weeks, look at a view he had not paid for in minutes.

Jules Verne did not invent that route. He read it off a timetable. The Valigia delle Indie had been running mail from London to Bombay via Turin and Brindisi since 1871. The Suez Canal had opened in November 1869. The Great Indian Peninsula Railway had closed its last gap across India in 1870, and the American transcontinental line had been spiked together at Promontory Summit in May 1869. For about three years before Verne sat down to write, and for the first time in human history, the world could be circled on scheduled public transport by anyone with the fare. The novel is a love letter to a timetable that had only just come into existence.

Which is what makes retracing it strange and rather moving. Most of Fogg's rails are still there, and some are still the best way to travel — the climb out of the Western Ghats above Kasara, the Fréjus tunnel under the Alps, the Central Pacific ledge above the American River. What has gone is the sea. Every scheduled passenger steamer on his route was withdrawn between 1914 and the 1970s, and the parts of the journey Verne found most romantic are the parts you now fly.

This guide keeps his route, his ports and his calendar. Where a leg survives, take it. Where it doesn't, the guide says so plainly and gives you the nearest honest equivalent — because the gap between 1872 and now is the most interesting thing you'll learn on the way round.

The trick at the end of the book

Fogg travels east and crosses the international date line without noticing, so he gains a full day and arrives believing he is a day late. It is not a novelist's cheat — it is real, and it will happen to you between Yokohama and San Francisco. Book the Pacific crossing knowing you land on the same calendar date you left, and keep the day in hand. Verne's readers checked the arithmetic for months.

The ledger

Eighty days, accounted for

What survives · what is flown · what you can still ride

The novel's own arithmetic is unforgiving and worth keeping: seven days London to Suez, thirteen Suez to Bombay, three across India, thirteen Calcutta to Hong Kong, six to Yokohama, twenty-two across the Pacific, seven across America, nine home. Eighty exactly, with nothing spare.

Modern flying gives back an enormous amount of that time. The honest way to spend the surplus is not to go faster — it is to stop where Fogg couldn't. He passed Benares at speed and never left the carriage in Paris. You can give India a week, sleep on the Ganges, and still make Pall Mall on the 21st.

Packing for four climates

Build the bag in three shells. A tropical shell — two pairs of light cotton trousers, long sleeves for temples and mosques, a compact umbrella for a stray post-monsoon shower in Bombay, and sandals you can slip off a hundred times a day at ghats and shrines. A temperate shell — the London mackintosh, one warm mid-layer, and a jacket for November evenings on Victoria Harbour and the Yokohama waterfront. A winter shell, which you can buy in San Francisco rather than carry from London: a genuinely heavy coat, gloves, hat, and boots with grip for icy platform edges in Utah and Wyoming.

Non-obvious items that earn their weight: a head torch for the Summit Tunnel above Donner Pass; modest dress (covered shoulders and knees, a scarf for women) for Egyptian mosques and Indian temples; a plug adaptor covering UK, EU, India, China, Japan and US; and a paper copy of your onward tickets, because you will cross several borders where a dead phone is a real problem.

Time it right

The eighty days are unusually well-stocked

Verne chose 2 October for plot reasons. It happens to be one of the best departure dates in the year. Sail on it and the calendar hands you the Hindu festival season across India, the Deepavali light-up in Singapore, chrysanthemums and children in kimono in Tokyo, and then — as the route turns for home through an American and British December — six straight weeks of Christmas. One coincidence is remarkable: Fogg lands at Bombay on 20 October, and in 2026 that is Dussehra night.

Days 1–4 · 2–5 October

London and the Channel

Chapters I–VII · The wager, Savile Row, and the boat-train to Dover
Pall MallCharing Cross → Dover → Calais → Paris

Fogg's London is astonishingly small. From his house on the corner of Savile Row and Burlington Gardens to the Reform Club is a fifteen-minute walk; from the club to Charing Cross station is another twelve. Verne built the finale out of that compactness — the whole novel's last five minutes depend on those few hundred yards of St James's. You can walk the entire opening chapter before lunch.

Early October is the best possible time to be doing it. The royal parks turn gold and copper, the summer crowds have gone, and the light comes in low and slanted — 10–16 °C, a real chance of rain, sunset creeping back from 6.40 p.m. to just past six by mid-month. Pack the mackintosh Fogg's valet would have packed.

The one thing you cannot do is go inside the Reform Club. It remains a private members' club and non-members cannot walk in; the façade on Pall Mall is always visible, and the architectural editor of Country Life reckons the interior "almost unchanged since the days of Phileas Fogg." Your realistic routes in are a member's invitation or the London Open House Festival — which in 2026 runs 12–20 September, before you leave. Plan for the exterior and be pleasantly surprised.

The Channel, honestly

Fogg took the boat-train from Charing Cross to Dover and crossed by packet steamer to Calais. Charing Cross still sends trains to Dover Priory in about 1h45. The ferry, however, no longer accepts foot passengers on Dover–Calais — you need a vehicle. So you have two faithful options: hire a car and take the DFDS or P&O sailing across the same 37 kilometres of water Fogg crossed, or take Eurostar from St Pancras and get off at Calais-Fréthun, which keeps the geography and loses the sea. Purists hire the car for one day; everyone else takes the train and spends the saved afternoon on the White Cliffs.

Verne never came here

The club historian Seth Thévoz has shown that Verne almost certainly never set foot in the Reform Club — he worked from published guidebooks and floor plans. It is why his count of nine windows in the dining room is exactly right (it is on the 1840 plans) while his description of the library is decades out of date, and why he turned a loudly political Liberal institution into a den of rich gamblers. Even the 1956 David Niven film cheated: its exterior shots were the United Service Club four doors east, now the Institute of Directors, and also freely viewable from the pavement.

Eat where the club would have sent you

London's chophouse tradition is the food of Fogg's world and two of its greatest survivors are within a few minutes of his route. Order the joint, the game, and the savoury — in October the game season is at its peak, and grouse, partridge and pheasant are on every serious menu in the city.

The geography that makes the novel work also makes the choice of hotel easy: base yourself in the wedge between Charing Cross and St James's Park and you can walk both ends of the story. One property is almost too perfect — the Clermont is the Victorian terminus hotel built above Charing Cross station in 1865 by the very railway company that ran Fogg's boat-train.

Days 4–8 · 5–9 October

Turin, and the road that runs out

Chapter VII · Paris, Mont Cenis, and the P&O berth at Brindisi
Paris Gare de LyonTurin → Bologna → Bari → Brindisi

This is the leg Verne dismisses in a sentence and it is the one that has changed least. Fogg's express ran from Paris to Turin through the Fréjus tunnel — opened 17 September 1871, fourteen months before the novel was published, the first great Alpine rail bore and the wonder of its age. You still go through it. Sitting in a carriage as the line climbs past Bardonecchia and plunges under the mountain wall is the most tangible Fogg moment in Europe.

In 1872 Paris to Brindisi took 35–40 hours. Today Turin to Brindisi is 9–11 hours on a Frecciarossa and a regional connection, so the leg has room in it — enough for two nights in Turin, which is a city that punishes hurry. Early October gives it 14–22 °C, soft autumn light, morning fog off the Po, and the vendemmia filling the Piedmont hills. Brindisi, six hundred kilometres south, is still reliably 20–26 °C with the Adriatic swimmable and the summer crowds gone.

Why Brindisi matters

From 1871 to 1914 Brindisi was the terminus of the Valigia delle Indie. P&O steamers left this harbour every Sunday at two o'clock for Port Said, Aden and Bombay. That is the service Fogg boards as the Mongolia, and it is why Verne's route goes the long way down Italy instead of sailing from Marseille. Walk from Brindisi Centrale down Corso Garibaldi to the water — that straight road was cut in the 1860s specifically to carry mail passengers from the train to the ship — climb the Scalinata Virgiliana, and stand beside the surviving Roman column that marks the end of the Appian Way. Rome's road ends there; the sea begins. It is the emotional hinge of the whole European leg.

And it is where the route breaks for the first time. There is no passenger sailing from Brindisi to Egypt. The ferries still leaving that harbour go to Greece and Albania. See the feature below for what to do about it.

What to do about each gap

Brindisi → Egypt. Fly, but keep the departure ritual: spend the last evening on the Lungomare, walk up to the Roman column at dusk, and leave in the morning. The alternative that preserves the sea — Brindisi to Greece by ferry, then onward — costs three days and lands you nowhere near the Canal.

The Canal itself is the exception. You can still transit the Suez Canal by ship, and you absolutely should. It takes 12–16 hours southbound and is the single most Verne-faithful passage left on the route — see the Canal leg below.

Suez → Bombay, Calcutta → Hong Kong, Yokohama → San Francisco. Flown. There is no romantic workaround; container-ship berths are not a bookable tourist product and the schedules do not fit eighty days. Accept it, and spend the recovered fortnight ashore in India instead — which is where the novel's best chapters are anyway.

New York → the British Isles. This is the one gap worth real effort. Cunard's Queen Mary 2 still makes the North Atlantic crossing from Brooklyn to Southampton — seven nights, the last scheduled ocean liner service on earth, and the closest thing in existence to what Fogg chartered. December sailings are sparse and sell out. Check this before you book anything else; if it fails, fly to Cork and pick the novel up at Cobh.

The consolation

Seven of twelve legs still run on rails Fogg's train ran on, and three of them — the Fréjus tunnel, the Thal Ghat incline above Kasara, and the Central Pacific ledge at Cape Horn above the American River — are engineering that was brand new when Verne wrote about it and is still in daily use. No other literary journey on earth has survived this well.

Days 9–15 · 10–16 October

The Canal that made the wager possible

Chapters VI–IX · Suez, the consulate, and Detective Fix on the quay
Port SaidIsmailia → Suez · detour to Cairo

Nothing in the novel is more load-bearing than this ditch. The Suez Canal opened in November 1869; without it, circumnavigation in eighty days was arithmetically impossible, and Verne's whole premise collapses. Fogg's Mongolia reaches Suez on 9 October — day seven — and Fix is waiting on the quay. Fogg barely leaves the ship. Passepartout goes ashore to find the English consulate and have the passport stamped, and that is the entire Egyptian episode.

October is Egypt's sweet spot: the summer furnace has broken, Cairo runs 28–32 °C, the Delta and Canal Zone sit under clear powder-blue skies, and the heavy November–January tourist crush has not started. Ramadan is not a factor.

Transiting the Canal

The 163 km waterway is, as Rough Guides cheerfully admits, "a pretty dull waterway relieved only by the interesting cities of Port Said and Ismailiya" — and that plainness is exactly the point. The spectacle is the ships: bulk carriers, LNG tankers and container stacks gliding past at walking pace above a flat desert horizon, their superstructures looming over the banks. Convoys pass in the Bitter Lakes bypass. Southbound transit takes 12–16 hours; the Suez Canal Authority publishes convoy slots, but not far in advance, so coordinate with your vessel's operator as the date approaches.

Photography in the Canal Zone

Photographing port installations and military sites is illegal in Egypt and enforced by security officers on the ground — in Suez especially. Ship photography from the waterway itself is generally tolerated; ask your vessel's crew. Land-side photography of canal infrastructure is restricted, which is why the vetted vantage points below matter.

The two towns Verne's readers knew

Port Said went from empty sandbar to a city of ten thousand in a decade, and by the late 1800s more than half its fifteen thousand residents were European. Its signature three- and four-storey wooden verandas exist nowhere else in the world. Kipling called the Canal's entrance one of "three great doors in the world where, if you stand long enough, you shall meet anyone you want." Ismailia, 80 km south, is the Canal Company's own garden city — built from scratch in the 1860s for de Lesseps' European staff, and the south side of the railway line still looks, in Rough Guides' phrase, "almost as they must have done in the 1930s, with bilingual street signs nourishing the illusion that the British Empire has just popped indoors for a quick cocktail."

Suez town itself, Fogg's literal landfall, is bluntly described by the same source as "a neglected and untidy city but a vital transport nexus." Go for the Al Corniche walk and the supertankers, not for the architecture.

The detour Fogg was too obsessed to take

Verne's Fogg does not visit Cairo. Every other Victorian traveller arriving through the Canal did — Thomas Cook ran his first Egyptian tours in 1869, the same year the Canal opened, and by 1872 Nile steamers were a fixture. Cairo is not on Fogg's itinerary; it is the world Fogg was passing through at speed, and two days here is the single best use of the time that flying has given back. Note that the Giza plateau works differently now: the main entrance has moved to a new visitor centre on the Fayoum Highway, private cars and coaches are barred from the plateau, visitors ride included electric shuttles between stops, and tickets are sold online only — the Sphinx Gate has no ticket office.

Eat — koshary, pigeon, and a legacy bar

Egypt's national dish is koshary, and the argument about who makes it best has one obvious opening move. Beyond it: ful medames for breakfast, hamam mahshi as the dish worth ordering once, molokhia, and feteer meshaltet. Egyptians eat late — lunch is often the main meal at 1–4 p.m., dinner from 8 p.m. and well beyond.

Sleep — including the room where the Canal was dreamt up

One of these is not like the others. Maison Maxim de Lesseps in Ismailia is Ferdinand de Lesseps' own villa, built to house him during the Canal's construction, left in a hurry with much of his furniture still in it, and now a 27-room hotel next door to the Suez Canal Museum. Its restoration "sought more to preserve the home than to actually modernise it." The technological achievement that makes Fogg's wager possible was conceived in the rooms you sleep in. Nothing else on the eighty days comes close.

Port Said has no grand hotel of the Clermont's calibre; treat it as a half-day or one-night stop. Suez town has effectively no visitor accommodation worth the name — this guide bases the Canal leg at Port Said and Ismailia instead, which is where the Canal's history actually lives.

Days 16–19 · 17–20 October

Bombay, on the night the demon burns

Chapters X–XI · Apollo Bunder, the pagoda, and the 8 p.m. train east
Apollo BunderChhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus

You arrive on the exact date Verne chose — 20 October — and the city hands you a coincidence no planner could arrange. In 2026, 20 October is Dussehra. After dark, effigies of the demon-king Ravana twenty to thirty metres tall, packed with firecrackers, are detonated on public grounds across Mumbai; the largest is at Shivaji Park in Dadar. Fogg steps off the steamer into a city already in festival, the pagoda episode follows the next morning, and for once the novel and the calendar agree.

October is the best month to be here. The monsoon withdraws mid-month, so by the 20th the city is usually dry, bright and cooling — highs of 31–33 °C, nights of 25–26 °C, humidity dropping fast from the soggy September levels. Evenings on the harbour front are genuinely pleasant. Carry a small umbrella for a stray shower.

Fogg's Bombay is compact and almost entirely walkable: the Gateway, the Fort precinct, Malabar Hill, Colaba and Crawford Market sit in the southern tip of the peninsula. UNESCO lists 92 buildings in the Fort under its Victorian Gothic and Art Deco Ensembles inscription — walking a single kilometre south from the terminus takes you past the High Court, the Rajabai Clock Tower, Elphinstone College, the University, the David Sassoon Library and Asia's oldest stock exchange.

The pagoda, and what actually happened

The novel's plot turns on Passepartout blundering shoeless into a pagoda on Malabar Hill and profaning it — the incident that puts Scotland Yard on Fogg's trail. Verne's pagoda is a composite fiction, but the real sacred hill precinct is the Walkeshwar temple complex and Banganga Tank, a rectangular freshwater stepwell from 1127 ringed on four sides by ghats and small shrines, and one of the most extraordinarily calm pockets in the city. Women bathe, priests perform puja, pigeons wheel, and the skyscrapers of the world's most expensive real estate rise just above the roofline.

There is a second, deeper thread here. Aouda — the young widow Fogg rescues — is identified in Verne's text as a Parsi, a member of the Zoroastrian community that built commercial Bombay and gave it Jamsetji Tata and Dadabhai Naoroji. Non-Parsis cannot enter an active fire temple, so the only legitimate way inside that world is the FD Alpaiwalla Museum on Malabar Hill, reopened in March 2025, which holds a life-sized replica fire temple complete with recorded prayer and the scent of sandalwood.

Days 20–27 · 21–28 October

Across India, on the iron thread

Chapters XI–XIV · The Ghats, the elephant, Allahabad and Benares
Bombay CSMTNashik → Bhusawal → Prayagraj → Varanasi → Howrah

This is the best leg of the novel and the best leg of the trip. Verne imagined it as a single iron thread from Bombay to Calcutta, broken at a forest clearing somewhere between the Vindhyas and the Ganges plain — the gap that forces Fogg to buy an elephant named Kiouni for two thousand pounds and cross to Allahabad through the jungle. The gap was real when Verne wrote; the line closed in 1870 and has run unbroken ever since. No elephants required, and almost the entire alignment Verne was describing is still in daily use.

Fogg crosses in three days. Give it eight. Late October on the Gangetic plain is post-monsoon and superb: the Ghats still lushly green, Nashik and Bhusawal at 28–32 °C, Varanasi around 30 °C and dry. Two festival nights bracket the window — Dussehra on the 20th behind you, and Sharad Purnima, the harvest full moon, on the 25th, which is the night to be on the ghats.

The climb every early traveller remembered

The train out of Bombay hits the Thal Ghat — the steepest mainline incline in India, 9.5 km at 1-in-37, negotiated by curves, tunnels and the Ehegaon viaduct, 219 metres long and 55 metres high. It opened on 1 January 1865, so it is precisely the engineering Verne was writing about, and the ascent from Kasara to Igatpuri takes about twenty-five minutes of genuinely dramatic mountain railway. Book a window seat on a morning express and watch the valley drop away. Stop at Igatpuri for tea and you can watch the banker locomotives shunting.

Where was Kholby?

Verne's "Kholby" — the station where the rails run out — has never been definitively identified by scholars. The best candidates are Khandwa or somewhere in the Vindhya crossing between Itarsi and Jabalpur. It doesn't matter much: what Verne got right is the geography, and the stretch of line through the Vindhya foothills onto the flat alluvial plain, with sugarcane fields and ox-carts at the level crossings and dusk over the Ganges basin, is still exactly as he described it.

Benares, which Verne names

Fogg passes Benares — modern Varanasi — and Verne names it directly. Give it two nights. A wooden rowboat from Assi Ghat to Manikarnika and back covers three kilometres of continuous riverfront at sunrise; the Ganga Aarti at Dashashwamedh Ghat every evening is the one unmissable hour in the city.

One real caveat. In a heavy monsoon year the Ganga can still be running high enough in late October to submerge the lower ghats, restricting boat rides to a short section and shifting the aarti off the waterfront. By the 21st–25th this is usually past, but it is not impossible — check conditions on arrival and keep the itinerary loose.

Days 28–31 · 29 Oct – 1 November

Calcutta, and five hours in a magistrate's court

Chapters XIV–XVI · Howrah, Judge Obadiah, and the Rangoon in mid-harbour
Howrah Stationthe Hooghly quays

Verne is precise about this morning: "Calcutta was reached at seven in the morning, and the packet left for Hong Kong at noon; so that Phileas Fogg had five hours before him." In those five hours Fogg is arrested at the station exit, taken by palki-gharri to a police magistrate, hears Passepartout convicted for the Malabar Hill pagoda, pays £2,000 bail, and walks to the quay. It all happens inside one square mile, and you can retrace the whole sequence in a single morning.

Late October is Kolkata's sweet spot — 25–32 °C by day, 18–22 °C at night, the monsoon withdrawn, the sky reliably blue. You also arrive in the afterglow: Durga Puja ended on the 21st, the pandals are down and the idols immersed, but the fairy-lights are still up, the sweet shops still stacked, and the city is exhaling. In Bengali culture the Bijoya greetings go on for weeks.

The colonial fabric here is more intact than almost anywhere in Asia. BBD Bagh — Dalhousie Square — still rings the Lal Dighi tank with the Writers' Building, the domed General Post Office on the site of old Fort William, the Currency Building, St John's Church with Job Charnock's grave, and the High Court. And the Calcutta High Court is the real-world counterpart of Verne's courtroom: established 1862, the oldest in India, a neo-Gothic pile by Walter Granville modelled on the Cloth Hall at Ypres, still a working court. Bring photo ID to the eastern entrance for a visitor pass, and walk the arched corridors past advocates in white bands and black gowns — a scene that has changed very little since 1872.

Where the Rangoon lay

"Fix saw them leave the carriage and push off in a boat for the steamer… 'The rascal is off, after all!'" The P&O steamers of 1872 berthed along the Strand Road and Prinsep Ghat waterfront, and the Rangoon is moored, Verne says, half a mile off in the harbour. Stand at the white Greek-Gothic Prinsep Ghat pavilion of 1841 at golden hour, hire a rowing boat for half an hour, and you get the on-water view Fogg had. The government's 90-minute Heritage River Cruise from Millennium Park jetty is the fuller version — north past Armenian, Nimtala and Mayer ghats with a live Bengali band on the upper deck, and Howrah Bridge passing close overhead.

Days 32–35 · 31 Oct – 3 November

Singapore, a coaling stop

Chapter XVII · Two hours ashore, in a carriage, among the nutmeg trees
the roadsteadBoat Quay · the Padang · Serangoon Road

The Rangoon "weighed anchor at Singapore the next day at four a.m., to receive coal" — and Fogg and Aouda turn a bunkering stop into a two-hour carriage ride through the island before re-embarking at eleven. Verne's description is unusually lyrical: "a park checkered by pleasant highways and avenues… rows of palms with brilliant foliage, and of clove-trees, whereof the cloves form the heart of a half-open flower. Pepper plants… sago-bushes, large ferns… nutmeg-trees in full foliage filled the air with a penetrating perfume."

Those plantations are entirely gone — the nutmeg and clove groves were wiped out by disease from the 1850s and built over. But the colonial bones are remarkably intact. The Padang where colonial cricket was played, the Singapore River quays where tongkang lighters ferried cargo from ships in the outer roads, Fort Canning Hill with the flagstaff that guided ships in, and the neoclassical cluster between St Andrew's Cathedral and Parliament are all where they were in 1872. Boat Quay handled three-quarters of the port's shipping business in the 1860s, and the shophouse façades survive.

Late October and early November sit in the inter-monsoon: 31–32 °C by day, 24–25 °C at night, and thunderstorms in the afternoon and early evening. The practical rule is simple — outdoor sightseeing before noon, museums over the midday heat, and the evening for Little India.

The best hours are after ten at night

Deepavali falls on 8 November, a week after you leave, but the Serangoon Road light-up runs from about September to mid-November — so your window catches it at full pre-festival peak, with arched light tunnels the length of the precinct and the Campbell Lane bazaar in full swing. Crowds peak 7–9 p.m.; from 10 p.m. the stretch is noticeably quieter and much better for photography.

Days 36–40 · 6–10 November

Hong Kong, where it all goes wrong

Chapters XVII–XXII · The Victoria port, the opium den, and the missed Carnatic
Victoria Harbourthe Praya · Hollywood Road · the Peak

Fogg reaches Hong Kong on 6 November and your itinerary shadows him almost day for day. This is where the plot detonates: Fix lures Passepartout into a quayside smoking-house, the Carnatic sails early without them, and Fogg has to charter the pilot-boat Tankadere to race eight hundred miles to Shanghai through fierce autumn seas.

November is the best month in the city, full stop. Typhoon season is definitively over, humidity has dropped hard from the summer highs, temperatures sit at a very pleasant 20–24 °C, rain is rare, and the sky turns the blue that gives the harbour its sharpest light and the Peak its clearest views. This is the window regulars reserve for. No public holidays fall in it, so museums run normal hours and nothing spikes.

What survives is more than most visitors expect. Passepartout "wandered towards the Victoria port" and found "a confused mass of ships of all nations… men-of-war and trading vessels, Japanese and Chinese junks, sempas, tankas, and flower-boats." Reclamation has buried the original Praya waterfront under the financial district, but the harbour still separates island from mainland, the Star Ferry has crossed it since 1880, the Peak Tram has climbed Garden Road since 1888, and Man Mo Temple on Hollywood Road — built 1847–62 by Chinese merchants to the gods of Literature and War — still smokes with sandalwood and hanging coils of incense.

The opium den, honestly

Verne places Fix's smoking-house "on the quay" near the Victoria port. No building survives and the shoreline is buried under landfill; the actual den district of 19th-century Hong Kong was Wan Chai, the island's oldest working-class quarter, where sailors, soldiers and harbour labourers mixed. The self-guided Wan Chai Heritage Trail through thirteen buildings — the 1847 Hung Shing Temple that once stood right at the water's edge, the 1915 Old Wan Chai Post Office, the blue Lingnan tenement on Stone Nullah Lane — is the closest honest analogue, and about two hours well spent.

The Tankadere cannot be repeated

Chartering a sailing schooner to cross the East China Sea in November is not a bookable product and would not be a sane one. The nearest experiential echo is an evening on a restored junk in the harbour — Aqua Luna's red sails or the pre-war sampan Dukling, both from Central Pier 9, 45 minutes, from about HK$200. Time it to pass through the 8 p.m. Symphony of Lights and you have the harbour from the water, at night, from a wooden boat. It is the right consolation prize.

Days 41–50 · 11–20 November

Shanghai and Yokohama — the treaty ports

Chapters XXI–XXIV · The Huangpu interception, and Passepartout with the circus
Wusongkou · the BundOsanbashi · Kannai · Yamate Bluff

Fogg does not visit Shanghai so much as intercept it. The Tankadere reaches the mouth of the Huangpu just as the Yokohama steamer is putting to sea; Fogg runs a flag at half-mast, the captain diverts, and the party transfers mid-water and sails on for Yokohama, arriving 14 November. It is the novel's most cinematic manoeuvre, and the place it happened is real and visitable: Wusongkou, where the Huangpu meets the Yangtze estuary, is where deep-draught ocean steamers waited while smaller boats ferried passengers up the shallower river to the Bund. The Qing-era cannon battery that guarded that confluence survives inside the Paotaiwan wetland park.

Mid-November is the right time for both cities. Shanghai runs 14–20 °C dropping to 10 °C in the evenings, with a cold front usually rolling through mid-month that accelerates the plane trees into gold — your window sits right at that inflection. Yokohama and Tokyo are at 16–18 °C with cool 9–11 °C evenings, low humidity, predominantly clear skies, and the opening act of kōyō in the Kantō lowlands.

Yokohama is the point, not Tokyo

In 1872 Yokohama was Japan's gateway; Tokyo was the interior capital and largely closed to foreigners. Verne sends Passepartout wandering the foreign settlement looking for work and he ends up with the Batulcar circus. Those streets are not reconstructions — Yamate (called "The Bluff") was the designated residential district for the foreign community from 1859 and still carries a string of preserved Western villas, free to enter, on the hillside above Motomachi. Verne names Benten-dōri directly: the curio street where foreign visitors bought porcelain, silk, ivory and photographs. And the Yokohama Archives of History occupies the former British Consulate General itself, on the square where Perry landed in 1854 to sign the convention that started all of it.

The extraordinary coincidence is the railway. Japan's very first line opened on 14 October 1872 — the same year as the novel — running from Shimbashi in Tokyo to what is now Sakuragichō in Yokohama. A monument marks the spot. Fogg's world and Japanese rail history begin in the same twelve months.

And 15 November is a Sunday, which is the official Shichi-Go-San date — give that day to Meiji Jingū in Tokyo and arrive before ten.

San Francisco, and a political riot

Chapter XXV · The floating quays, Montgomery Street, and the 6 p.m. from Oakland
Pacific Mail wharfOakland → the Pacific Railroad

Verne gives San Francisco one chapter and packs it with detail you can still verify on the ground. Fogg's party lands from the General Grant at the floating wharves near the foot of First Street on 3 December, takes a three-dollar carriage to the International Hotel on Jackson Street, watches the Chinese quarter go by "seemingly imported from the Celestial Empire in a toy-box," gets caught in a brawling rally between the partisans of Camerfield and Mandiboy on Montgomery Street — "which is to San Francisco what Regent Street is to London" — and boards the Central Pacific at Oakland station at six that evening. The whole arc is one long walking day.

Even the technology checks out. Verne describes "movable quays, rising and falling with the tide" — floating docks, which is exactly what you still see at the Ferry Building. And Passepartout falls through "worm-eaten planks," scattering cormorants and pelicans.

Early December is the city's cool damp season: 12–14 °C by day, near 8 °C at night, and a real chance of rain on any given day — December is statistically the wettest month. The compensation is that the fog retreats to the ocean in winter, and the low light on the waterfront is superb. Sunset is around 4.50 p.m.

One closure that matters

Hyde Street Pier at the San Francisco Maritime National Historical Park is closed for major reconstruction with no published completion date, and the historic tall ships — including the 1886 Balclutha — have been moved temporarily to Mare Island in Vallejo. You cannot board them. The 1939 Art Deco Maritime Museum building at Aquatic Park, shaped like an ocean liner, remains open and free, and its trans-Pacific steamship exhibits are the relevant part anyway. Days 56–69 · 4–17 December

The Pacific Railroad — seven chapters, one line

Verne gave the American crossing seven chapters and they are the most propulsive writing in the book: the branch line to Great Salt Lake, a Mormon lecture in a railway carriage, a storm-weakened bridge at Medicine Bow taken at full speed and collapsing behind the train, a Sioux raid at Fort Kearney, and a wind-driven sledge across the frozen Nebraska plains at forty miles an hour to reach Omaha. The line had been completed at Promontory Summit only three years before he wrote it. Most of it still runs, and this is the leg where the whole conceit of the trip pays off.

How to actually do it

The single most Fogg-faithful act available in 2026 is to book a sleeper roomette on Amtrak's California Zephyr from Emeryville to Chicago — about fifty-two hours over the exact transcontinental alignment Verne described, calling at Reno, Salt Lake City and Omaha, with meals in the dining car exactly as his passengers would have expected. Then the Lake Shore Limited, Chicago to New York, roughly twenty hours. Two nights and a day, and you have crossed a continent the way the novel does.

Book three to six months out for December; sleeping-car space is genuinely limited. Coach from about $150, a roomette from $350–600 depending on the window.

The one thing rail cannot give you

Wyoming. The transcontinental passenger line was rerouted south through Denver decades ago and Wyoming has no Amtrak service at all — so Medicine Bow and Cheyenne, the setting of the novel's most famous set piece, cannot be reached by train. If you want the bridge chapter, you hire a car and drive US-30, the old Lincoln Highway, which follows the original 1869 Union Pacific alignment across the high plains: about 55 miles from Laramie to Medicine Bow, through country that has barely changed. Freight trains still run the main line alongside you.

December on the high plains is not a formality

Wyoming in early December is genuinely wintry — sub-zero nights, hard wind, and blizzard conditions that close I-80 and US-30 with very little warning. Verne's bridge-destroying storm is entirely plausible in this season. Check wyoroad.info every morning, carry chains or drive AWD, and build a spare day into the schedule. The Medicine Bow Museum is open Memorial Day to Labor Day only and by appointment in December — telephone 307-379-2383 well ahead. The Virginian Hotel across the street is open year-round and is the more reliable stop.

Eat along the line

The route hands you four genuine American institutions in a row: Utah's oldest bar in a canyon above Ogden, a stockyards steakhouse in South Omaha that has been serving prime rib for a century, an 1898 German saloon in the Chicago Loop with sauerbraten still on the menu, and — a Nebraska claim worth arguing about — the hotel that says it invented the Reuben. Note that Salt Lake City dines early (6–8.30 p.m.) and Utah's liquor rules are unlike anywhere else on the route.

Sleep — in depots and Gilded Age lobbies

This leg has the best-themed hotels on the whole journey. One is a restored Union Pacific depot — the 1909 Salt Lake City station, with the old concourse as its lobby and the bar in the former ticketing hall. One is a 1911 frontier hotel three minutes from the last surviving grand 19th-century depot on the transcontinental line. One occupies the top floors of a 1909 Daniel Burnham building that was the most expensive ever built in Chicago.

Days 70–73 · 11–17 December

New York, and a ship bought outright

Chapters XXXII–XXXIII · Forty-five minutes late for the China
Grand Central Depotthe Battery → Sandy Hook → the Atlantic

The novel's cruellest moment. Fogg reaches New York on 11 December and discovers the Cunard steamer China sailed forty-five minutes earlier. He spends the night at the St. Nicholas Hotel on Broadway, walks alone to the Hudson on the morning of the 12th, scans the vessels at anchor, spots "a trading vessel, with a screw, well-shaped, whose funnel, puffing a cloud of smoke, indicated that she was getting ready for departure," and by nine o'clock has chartered the Henrietta for Bordeaux — then bribes the crew, locks the captain in his cabin, and burns the ship's own decking for steam.

Mid-December is cold and raw — 3–8 °C, wind off the Hudson, sunset around 4.30 p.m. — and the city is at peak holiday magnificence: the Rockefeller tree lit since late November, the Fifth Avenue windows fully dressed, the Saks light show nightly, and free skating at Bryant Park.

Fogg's New York is the southern tip of Manhattan, and it is still the most legible part of the city. Grand Central Terminal stands on the exact 42nd Street site of Grand Central Depot, opened by Cornelius Vanderbilt in 1871 — the station Fogg's transcontinental train would have terminated at. The Battery, where he found the Henrietta, is essentially unchanged in outline, and you can reproduce his exact act: stand at the water's edge on the esplanade and scan the vessels at anchor in the Upper Bay. Around Bowling Green, the old "Steamship Row" where Cunard and White Star sold ocean passages, some of the buildings survive.

The most haunting site on the route

At the foot of the demolished Pier 54, near West 13th Street, a skeletal steel arch still stands over the Hudson with the words "Cunard White Star Line" barely legible in faded paint. This was the home berth of the line for decades — the pier the Lusitania sailed from in 1915 and the pier the Carpathia brought the 713 Titanic survivors to in 1912. The Cunard pier Fogg missed in 1872 was further down the West Side and has no remnant at all; this arch, with nothing behind it, is the best physical trace of the empire of Atlantic steam that his whole journey depended on.

Eat — the chophouses that were already open

New York keeps more of Fogg's own decade on its menus than any other city on the route. Old Homestead opened in 1868 — four years before the novel — and Keens has been serving its mutton chop since 1885, a dish that had already all but vanished from London. The Grand Central Oyster Bar has been under the terminal's Guastavino tiles since 1913, and Katz's since 1888. This city dines late: 7.30–9 p.m. is prime, which matters when you are working to a rail timetable.

Sleep — the Financial District

End the American leg where Fogg ended it. The southern tip of Manhattan is the city's oldest quarter and the one he knew — Wall Street, Bowling Green, the East River counting houses of South Street Seaport — and in December it is calmer than Midtown, an actual working city rather than a shopping crowd. The Battery, where he went looking for a ship, is a five-minute walk from all three of these.

Days 74–80 · 18–21 December

Queenstown, Dublin, Liverpool — and Pall Mall

Chapters XXXIV–XXXVII · The last dash, the arrest, and the day he did not lose
Cobh (Queenstown)Cork → Dublin → Liverpool → London

Everything compresses. Verne puts Fogg ashore at Queenstown after burning the Henrietta's decking for steam, onto an express mail-train to Dublin, across the Irish Sea overnight to Liverpool, under arrest on the dock by Detective Fix, released, and into a chartered special train for London — all inside the final forty-eight hours. Every location is real and most of the infrastructure is still traceable.

Mid-December in Cork Harbour and Dublin runs 6–10 °C with frequent Atlantic showers, occasional bright cold spells, and sunset before 4.15 p.m. Liverpool adds a persistent Mersey wind. The short days work in your favour: by four in the afternoon the harbour towns are already lit for Christmas.

The single best building on this leg

The Cobh Heritage Centre occupies the original Victorian railway station on Deepwater Quay — the very building where emigrants checked in before boarding tenders out to liners anchored in the harbour, and the station from which Fogg's express mail-train departed for Dublin. In 1872 Queenstown was Ireland's main transatlantic mail-packet port, the call every New York steamer made. Standing on that platform, you are exactly where Verne placed his hero. Three million people left Ireland through this door.

Then walk the promenade. Casement Square to Deepwater Quay is five minutes, past the Annie Moore statue — the first emigrant processed at Ellis Island, who left from this spot — with the pastel terraces climbing behind you and the 91-metre spire of St Colman's above. In Fogg's era small tenders rowed passengers out to steamers waiting in the deep channel; the view across Cork Harbour to Spike Island and the open sea is unchanged.

The final rail dash, which still runs

Fogg catches "one of the express mail trains from Queenstown to Dublin." In practice that is Cobh to Cork Kent (25 minutes) and the through service to Dublin Heuston — which opened in 1844 as Kingsbridge — in about 2h30, hourly, from roughly €27. The Great Southern and Western Railway line he used is the line you use. Then Dublin Port to Holyhead by fast ferry (2 hours) or superferry (3h15), and Holyhead to Liverpool Lime Street by rail via Chester in about 2h15.

Liverpool Lime Street, where the novel has Fogg charter his special train, opened in August 1836 and is the oldest still-operating grand terminus mainline station in the world. Trains to London Euston run in about 2h10. Book the 21 December service well ahead — it is one of the busiest travel days of the British year.

Three things to verify before you go

Titanic Experience Cobh closes for "an annual short break over the Christmas Holidays" and the 2026 dates are unpublished — your 18 December arrival may fall on the edge of it; check titanicexperiencecobh.ie. Spike Island normally closes from late autumn through spring and is very likely shut in December — confirm at spikeislandcork.ie. And Dublin Castle's Christmas market is cancelled in 2026, because Ireland holds the EU Presidency and the Castle is unavailable until 2027. The Cobh Heritage Centre has no published Christmas closure and is open daily 09.30–17.30.

The last mile

From Euston to Pall Mall is twenty minutes. Verne's Fogg believes he is a day late, having lost his wager, and sits in his house on Savile Row in the dark — until Passepartout discovers that travelling east they have crossed eighty meridians and gained a full twenty-four hours. It is Saturday, not Sunday. He has eight minutes to reach the Reform Club, and he makes it.

Do the same. Walk from Charing Cross up to Pall Mall on the evening of Monday 21 December 2026, stand in front of No. 104, and note the time. Then go and look at the Norwegian spruce in Trafalgar Square, two minutes north, and have a drink somewhere warm. You will have earned it.

Optional

Six detours Fogg had no time for

Add-ons and day trips · Take one, or two, or none

Flying the lost sea legs hands back roughly three weeks. Spend some of it on these — each is grounded in the route, and each is a place a Victorian traveller with an ounce of curiosity would have stopped. None is in the core itinerary above; all six are reachable from a stop you are already making.

Elephanta Caves — half a day from the Gateway of India

The boat ride is the point as much as the caves. A sixty-minute ferry from the Gateway jetties crosses Mumbai Harbour past the industrial port and naval dockyards to Gharapuri Island, where seventh-century rock-cut Hindu temples are carved into a basalt cliff. UNESCO listed them in 1987. The centrepiece of Cave 1 is the Sadashiva Trimurti, a five-metre three-headed bust of Shiva as creator, preserver and destroyer, and the surrounding panels run the full Shaivite programme — Shiva as Nataraja, as the cosmic androgyne Ardhanarishvara, playing dice with Parvati. Portuguese colonial troops did deliberate damage in the 16th century and what survives is still extraordinary.

How: ferries every 30 minutes 9 a.m.–3.30 p.m., ₹150 return, ₹10 extra for the upper deck (take it, for the bay views). Entry ₹600 foreigners / ₹40 Indians. Start with West Hill and Cave 1 (45–60 min), then Cave 3, then walk to East Hill for the quieter Buddhist Caves 6 and 7 and the ruined stupa. A toy train (₹10) or 30-minute walk connects jetty to caves. Closed Mondays. Allow 4–5 hours including both crossings, and take the 9 or 9.30 ferry to beat the crowds and the heat. Watch the monkeys on the staircases — they are quick and bold.

October is ideal: monsoon ending, calm water, clear light. June–August sea conditions can cancel the ferry outright.

Ajanta and Ellora — two days off the Bombay–Allahabad line

This is the largest missed opportunity on the Indian leg, and it sits directly on Fogg's railway. Get off the express at Jalgaon or Bhusawal — every Bombay–Howrah service stops at both — and you are 55 km from Ajanta: thirty Buddhist cave monasteries cut into a horseshoe cliff above the Waghora River, holding the finest surviving ancient Indian paintings anywhere in the world, from the 2nd century BCE to the 6th CE. They were rediscovered in 1819 by a British cavalry officer on a tiger hunt. Prioritise Caves 1 and 2 for the Vākāṭaka murals, 16 and 17 for the narrative panels, 26 for the sculpted Parinirvana Buddha, and 9 and 10 as the oldest chaitya halls.

Then overnight at Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar (Aurangabad) for Ellora: thirty-four Buddhist, Hindu and Jain temples cut from a single basalt escarpment between the 6th and 12th centuries, of which Cave 16, the Kailasha Temple, is a free-standing monolithic temple complex excavated from the top down — every column, elephant, courtyard and frieze cut from one hillside, over generations. Enter it first, at opening, and you may briefly have it to yourself.

How: Jalgaon → Ajanta taxi ₹1,200–1,500 one way, or MSRTC bus via Fardapur. Ask to be dropped at the upper Viewpoint a few kilometres before the gate — better views of the horseshoe, and you walk down from Cave 27 toward Cave 1 in the right direction. Ajanta: Tue–Sun 9.00–17.30, closed Monday, ₹600/₹40. Ellora: Wed–Mon sunrise–sunset, closed Tuesday, ₹300/₹25, golf cart for the Jain cluster. Guides ₹1,800 official. Slip-on shoes. The recommended shape: Jalgaon → Ajanta (day 1) → overnight Aurangabad → Ellora (day 2) → rejoin the express east.

Dakshineswar and Belur Math — a river half-day from Calcutta

The most atmospheric excursion from Kolkata, and the one that suits a sea-and-river trip best, because you should do it by boat. Dakshineswar Kali Temple is a nine-spired Navaratna complex on the Hooghly's east bank, commissioned by Rani Rashmoni Devi and completed in 1855, dedicated to Bhavatarini and flanked by twelve Shiva temples around a courtyard open to the river. It was home to Sri Ramakrishna Paramhansa, and the small room where he lived is preserved. With Kali Puja ten days off when you visit, the temple will be buzzing with preparation — fresh garlands, new idols arriving, an electric devotional energy.

Then cross the river by ferry to Belur Math, the international headquarters of the Ramakrishna Mission, founded in 1899 by Swami Vivekananda, who is buried here. The 1938 main temple is a deliberate architectural synthesis: a gate resembling a Buddhist torana, a floor plan echoing a Christian cross, windows drawing on Mughal design — a built argument for the unity of religions. The Hooghly at sunset from its steps is one of the most serene views in Bengal.

How: Kolkata Metro (Blue Line) north to Dakshineswar, about 45 minutes, air-conditioned — or, better, the ferry upstream from Babughat, roughly an hour and far more scenic, ₹15–20. Dakshineswar to Belur Math by ferry across the river, 10 minutes, ₹10. Both free to enter; shoe locker a small fee at Dakshineswar, and no photography inside the sanctum. Socks help — the courtyard is vast and hot underfoot. Budget 1.5–2 hours at Dakshineswar, 1 hour at Belur Math, 5–6 hours in total. Belur Math is open 06.30–noon and 15.30–20.00.

Macau — a full day from Hong Kong, and Verne names it

Verne mentions Macao directly in Chapter XIX — "separated by about sixty miles from the Portuguese town of Macao" — and it was Hong Kong's older rival for the China trade, Portuguese since 1557, and a centre of exactly the opium-and-merchant-shipping world the novel inhabits. Today its UNESCO-listed Historic Centre is one of the most intact Sino-Portuguese urban landscapes in Asia: cobbled lanes, baroque façades, wave-pattern tiles and 16th-century fortresses standing alongside Cantonese temples. November is the best season — warm, dry, and quieter than the October National Holiday rush.

Stop by stop: the Ruins of St Paul's, the stone façade of the 1662 Church of Mater Dei at the head of a wide staircase, all that survived the 1835 fire — buy bak kwa and pastéis de nata on the lane in front. Mount Fortress immediately uphill, a 1617 battery with cannon still ringing the battlements and the best views in the city; the Macau Museum inside is MOP$15, credit card only. Senado Square, paved in wave-pattern Portuguese tiles, ringed by the 1784 Leal Senado and the neoclassical post office. And Rua da Felicidade, the former opium and pleasure-house district behind Almeida Ribeiro — the most directly Verne-resonant street in Macau — where Fat Siu Lau has been serving since 1903.

How: Turbojet from Sheung Wan to Macau Outer Harbour, roughly hourly, about an hour, ~HK$175 one way. Or the Golden Bus over the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge, HK$65 and ~45 minutes to the bridge terminal. Bring your passport — it is an immigration crossing both ways. Taxi terminal to Old Town MOP$50; bus No. 3 MOP$6, exact cash. Return ferry by 7 p.m. to be back for dinner. Nine hours on the ground is comfortable.

Kamakura — a day from Yokohama, at the front edge of the maples

Japan's 13th-century samurai capital is 25 minutes from Yokohama Station and mid-to-late November is the best moment of its year: the temple precincts glow crimson and gold without the shoulder-to-shoulder crowds of late November. It also reaches Fogg's world by rail heritage — the Yokosuka line and the Enoden interurban are both part of the pleasure.

Tsurugaoka Hachimangū, the spine of the town, is approached along the 800-metre Dankazura avenue lined with cherry trees and stone lanterns, with the shrine on a hill at the end and views back to the sea; the flanking ginkgos are turning gold in mid-November. Free. Hōkoku-ji, the "bamboo temple," is a dense grove of two thousand mosō culms with a tea house serving matcha (¥700) inside it — ¥500 entry, and foliage peaking late November, so you catch it early and beautiful. Kōtoku-in holds the 13.35-metre bronze Amida Buddha, sitting in the open air since a tsunami washed away his hall in 1498 — ¥300, and ¥20 more to go inside the statue. Hase-dera next door has a terrace over Sagami Bay and a cave of votive Jizō figures, ¥400.

How: JR Yokosuka line, Yokohama → Kamakura, 25 min, ¥350; from Tokyo Station 60 min, ¥940. The Enoden runs along the coast to Hase for the Great Buddha, ¥260 a ride or ¥800 for a Noriorikun day pass. Temple precincts close 16.00–17.00 — start by 8.30. A weekday (16–18 November) is noticeably calmer than the weekend.

Sacramento — a day from San Francisco, where the line began

The best day trip on the American leg, and arguably the best on the whole route for a rail-minded traveller. Old Sacramento preserves the exact district from which the Central Pacific was built eastward — and Verne has Fogg's party pass through the city "towards midnight, while fast asleep," which is exactly the sort of detail that makes a stop feel earned.

The California State Railroad Museum is a Smithsonian Affiliate with over 2.5 acres of exhibits and the finest railroad collection on the Pacific coast: a roundhouse space filled with restored 19th-century locomotives, including Central Pacific engines. Among the exhibits is the Southern Pacific stained-glass window rescued from the Oakland Mole ferry terminal — a direct physical fragment of the place Fogg boarded his train. The interpretive panels on Chinese railroad labour and the Sierra Nevada construction are essential context for everything you will see over the next three days.

How: Amtrak Capitol Corridor from Emeryville (some services from Oakland Jack London Square), about 2 hours each way, ~$30–40 return, several trains daily. Or drive I-80 in about 90 minutes. Museum open daily 10.00–17.00, ~$12 adult, allow 2–3 hours; the reading room and library open Tue–Sat 13.00–17.00 for primary sources on the 1869 completion. Then walk two blocks west to the Old Sacramento waterfront — brick-and-timber commercial buildings from the 1850s–70s, many original — and the replica 1876 Central Pacific Passenger Station on Front Street. An 8 a.m. departure gives you the maximum day; five to six hours on the ground is right.

Book Capitol Corridor at least 24 hours ahead; trains sell out on popular days. If driving, leave Sacramento by 2 p.m. on a Friday — I-80 westbound backs up badly.
