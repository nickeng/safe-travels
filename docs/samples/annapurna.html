अन्नपूर्ण परिक्रमा · The Annapurna Circuit, Nepal

From rice terraces at 760 m to a prayer-flag cairn at 5,416 m — the great loop around the Annapurna massif, walked in the clearest month of the year.

The sound you remember is not the mountains. It is the bells — mule trains coming round a blind corner on a cliff path, forty animals deep, and the shouted "side, side!" that gives you two seconds to press yourself against the rock. Below, the Marsyangdi runs grey with glacier flour. Above, somewhere you cannot see yet, is a pass at 5,416 metres with prayer flags shredding in the wind.

The Annapurna Circuit walks a full loop around the eighth-highest massif on earth, and no two days of it look alike. You start in subtropical foothills at 760 m among rice terraces and banana trees. Eight days later you are on a Tibetan plateau at 3,540 m where the houses are flat-roofed stone, the women wear chuba, and the only fuel is dried yak dung. Then you cross Thorong La — the highest trekking pass most people will ever walk — and drop 1,700 m into a high desert on the other side, where the villages are whitewashed, the orchards smell of apples, and the wind arrives at ten in the morning like a scheduled event.

October is the single best month to do this. The monsoon clears in late September and takes the haze with it. What is left is a sky that photographers describe as absurd — Annapurna II, III and IV, Gangapurna, Tilicho Peak and Dhaulagiri all standing sharp enough to count the flutings on their faces. The trail is dry and firm. Thorong La is at its most reliable of the year: no monsoon mud, no deep winter snow.

It is also the busiest month, and the month Nepal's biggest festival lands squarely in the middle of. Both of those are worth planning around rather than avoiding — see below.

The shape of the walk

This is the profile that governs everything — your pace, your rest days, your appetite, your sleep. Read it as a story with one climax: a long patient rise over eight days, a single hard morning at the top, then a very long descent through three climate zones back to warm air and running water.

The right-hand half is what most trekkers underestimate. After the pass there is still a week of walking — including the single biggest climb of the whole circuit, the 1,700 m haul out of Tatopani up to Ghorepani, done on legs that already think they are finished.

The one rule that has changed — check it first

Since April 2023 Nepal has required trekkers in its national parks and conservation areas — including the whole Annapurna Conservation Area — to walk with a licensed guide from a registered agency. Sources disagree sharply on how consistently this is enforced on the Circuit, and the rule has been revised more than once. Confirm at the Nepal Tourism Board counter when you buy your ACAP, because it changes both your budget (USD 25–50/day) and your plan. For a first major trek at this altitude, a good guide is worth hiring regardless: they phone ahead for beds in peak season, read the weather, and — the part that matters — recognise altitude sickness in you before you do.

Solid green is the sweet spot: dry trail, cloudless mornings, every teahouse open and staffed. Gold is workable but compromised — pre-monsoon haze in spring, real cold from mid-November. The hatched stretches are the two seasons when the pass genuinely closes: winter snow, and the summer monsoon that turns the lower valley to mud and leeches.

Time itदशैं

Dashain lands mid-trek

11 – 25 October 2026 · Tika day Wed 21 Oct

Nepal's greatest festival runs the entire first half of your window, and for a trekker it cuts both ways. On the trail it is pure gift. In the cities it is a logistics problem with a hard deadline.

Dashain (बडा दशैं) is fifteen days honouring the goddess Durga's victory over the demon Mahishasura — Nepal's Christmas, Thanksgiving and national homecoming compressed into one. The whole country reorganises around it. Barley seeds (jamara) are sown in dark rooms on day one and cut fifteen days later. Bamboo swings — linge ping — go up over the rice terraces in every lower village on the circuit. Kites fill the afternoon sky above Kathmandu's rooftops. On Vijaya Dashami, elders press tika onto the foreheads of everyone younger and hand over small notes of cash.

Walking through the Marsyangdi villages during this is the warmest the trail gets all year — decorated doorways, families in new clothes, communal feasting, and lodge owners who will press tika on a passing foreigner as readily as on a nephew. Teahouses stay open throughout; the Circuit's high season and Dashain coincide every single year, and the families running those lodges plan their income around exactly this fortnight.

What it actually does to your plan

Get your permits by 16 October. The Nepal Tourism Board office closes from Phulpati (17 Oct) through roughly Dwadashi (23 Oct). If you land on the 18th without a permit you will wait almost a week. This is the single hardest constraint in the whole trip.

Book transport before you leave home. Buses and domestic flights between Kathmandu and Pokhara sell out for the 17–21 October window as the entire country travels home. Prices spike. If your return leg falls in there, book it before you start walking.

Draw cash early. Banks close and Pokhara's ATMs are known to run dry over the festival. There is no ATM on the circuit between Chame and Jomsom regardless.

Guides and porters go home for Tika. Agencies book out fast for pre-Dashain departures — reserve 6–8 weeks ahead for an October start.

The ideal solo timing

Land 7–9 October. Permits and gear on the 8th–9th, while offices are still open and Kathmandu is filling with festival energy. Start walking around the 11th (Ghatasthapana) so you pass through the lower villages during the festive build-up, and are high on the mountain — where none of the closures matter — through the 17–23 October crunch. You come down into Pokhara around the 24th as the country reopens.

If your dates slip later

Tihar (तिहार), the five-day festival of lights, falls 7–11 November 2026 — marigold garlands on every door frame, oil lamps on every ledge, and on Kukur Tihar the street dogs of Pokhara wearing flower necklaces. Laxmi Puja (8 Nov) is the luminous peak. Lighter disruption than Dashain, and beautiful to come back to.

The variableAir

Altitude is the whole trek

Everything else is just walking

Fitness is not what stops people on the Annapurna Circuit. Altitude is. You will spend five nights above 3,500 m before you reach the pass, and the difference between a trekker who crosses Thorong La feeling strong and one who turns back at Phedi with a splitting headache is almost never strength. It is patience — how willing they were to waste a day.

The rule is simple and non-negotiable: above 3,000 m, do not sleep more than about 500 m higher than the night before. Your highest point during the day can and should be far above where you sleep. That is the whole trick, and it has a name.

Climb high, sleep low

Walk up to 4,600 m on your rest day. Come back down to 3,540 m to sleep. Your body spends the night manufacturing red blood cells at an altitude it can actually recover at, having been shown what it needs to prepare for. This single drill, done properly at Manang, is the highest-value day of the trip — and it looks, from the outside, exactly like a day off.

One hour that is worth the whole rest day

The Himalayan Rescue Association has run a volunteer-doctor aid post in Manang since November 1981, and every afternoon in season they give a free public lecture on AMS, HAPE and HACE — what the symptoms feel like from the inside, and exactly when "descend" means right now rather than tomorrow. The HRA's own long-standing claim is that no trekker who attended their lecture has ever died of mountain sickness. Go. There is a second HRA clinic at Thorong Phedi.

Thorong Phedi or High Camp?

The only lodging choice on the circuit with a safety dimension.

High Camp (4,849 m) saves you 400 m of the dawn climb — but it breaks the 500 m rule on the worst possible night, in a dormitory, with pit toilets and no showers. Trekkers who pushed on report it "was freezing cold, the facilities weren't great, and they didn't sleep."

Sleep at Phedi (4,450 m). Walk up to High Camp in the afternoon for the view and the acclimatisation, then come back down. That is the drill, and it is free. Start an hour earlier instead.

Dal bhat power, 24 hour

Thakali cooking · the teahouse deal · why they call it the apple pie trek

No trekking route on earth changes its cuisine as sharply with altitude as this one. You begin in humid subtropical valleys where Gurung families cook rice and fresh greens, climb through the Thakali heartland where ghee-soaked black lentils and fermented pickle define the table, and come out the far side in rain-shadow Mustang, where buckwheat, yak and apple brandy tell you plainly that Tibet is just over the ridge.

The Thakali people, originally from the Thak Khola valley in upper Mustang, made their fortune on the ancient salt route between Tibet and the Indian plains — and prosperity bought them a cuisine of unusual sophistication for a high-altitude community. Its signatures are jimbu, timur, liberal ghee, and fermentation. The Thakali khana set — the meal the whole trekking world now just calls dal bhat — is their canonical form: rice or dhido with black-lentil soup tempered in jimbu and ghee, a vegetable curry, stir-fried greens, tomato-timur chutney, and fermented gundruk.

Refills are unlimited and expected. Stopping the server mid-pour is the only polite way to say you have had enough.

Small and frequent beats large and rare at altitude. The mid-morning khājā is the occasion visitors most often miss, and the reason they arrive at the next village depleted.

The teahouse deal — please honour it

Rooms cost USD 5–15 because you eat dinner and breakfast where you sleep. That is not etiquette, it is the business model, and in a village where every sack of rice arrived on a mule it is the only thing keeping the lodge open. Sleep at one lodge and eat at another and you should expect to pay properly for the room.

Eating smart above 3,000 m

Above 3,000 m — and unmistakably by Manang — your appetite simply switches off. That is a normal response to low oxygen and it is genuinely dangerous, because you are burning 3,000–4,500 calories a day. Order soup first; it lowers the activation energy for eating when you feel nothing. Take the dal bhat in small portions with refills rather than one big plate. Eat on the move.

Stick to freshly cooked vegetarian food. Every operator and every experienced trekker says the same thing, for the same reason: above 3,500 m the cold chain is unreliable and meat sits unrefrigerated. Eggs are the exception — they arrive in shell and are cooked to order.

There is no treated water anywhere on the circuit. Treat everything: a UV pen is fastest, chlorine-dioxide tablets are the cheap backup, and ACAP-run filtered refill stations in several villages charge NPR 40–100. Ask for tatho paani (hot water) at your lodge. Target 3–4 litres a day and drink it warm — thirst is a late signal at altitude.

Alcohol is worth skipping until you are descending. It dehydrates, worsens AMS and wrecks already-difficult sleep. A glass of Marpha apple brandy at 2,670 m on the far side of the pass is a different proposition from a beer in Manang at 3,540 m the night before you go higher.

Leg 1 · Earthपृथ्वी

Kathmandu

1,400 m · 2 days before, 1 after · Permits, gear, temples

Two days here is an errand list wearing the costume of a city break — and the errands are non-negotiable. But October gives you the best Kathmandu of the year: the monsoon has scrubbed the air, the Himalayan skyline is visible from the valley rim on a clear morning, and the whole city is decorating for Dashain.

Get the permits first, on your first full morning, before anything else. The Nepal Tourism Board office at Bhrikutimandap issues both your ACAP and TIMS card in one stop — bring your passport plus two photocopies, two passport photos, a copy of your visa, cash in rupees, your rough itinerary and your insurance details. Off-season you are out in thirty minutes; in peak October allow two hours. Then walk the gear strip in Thamel, and make a stack of photocopies of everything before you leave — checkpoints along the trail will want them.

With the afternoons that remain, do the three great religious sites, and use one clear morning on Chandragiri — a cable car lifts you above the valley smog to a viewpoint where, on an October day, you can see from Annapurna to Everest. Standing there looking at the range you are about to walk into is the best possible overture to this trip.

The three great sites

Do these in the afternoons, once the permits are done. Boudhanath at dusk and Pashupatinath at dusk are both evening propositions, so they need separate days.

Squares, errands, and the first look at the range

Photograph

The single most time-critical shot in the city has nothing to do with temples: the dawn produce and flower market in the lanes beside Durbar Square, which starts before seven and has cleared out by eight.

Eat

Kathmandu is at least two food cultures running in parallel. The Newar people, the valley's original inhabitants, built a cuisine of ferocious complexity that still lives in the backstreet bhatti of Patan. Alongside it runs the broader Nepali kitchen, Thakali food from the north, and momos so thoroughly naturalised that most Kathmanduites think of them as their own. Eat at khājā time — mid-afternoon — when the courtyard bhattis are at their most alive.

Sleep

Thamel is where you stay before the trek — every gear shop, agency and pharmacy within a ten-minute walk, and other trekkers to compare notes with. For the night you come back, consider trading up: Lazimpat for heritage quiet fifteen minutes' walk north, or Boudhanath, where the Hyatt Regency has a back gate that opens straight onto the stupa's kora path. Almost every Thamel hotel stores your non-trekking luggage free while you walk — confirm on check-in.

Leg 2 · Waterमार्स्याङ्दी

Up the Marsyangdi

760 → 3,660 m · Days 3–5 · Besisahar · Chame · Pisang · Ghyaru · Ngawal

This is the leg that does the transformation. You start among banana trees and rice terraces and finish, three days later, in a Tibetan-influenced stone village at 3,660 m with the Annapurna massif filling the entire southern sky. The river is the through-line: the Marsyangdi, grey with glacier flour, crossed and re-crossed on suspension bridges that bounce.

The jeep decision. A rough road now runs from Besisahar as far as Manang, and the stretch below Chame is where it has done the most damage — dusty, noisy, and the least interesting walking on the circuit. Take the jeep from Besisahar to Chame on day 3 (~5–6 hr, bumpy, unforgettable in its own way) and spend the two days you save on the high route above Pisang instead. If you would rather walk it, the lower villages are lovely and the teahouses there are the most comfortable of the whole trek — Jagat, Dharapani and the high-meadow variant through Timang are the stops to aim for, and Timang delivers your first clear sight of Manaslu (8,163 m).

The one route choice that matters on this leg

Above Pisang the trail splits. The lower route follows the jeep road through Lower Pisang and is faster. The high route climbs 370 m to Ghyaru (3,670 m) and then contours to Ngawal — and it holds the finest mountain views on the entire circuit, passes through two medieval Tibetan villages the road never sees, and sleeps you higher, which is better for acclimatisation. Every guide and every independent trekker recommends it unconditionally. It costs an extra hour. Take it.

See & do

Sleep

Teahouse standards are at their peak on this leg and decline steadily from here — road access means supplies arrive often, rooms are better insulated, and hot showers ($2–4 extra) are reliable. Arrive by 4 pm in October; rooms rarely run out down here, but the good ones go early.

Leg 3 · Fireमनाङ

Manang, and the wasted days

3,540 m · Days 6–8 · Three nights, two of them deliberately still

Manang is where the trek stops being pleasant and becomes serious — and where the most important thing you do is nothing. It is the natural halfway house of the circuit: a broad dry valley of flat-roofed stone houses with Gangapurna's glacier hanging directly above the rooftops, a main street busy with trekkers from thirty countries, and side lanes that have not changed in a very long time.

It has also become an unlikely mountain town. There are bakeries here at 3,540 m turning out fresh bread, apple crumble and real coffee. There is a cinema that shows Caravan, the Nepali film shot in these valleys. There is an Everest Link wifi signal that actually works. And there is the Himalayan Rescue Association aid post, which is the reason to base yourself here rather than in quieter Braga twenty minutes down the trail.

Spend three nights. Use the two full days on the acclimatisation drill laid out above — Praken Gompa and the HRA lecture on the first, Ice Lake on the second. Then stock up on snacks, because prices from here to Muktinath only go one way.

Eat

Sleep

Manang has the best lodging on the circuit, and the choice worth thinking about is Manang or Braga. Manang has the bakeries, the wifi and the altitude clinic. Braga, twenty minutes back down the trail, is quieter, has a 500-year-old monastery on its doorstep, and is the better trailhead for Ice Lake. For a first major trek, the clinic tips it to Manang — with Braga as the pick if the village noise is exactly what you want to escape.

Leg 4 · Airथोरोङ ला

Thorong La, 5,416 m

Days 9–11 · Yak Kharka · Thorong Phedi · the pass · Muktinath

You leave at four in the morning in the dark, in a temperature somewhere below −10 °C, and you join a line of headtorches already strung up the slope above you. Nobody talks much. The trail is unmistakable and it goes up for four hours. Then the ground levels, and there is a cairn, and a mess of prayer flags shredded to ribbons by wind that never stops, and a hand-painted board that says THORONG LA PASS 5416M — and 360 degrees of the Annapurna massif and Dhaulagiri, with the brown plateau of Mustang running north to Tibet.

It is not technical. There are no ropes, no crampons, no climbing skill required. It is simply high — oxygen at the summit is roughly half what it is at sea level — and long. The full day from Thorong Phedi to Muktinath climbs about 1,000 m and drops 1,600 m over 12–15 km, and most people are on their feet for eight to ten hours.

Respect the weather, genuinely

October is the most reliable month of the year on this pass — dry surface, clear visibility, a trail you cannot lose. But early snowfall does happen, and a snow closure can strand trekkers on either side for one to three days. The October 2014 storm here killed 43 people. Ask your lodge owner about conditions the evening before, find out which parties are leaving early, and carry a food and cash buffer. If the answer is "not today", it is not today.

Two nights before, at 4,050 m

The days from Manang to Phedi are meant to be short — three hours, then a long empty afternoon. That is the point, not a scheduling failure. From Yak Kharka the classic drill is an afternoon stroll up toward Ledar (4,200 m) and back down to sleep. Mule trains pass. Yaks graze on nothing. The Chulu Range fills the view ahead. There is little to do but walk slowly, drink water and take in the scale.

Leg 5 · Skyमुस्ताङ

Down the Kali Gandaki

3,760 → 1,190 m · Days 11–13 · Muktinath · Kagbeni · Jomsom · Marpha · Tatopani

The far side is a different planet. You step off a moonscape of ice and scree into a Tibetan-inflected high desert of ochre cliffs, whitewashed villages and chortens strung with flags — and then, south of Marpha, the valley abruptly goes green. All of it happens inside the deepest river gorge on earth, wedged between Dhaulagiri (8,167 m) and Annapurna I (8,091 m), both over 5,500 m above the water you are walking beside.

First comes Muktinath, one of the holiest places in both Hinduism and Buddhism, where pilgrims walk a circuit bathing under 108 bull-head spouts of glacial water and a natural gas jet burns as an eternal flame inside a stream — fire within water, worshipped by both faiths. In October the Dashain pilgrimage traffic makes it extraordinary. Arriving here after the pass, with thicker air and the smell of incense and juniper smoke, is one of the great reliefs of the trip.

On the way down to Kagbeni, take the thirty-minute detour to Jharkot, where a red gompa sits on the edge of the hillside with the whole Mustang valley below it — the best terrace view on the descent.

The Kali Gandaki wind — the one thing that will shape every day here

Cold air off the Tibetan plateau and warm air from the south trade places through this gorge every single day, and the 7,000 m valley walls turn the exchange into a wind tunnel. It reaches Kagbeni at full force by 10 am and peaks around noon to 2 pm, carrying abrasive dust. Walking south you walk into it. The rule is universal and simple: be moving by 7–8 am and have your exposed sections done before eleven. Carry a buff and sunglasses. South of Marpha the valley widens and the wind largely dies. It also closes Jomsom airport to afternoon flights — book the first slot of the day, and budget a jeep as backup.

Walk it or jeep it — an honest section-by-section

A jeep road runs the length of this valley now, which makes every day a decision. This is the consensus of trekkers who have walked all of it:

Look down as well as up

The Kali Gandaki riverbed between Jomsom and Kagbeni is one of the world's great sources of shaligram — black ammonite fossils in smooth dark stones, sacred to Vaishnavite Hindus as natural forms of Vishnu. They formed when this valley was the floor of the Tethys Sea, and the collision that built the Himalaya lifted them to 2,800 m. The compressed and folded sedimentary layers in the canyon walls above you are the same story at a different scale. Take only what is freely offered; ACAP rules apply.

Leg 6 · Earthपुन हिल

The last sunrise

Days 14–16 · Tatopani · Ghorepani · Poon Hill · Pokhara

The string of flags has run through all five colours, and now it starts again at earth — which is exactly what these last days are. You come back down into terraced farmland, warm air and rice harvest, and you finish with one more 4 am alarm for the most famous sunrise in the Himalaya.

The hardest climb comes last

Nobody warns you about this properly. The haul from Tatopani (1,190 m) up to Ghorepani (2,874 m) is 1,700 m of ascent in a single day — the biggest climb of the entire circuit, done on the fourteenth day, on legs that have already crossed a 5,416 m pass. Around 15–22 km, seven to nine hours. It is stone stairs from Shikha onward, relentlessly.

Start from Tatopani no later than 7:30 am — 7:00 if this is your first major trek. Take a long lunch at Shikha before the final push. If the day breaks you, split it with a night at Shikha (1,935 m); nobody will judge you and you will enjoy Poon Hill considerably more.

Soak in Tatopani's hot springs the evening before. That is not indulgence, it is preparation.

Poon Hill, and the crowd

Wake at 4:00–4:30. Forty-five to sixty minutes of stone steps by headtorch, a NPR 100 gate fee, and then a small observation tower where — in peak October — hundreds of people are already gathered. Be at the top by 5:00 am if you want a front position.

What you get is worth the scrum: a panorama spanning three ranges at once. Dhaulagiri (8,167 m) to the northwest, the Annapurna group across the centre — Annapurna I at 8,091 m, Annapurna South, Hiunchuli — and the unmistakable unclimbed tent-peak of Machhapuchhre (Fishtail, 6,993 m) to the east. First light hits Dhaulagiri before it reaches the Annapurnas, then the whole horizon runs gold, orange, and finally brilliant white. In post-monsoon October air, every peak is clean-edged. No other short-trek viewpoint in Nepal spans this much sky.

If your first morning is clouded out, stay a second night and try again. It is the only viewpoint on this trip worth a re-run.

The forest between

The 15 km from Ghorepani east to Tadapani passes through what guides call the largest continuous rhododendron forest in the Himalaya. The flowers are a spring phenomenon — but in October the canopy mixes giant rhododendron with maple in full autumn colour, moss-draped oak and giant fir, with waterfalls running full off the side streams and langur monkeys in the high branches. Tadapani itself has an unobstructed eastern view of Machhapuchhre that is more intimate than Poon Hill's panorama, and you will often have the teahouse deck to yourself.

The finish

Pokhara — do almost nothing, deliberately

You arrive at 800 m having spent two weeks between 760 and 5,416 m, and your body is running a real deficit: accumulated sleep debt from altitude, depleted glycogen, wind-scoured skin, and legs that have absorbed tens of thousands of downhill steps. The most common mistake here is treating Pokhara as another destination to conquer.

Don't. Take one genuinely idle day first — a massage, the lakeside, food, sleep — and then be selective. The single highest-value hour of your entire stay is an hour at Seeing Hands, a social enterprise that trains and employs visually impaired massage therapists on Middle Path Street. Book at least a full hour of full-body work; after a circuit the specific damage is quads and calves from the Thorong La descent, plus shoulders from a fortnight under a pack. Book ahead in October, and tip.

Then: a wooden rowboat on Phewa Tal with the whole Annapurna range reflected in it, one 4:45 am taxi to Sarangkot for a sunrise that rivals Poon Hill for a fraction of the effort, and — the one museum that will land differently because of what you just did — the International Mountain Museum, which has a relief model of the Annapurna region you can trace your own route across. Statistically, 31 October is the clearest day of the year here.

Don't swim in Phewa Lake; it's reported contaminated. Begnas, east of town, is the clean alternative. And on the forest trail up to the World Peace Pagoda, solo travellers — women especially — should go mid-morning when the path is busy, or take a taxi to the top: harassment cases have been reported there.

The lake and the ridge

Recovery, and one last mountain view

Eat — the meal you have been thinking about since Manang

Pokhara is the Thakali homeland, so the dal bhat here is the real article rather than the trekking-lodge version — richer, more aromatic, five or six rotating sides instead of two. Thakali kitchens stop serving lunch at 2:00–2:30 pm sharp, so plan around it. Then there is the other half of the town: a Lakeside strip built by fifty years of arriving trekkers craving exactly what you are craving, which is unexpectedly good at pasta, wood-fired pizza and cake.

Sleep

Lakeside Central is where you want to be for zero logistical friction — laundry, pharmacies, massage, gear storage and forty restaurants within a ten-minute walk, and a solo-but-never-lonely atmosphere where eating alone is completely normal. Trade up if you want a splurge: the peninsula, or the ridge at Sarangkot where you wake up inside the sunrise instead of commuting to it.

If you have+ Days

Three detours worth the extra days

What the 16-day plan leaves out

None of these is in the itinerary above, and the trip is complete without them. But each is the thing a returning trekker says they wish they had made room for.

The one most people regret skipping

Tilicho Lake · 4,919 m

+3–4 days from Manang · on foot only · no jeep road

One of the highest large lakes on earth: a turquoise sheet cupped in a glacial amphitheatre beneath Tilicho Peak (7,134 m) and the sheer ice wall known as the Great Barrier. In October it is fully ice-free and at its most vivid blue-green; by late October the margins begin to freeze. Because the detour costs real effort, far fewer trekkers make it — and the silence up there is something first-hand visitors describe as almost physical.

Here is the argument that actually matters for a first-timer: it does your acclimatisation for you. You reach 4,919 m and sleep two nights above 4,000 m before you ever go near Thorong La. Trekkers who do this consistently report crossing the pass with fewer symptoms than those who go straight there. As one couple put it, "because we reached the line of 5,000 metres, we were well acclimatised and crossed Thorong La without greater difficulties."

The day that needs respect is the traverse from Shree Kharka to Tilicho Base Camp — a two-mile crossing of an active debris slope below sheer rock walls, narrow in places, with continuous small rockfall. It is not technically hard; mule trains use it. But it degrades badly through the day: "the sun and hot weather in the afternoon loosens the rocks, and the path is more dangerous." Leave Shree Kharka by 7–8 am while the ground is still frozen, move continuously through the exposed sections rather than resting in them, carry poles, and if you hear bells, find a stable spot and wait for the mules. Leave your main pack at Shree Kharka — lodges there store luggage, and one trekker called that "the best advice I got."

How it fits 16 days: it doesn't, quite. Either accept a 19–20 day trip, or swap out the Ghorepani/Poon Hill finish and exit at Jomsom or Beni instead. If you're choosing between Tilicho and Poon Hill, the honest answer is that Tilicho is the rarer experience and Poon Hill is the more reliable one.

💡 Sleep at Shree Kharka, not Tilicho Base Camp, the night before the lake. Base Camp has only three lodges and maybe 30–50 beds total, and in October guided groups of 8–15 fill them before mid-afternoon. Shree Kharka is quieter, has better food, stores bags — and puts you in position for the early-morning slide crossing.

One extra day on the way out

Ghandruk & Tadapani · 1,940 m

+1 day from Ghorepani · 3–4 hr from Tadapani, then 3–4 hr down to Nayapul

Instead of dropping straight from Ghorepani to Nayapul, contour east through the rhododendron forest to Tadapani and down to Ghandruk — the largest Gurung settlement in Nepal and one of the best-preserved traditional villages in the whole trekking region. Two-storey stone houses under slate roofs, lanes so narrow that mules scrape the walls, terraced fields tumbling south, and Annapurna South, Hiunchuli and Machhapuchhre filling the entire northern wall of sky. It has resisted the concrete-and-tin modernisation that has taken most trail towns.

The Gurung Museum on the main lane — twenty minutes, small fee — displays costumes, weapons and ceremonial objects, and the community's long association with the British Gurkha regiments. It gives context to everything you then walk past. Wake for sunrise from a teahouse rooftop and listen to the monastery prayers starting below you; the old quarter, Purano Ghandruk, is the best village-with-peaks composition on the circuit. Then walk out through Kimche and the Modi Khola villages in October, past women cutting and stacking rice along the terraces — the loveliest hour of walking on the whole trip, precisely because you know it's ending.

A day at either end, from Kathmandu

Bhaktapur · 13 km east

45 min by taxi (~NPR 1,000) · USD 18 heritage ticket, valid 7 days · full day

The showpiece of the Kathmandu Valley, and the best possible use of a spare pre- or post-trek day. Bhaktapur is a medieval Newar city with a largely car-free brick core, and it has been preserved far more carefully than the capital — the density of architecture, religious life and food culture makes it feel like a genuinely different world half an hour from Thamel.

Work east to west through the four squares. Dattatreya Square is the oldest, with a 1427 pagoda said to be carved from the timber of a single tree, and the 15th-century Peacock Window — the "Mona Lisa of Nepal" — best photographed from the first floor of the souvenir shop opposite. Bhaktapur Durbar Square holds the 55-Window Palace and the gilded Golden Gate. Taumadhi Square is dominated by Nyatapola, a five-storey pagoda from 1702 and the tallest traditional structure in Nepal, one of the few here to survive the 2015 earthquake untouched. Pottery Square is a working zone where the Prajapati community still throws clay on wheels; on a dry October day the whole square is covered in black pots drying in the sun.

Eat Newari while you're here: samay baji on a Taumadhi rooftop, bara from a lane stall, and juju dhau — Bhaktapur's "king curd", buffalo-milk yoghurt set in unglazed clay pots, thick enough to hold a spoon upright — on the way out. If you can, stay overnight: the square at 7 am, in low raking light with locals at morning prayer instead of tour groups, is the finest photograph in the valley.

  • — The mandatory-guide rule. Sources genuinely conflict on whether and how the 2023 licensed-guide requirement is enforced on the Circuit. Confirm at the Nepal Tourism Board counter; it changes your budget by USD 25–50 a day.
  • — ACAP and TIMS fees. Revised periodically. Figures here are NPR 3,000 and NPR 2,000; some 2026 operator sources suggest TIMS may no longer be required for ACAP treks. Check at the counter.
  • — Exact NTB closure dates over Dashain. "Phulpati to Dwadashi" (17–23 Oct) is the pattern every year, but confirm with the NTB or a Thamel agency before booking flights.
