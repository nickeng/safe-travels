Antarctica

Two questions decide this trip: when you go, and which Antarctica you go to. Here are both answers.

Nothing prepares you for the sound. Not the cold — you packed for the cold — but the noise a place makes when there is no engine, no aircraft, no insect, no leaf. A tabular iceberg the size of a city block grinds against pack ice and the whole hull hums. Ice a thousand years old fizzes as it releases its trapped air beside the Zodiac. Then, from a black-sand amphitheatre above you, a hundred and forty thousand chinstraps talking at once. Antarctica smells of nothing at all until the wind turns and a penguin colony arrives on it, sharp as ammonia. And the light never behaves: it rakes sideways for four hours at a stretch, or it goes flat white and blinding, or a shaft breaks through a storm cell and turns a berg cobalt for ninety seconds.

This is the only continent with no permanent human population, no towns, no roads and no visa. You cannot independently travel here; you join a ship, or in a handful of cases a plane, and you go under the rules of the IAATO and the Antarctic Treaty. Which is why the two questions that matter are the ones you asked — when, and which route. Get those right and everything else follows.

The short answer on timing: the season is roughly late October to late March, and nothing else. Within it, three genuinely different trips. Late October–November gives you pristine untracked snow, courting penguins, the biggest most intact icebergs, hours-long golden light — and the only window on Earth for emperor penguins. December–January gives you chicks, near-24-hour light, the warmest weather and the widest access. February–March gives you whales in numbers that surround the boat, fledging chicks, the return of real sunsets, the emptiest landing sites and the best prices.

For a traveller weighting wildlife and photography equally, the sharpest answer is February. It is the one month where peak whale density, chicks still in noisy crèches, minimum sea ice (so the Antarctic Circle is genuinely reachable), and a returning golden hour all overlap. Swoop's late-season specialists put it plainly: if maximum wildlife and photography time in Antarctica itself is the only thing that matters, a 12–14 day Peninsula-and-Circle voyage sailing in February is the sweet spot. Use the tabs at the top to switch the whole guide between the three seasons.

Know before you look

  • No visa, no currency, no fixed itinerary. Your ship's captain rewrites the day every morning for ice and weather. Plan around experiences, not specific sites.
  • The 100-person rule. No more than 100 passengers ashore at one site at one time. Over 500 passengers and a ship cannot land anyone at all. Ship size is your shore time.
  • Book 12–18 months out — 18–24 for Christmas/New Year, and as early as the season opens for emperor-penguin departures.
  • Arrive two days early at your gateway city. Flights into Ushuaia and Punta Arenas are routinely delayed by wind and fog, and no ship waits.
  • 5 metres from wildlife, always. Drones are effectively banned. Biosecurity — vacuuming every layer and bag — is enforced, not a formality.
The honest caveat

Nothing here is guaranteed. Emperor-penguin landings at Snow Hill succeed under half the time. The Lemaire Channel can be ice-choked. South Georgia landings are cancelled by surf. Go with calibrated expectations and the continent will over-deliver anyway.

Question one

When to go

The Antarctic season is short, and every fortnight inside it trades something away. Pick the tab that matches what you most want to photograph — the guide's photos, places, map and palette all change with it.

01

Late October & November — the ice at its most alive

Early season is Antarctica at its most volatile and its most photogenic. Temperatures swing between −10 °C and 0 °C and katabatic winds cut through good gear; within an hour the sky can go from heavy snowfall to piercing blue. Sea ice is only beginning to loosen and new ice still forms overnight, so the Lemaire Channel is often choked shut — transiting it is a gift, not a schedule item. Many landing sites are still buried in clean snow, which means no guano beaches, no smell, and white backgrounds at every colony. Port Lockroy isn't staffed until early November. Almost no other ships are out, so you routinely have a site entirely to yourself.

What's uniquely in season: Adélie, gentoo and chinstrap penguins streaming back to their colonies, males frantically gathering stones as courtship gifts and stealing them from each other; the first eggs from late October through mid-November; Weddell seals pupping on fast ice; elephant seal bulls fighting for beaches on South Georgia and the Falklands. And, in the Weddell Sea, the one window in the year when emperor penguin chicks are still standing on solid sea ice.

For photographers the case is simple and the research is unanimous: low sun angles produce sunrises and sunsets that last for hours, icebergs are still massive and intact with deep blue crevasses that will have melted by February, and snow covers the colonies in fresh white perfection rather than the brown guano-streaked ground of peak season. Cross-country skiing and snowshoeing on unbroken annual sea ice are possible in early November and nowhere near it later.

02

December & January — the midnight sun

Midsummer is the beating heart of the season and the reason most people come. Water-level temperatures run 0 °C to 8 °C, the seas are the calmest of the year, and daylight stretches to 20–22 hours: there is no true night, only a long low polar twilight around midnight that stains icebergs amber and violet before the sun climbs again. Sea ice has retreated enough that Zodiacs can work in among brash ice and bergy bits almost everywhere, and the Antarctic Circle becomes a realistic objective.

The rookeries are at maximum chaos. Chicks hatch through December and by January they are forming crèches — begging, chasing, squabbling, while adults commute relentlessly to the sea. Every seabird breeding on the Peninsula is present. Whale numbers climb steadily; by late January humpbacks are regularly encountered right through the Gerlache Strait and Wilhelmina Bay. South Georgia's beaches hold king penguin chicks in brown woolly down and fur seal pups on every metre of sand.

The honest trade-off is the light. Midsummer sun is bright, high and high-contrast, reflecting fiercely off snow and ice; the warm palette of sunrise and sunset belongs to the shoulders of the season. Oceanwide guide Matt Cheok's advice for January is to slow down and shoot wider: "the light feels endless… take your time on landing sites and let the scene play out. You don't always need to shoot so tightly — use a wide-angle lens to capture more context." This is also the most crowded and most expensive window; Christmas and New Year departures book 18–24 months out.

03

February & March — everything at once, and the light comes back

February and March are the most rewarding months on the Peninsula for one blunt reason: the animals are everywhere at the same time. The krill bloom has been building since November and by February the entire food chain is running flat out. Humpbacks and minkes feed in densities that regularly surround a Zodiac — and in March, with feeding pressure easing, they become actively inquisitive and approach the boat rather than merely tolerating it. The chicks that hatched in December are now adolescent, creching in loud masses and making their first ungainly, catastrophic trips into the water. Leopard seals know this and patrol the ice edge accordingly.

February averages around 1 °C with long, stable days; March drops to roughly −5 to 0 °C, gets windier, and loses about fifteen minutes of daylight a day. Sea ice is at its annual minimum, which makes February the most reliable month of all for an Antarctic Circle crossing and opens the Weddell Sea's tabular-iceberg country. One caveat to plan around: the airfield at Frei Station on King George Island typically closes mid-February, so March departures nearly always sail the Drake.

Then the light. Every day the sun runs a lower arc, and by March golden hour stretches for two to four hours and icebergs turn amber and rose. Real darkness returns from about 25 February at 65°S — the first star fields since October, and with luck the aurora australis from the deck. From roughly 10–15 March most ships have repositioned north: no other vessels on the horizon, landings where your group is the only humans for hundreds of miles, and unsold cabins going 20–30% below list.

Gear

What to pack

Your operator supplies the two things you can't improvise: a parka (usually yours to keep) and rubber muck boots for wet landings. Everything below is what actually decides whether you come home with the pictures.

Shooting in the cold

  • 3–4 spare batteries — at −10 °C lithium cells die in 30–60 minutes; keep spares in an inside vest pocket against your body
  • Wide zoom, 16–35 mm — icebergs, colony context, and South Georgia beaches where a telephoto is useless
  • 300–500 mm reach — wildlife from a Zodiac, orca from the deck, emperors across flagged sea ice
  • Dry bag + rain cover — Zodiac spray, blowing snow and splash landings are guaranteed, not possible
  • Polariser — cuts surface glare to reveal penguins swimming underwater off Cuverville
  • Microfibre cloths, several — condensation every time you step inside; salt spray every time you step out

On your body

  • Waterproof trousers — you sit on a wet Zodiac tube for hours; jeans end the excursion
  • Liner + heavy gloves — liners let you work dials without bare skin on frozen metal
  • Balaclava or neck gaiter — katabatic wind, especially early season
  • High-factor sunscreen and lip balm — UV bouncing off ice and snow burns in cloud
  • Sunglasses plus a spare — snow blindness is real and a dropped pair is gone
  • Seasickness meds, prescription — scopolamine patch or ondansetron, started 12–24 h before the Drake

Leave at home

  • Drone — effectively banned near wildlife under IAATO rules and refused outright by most operators; South Georgia won't permit one at all
  • Tripod for the colonies — tripod stools were prohibited outright on 2026 expeditions under avian-flu protocols; you also can't kneel or lie down
  • Umbrella — meaningless in Antarctic wind; a hood is the only answer
  • Velcro-heavy outerwear — it collects the seeds and grit that biosecurity vacuuming exists to remove

Biosecurity, honestly

You will vacuum every layer, every bag, every camera strap and every boot tread before you land — at home, and again on board before each landing. Between June 2025 and May 2026 South Georgia's government ran 82 vessel searches with biosecurity dog teams. Failing to comply is an offence, not an embarrassment.

Avian influenza has been confirmed in the region since 2023 and has caused dynamic site closures. Do not touch anything dead or sick, keep off the ground, and check current protocols with your operator — they change season to season and can affect the postures you're allowed to shoot from.

Early-season extra

Electric hand warmers and eye shades earn their weight in late October, and a lens focus-limiter setting is worth learning before you go — in heavy snowfall autofocus locks onto the nearest snowflake instead of the orca.

Midsummer extra

Bring a proper sleep mask. At 22 hours of daylight your cabin never truly darkens, and the best light of the day arrives around 11 pm — you will want to be awake for it and asleep at some other point.

Late-season extra

Pack for astrophotography. Real darkness returns to 65°S around 25 February, giving you the first star fields since October and a genuine shot at the aurora australis from the deck — so bring a fast wide lens and something to brace it against in wind.

Dates worth aiming at

Days that mark the season

Antarctica has no population and therefore no festivals — but three dates carry real weight on board, and one of them is a photographer's reason to choose a departure.

≈ 21 December

The Antarctic solstice

At 64°S the sun stops setting for weeks. It tracks low around the horizon in a continuous loop, sitting 5–15° above it for hours — roughly 10 pm to 2 am ship time — laying long warm light across pack ice and tabular bergs. Expedition teams mark it with deck gatherings and dinners; Quark has timed departures specifically to be in Antarctic waters on the day. The single best planned light of midsummer.

21 November

The Endurance anniversary

On 21 November 1915 Shackleton's ship finally sank in the Weddell Sea pack after ten months beset. Every November voyage passes through the general vicinity, and expedition historians lecture on it that evening — the resonance of looking out at the same ice is the point. The wreck itself was rediscovered in March 2022, three kilometres down and far south of where tourist ships can reach.

1 December

Antarctica Day

The anniversary of the 1959 signing of the Antarctic Treaty — the agreement that demilitarised the continent and dedicated it to science, and the reason you can visit at all. Marked at research stations including Scott Base and McMurdo, and worth marking on board. Antarctica Day campaign

Also worth timingOn the Ross Sea route, January departures fall on the anniversaries of Amundsen reaching the Pole (14 December 1911) and Scott reaching it (17 January 1912) — lecturers mark both at sea, within sight of the shelf the parties crossed.

04

Sail the Drake, or fly it

Eight hundred kilometres of Southern Ocean sit between Cape Horn and the South Shetlands, and you either cross them or you don't. Sailing takes about 48 hours each way from Ushuaia and ranges from the glassy "Drake Lake" to a six-metre "Drake Shake" — one traveller who crossed four times reported four completely different oceans. It is also, genuinely, part of the trip: wandering and black-browed albatrosses, giant petrels, prions and cape petrels ride the ship's updraughts at arm's length for two days, the lecture programme builds real anticipation, and the slow approach earns the arrival.

Flying — a two-to-three-hour charter from Punta Arenas to King George Island, where your ship waits — buys back four days. An 8-day fly/fly voyage delivers roughly the Peninsula time of an 11-day sailing. The costs are real: roughly $2,000–5,000 more per person, and two simultaneous weather windows must align, so 12–48 hour delays are routine. A hybrid — fly south, sail north — is the compromise most operators now sell, and arguably the best of the three.

One caveat for late-season travellers: the Frei Station airfield on King George Island typically closes in mid-February, so March departures sail, whether you like it or not.

Ship size is your shore time

IAATO's single most consequential rule: 100 passengers ashore per site, maximum. Everything follows from it.

≤100 paxEveryone lands at once. No rotations, no waiting.
~130 paxKayakers split off; effectively zero compromise.
~200 paxTwo rotations. ~30 min less ashore; you may be aboard when the whales show.
500 paxFive rotations. Fine for scenery, poor for South Georgia.
>500 paxNo landings at all. Scenic cruising only.

For wildlife and photography, 130 passengers or fewer is the clear recommendation. South Georgia demands it: the walk from the beach to Salisbury Plain or St Andrews Bay is 20–30 minutes each way, and a big ship simply doesn't give you enough time ashore to make it worth doing.

Ice class, in one line

PC6 or Lloyd's 1A is plenty for the Peninsula, the Circle and South Georgia. PC5 or stronger is strongly preferred for deep Weddell or Ross Sea ice-edge work. Only Le Commandant Charcot (PC2) can reliably reach the heart of the Weddell rather than merely attempt it.

05

So which one?

If you have 10–14 days and want the best photographs: Peninsula plus Antarctic Circle, sailing from Ushuaia, departing February. Whales at maximum, chicks still creching, minimum ice so the Circle is genuinely on, golden hour back, and fewer ships. Around $9,000–18,000 per person.

If you can give it three weeks and the budget stretches: Falklands, South Georgia and the Peninsula. Six penguin species, the greatest concentration of charismatic megafauna on Earth, and — as Swoop's polar specialist John Newby puts it — "I wouldn't consider a larger vessel if you're visiting South Georgia. If you're voyaging to the end of the Earth, you want time to properly enjoy it." Late December to early February is the window here.

If emperor penguins are the whole point: a helicopter-equipped Weddell Sea voyage in November, booked 12–18 months out, with your expectations calibrated — Oceanwide reports a success rate under 50%, and some seasons nobody lands at all. Or the fly-in camp at Gould Bay, which costs an order of magnitude more and delivers four days living beside the colony.

Where you actually go · 90% of all voyages

The Peninsula, sound by sound

A 1,300 km arm of the continent reaching north toward South America, flanked by the Gerlache Strait and its archipelago on one side and the Weddell Sea on the other. This is the Antarctica you have seen photographs of. Sites below are tagged to the season you've selected — switch tabs and the set changes.

06

The channels

The Peninsula's great subject isn't an animal, it's the ice — and the corridors cut through it. Ships transit these in the small hours precisely because that is when the light is worth being awake for. None of it is a landing; all of it is shot from the bow, the aft rail, or a Zodiac at water level.

07

Ice, weather & light

Antarctic weather is a subject in its own right, not an obstacle to one. Fog, storm cells, shafts of sun and the returning golden hour all produce images you cannot get anywhere else — and all of them reward being on deck when everyone else has gone inside.

Technique that matters here

Overcast is your friend: flat light kills glare and lets the blue in the ice saturate. Ask your Zodiac driver to put the boat on the shadow side of a berg — that's where the interior cobalt shows. Shoot RAW and expose about ⅔ stop over for snow. Crop wide: a letterbox ratio strips out dead water and makes an iceberg read horizontally. And keep a camera charged and to hand at all times — orca and porpoising penguins arrive without warning, mid-dinner.

08

Behaviour, up close

Penguins are not shy here; the rules exist to protect them from you, not you from them. Keep five metres and they will routinely close the gap themselves. Get low and slow — eye level with a knee-high bird is the difference between a snapshot and a portrait — but note that under current avian-flu protocols you may not kneel, sit or lie down at many sites, and tripod stools were banned outright on 2026 expeditions.

Wildlife · the reason most people come

The colonies

Penguin numbers here stop meaning anything as numbers. A hundred and forty thousand pairs on one volcanic island; a hundred thousand on one cinder cone. What varies between sites is not scale but species — and the western Peninsula and the Weddell side hold different birds.

09

The great rookeries

Gentoos and chinstraps, on the sheltered western side where most voyages spend their days. These are the landings almost every Peninsula itinerary includes, and the ones where you'll spend hours rather than minutes.

10

Adélie country — the Weddell side

Cross to the eastern side, through the Antarctic Sound, and the species changes. Adélies are the true ice-obligate penguin: sparse on the western Peninsula, overwhelming here. Reaching these sites needs an itinerary that goes east, and ice that lets you.

Marine mammals

Whales, seals & the shore

Most Peninsula landings are on offshore islands. A handful put you on the continent itself — and a handful of bays concentrate krill so densely that the humpbacks come to you. In February and March these are the best hours of the voyage.

11

The whale bays & continental landings

Sheltered water, calving glacier fronts, and the krill that draws everything else. Engines go off when a whale approaches within 100 m and the animal dictates the encounter from there — which is why the best of these hours happen at a drift, in silence.

12

Out on the open water

The encounters you cannot schedule: seals asleep on drifting floes, orca surfacing beside the hull, and two days of albatross on each Drake crossing. All of it from the deck or a Zodiac, all of it unannounced.

The human record

What people left behind

Antarctica preserves everything. Whale-oil tanks rust but never rot; a hut abandoned in 1912 still has tinned food on the shelf. These are the continent's most photogenic subjects that aren't ice or animals — industrial ruin and heroic-age domesticity in a landscape that erases nothing.

The rarest thing in this guide

The emperors

Emperor penguins are the only bird that breeds through the Antarctic winter, incubating a single egg on their feet in total darkness at −40 °C. By the time a ship can reach them in November, the chicks are grey bundles a few months old and the adults are still commuting across fast ice to feed them. Then the ice breaks up, the colony disperses to sea, and it is over for another year.

That is the whole problem. There is no emperor colony you can visit in January. The window is roughly late October to early December, it depends on sea ice you cannot forecast, and the two ways in are wildly different propositions.

Swoop Antarctica, citing IAATO's visitor mapping, reckons that in the five years to 2023 fewer than 600 people visited the Snow Hill colony. Oceanwide, which has run these voyages longer than anyone, reports a success rate under 50%. As photographer Jyoti Baid described her November crossing: "ice cleared just enough for our ship to navigate deep into the Weddell Sea… One week later, shifting ice sealed the route entirely." The year before hers, no one landed at all.

What happens when it works is worth the odds. Two twin-engine helicopters — always flown in pairs — lift you in groups of seven over fractured pressure ridges and set you down roughly a mile from the colony, the IAATO buffer. You then walk 45 minutes across flat fast ice along a flagged route, and the colony arrives without warning in a landscape that is otherwise entirely silent and entirely white. First-hand accounts describe it as among the most emotionally overwhelming moments of their lives. The birds, having no land predators, are indifferent to you: individuals and small groups walk right up.

Two routes, one window

By ship + helicopter — Snow Hill Island
13–17 days from Ushuaia. Around 4,000 breeding pairs (recent counts suggest up to 8,000) on fast ice off the island. Requires Ultramarine (Quark, PC3, two helicopters) or Ortelius (Oceanwide, two helicopters), or Le Commandant Charcot for guaranteed deep-Weddell access. $11,000–29,000+ pp. November, essentially.

By ski-plane — Gould Bay, Weddell coast
9 days from Punta Arenas via Union Glacier. The most southerly emperor colony on the continent and the only one with a field camp beside it — you sleep on the sea ice about 3 km away and walk in. Two departures a year, mid-November, run by Antarctic Logistics & Expeditions. From $64,595 pp. Sells out first of anything they offer.

Photographer's note

Joshua Holko, who has led five Gould Bay expeditions, shoots the colony at night — the sun never sets, so he works the hours when it sits lowest and rakes warm light across the ice. "We spent hour after hour photographing the penguins during the midnight sun… I completely lost track of time." A 400 mm f/2.8 is the workhorse; 16–35 mm for colony context; a step ladder for elevation; sleds to haul it the 45 minutes in. In 15–20 knot wind the birds get plastered with blown snow, which he rates as the most emotive conditions of all.

The third wayOn a Ross Sea voyage, emperors are encountered rather than visited — juveniles dispersing from the Cape Crozier and Cape Washington colonies, standing on floes at the ice edge or porpoising past the bow. Aurora's ornithologist Tracey-Ann Hooley: "Encountering the emperors out here is a big deal, especially because we aren't seeking out their colonies and so have no way of knowing exactly where they're going to be on any given day." Add 6–10 days · the single biggest upgrade

South Georgia

A 170 km crescent of glaciated mountains at 54°S with no airstrip, no accommodation ashore and no way to arrive except by expedition ship. It holds roughly 5% of the world's king penguins, 95% of its Antarctic fur seals, about half the planet's southern elephant seals, and the largest visitable wandering albatross population anywhere. No safari, no wildlife cruise, nothing else on Earth delivers biomass at this density.

The scale doesn't translate to description. At St Andrews Bay an outwash plain backed by four snowy peaks holds an estimated 150,000 breeding pairs plus another hundred thousand moulting and resting birds — a constant metallic braying of adults layered over the high whistling of woolly brown chicks. The guano reaches you before the beach does.

Crucially for photographers, South Georgia's wildlife is almost entirely land-based, unlike the Peninsula where much of the action is in the water. That means extraordinarily close access to subjects, with mountains rising directly from the sea behind them. Bring the wide angle; the telephoto will spend less time on the camera than you expect.

Getting there: two sea days from the Falklands, or 1.5 from the Peninsula's tip, on a 17–23 day combined voyage. Most itineraries allow 4–5 days and 6–10 landings. A South Georgia–focused voyage without the Peninsula runs 15–19 days. Attempting it shorter isn't possible — the transit time is fixed by geography.

Before you go

  • Entry permit — required for every visitor since 1 August 2025 under the GSGSSI Entry Control Ordinance. Your operator obtains it during booking; confirm they have.
  • Biosecurity — vacuum all clothing, footwear, dry bags and camera gear before departure and again on board before every landing. Do not put a bag on the ground at Grytviken.
  • Grytviken is compulsory — it's where customs and biosecurity checks happen, so every ship calls there.
  • 42 authorised landing sites exist; your expedition leader picks daily on conditions. No landing is guaranteed, and St Andrews Bay — steeply shelving and open to the sea — is the hardest of the big colonies to land at. Many voyages see it only from a Zodiac.
  • No drones, no helicopters. A Regulated Activity Permit is required and is not granted to recreational visitors.
13

The colonies & the cliffs

Five landings define the island. Two are king penguin mega-colonies of a scale that resets your sense of what a lot of animals means; one is the loveliest single bay in the sub-Antarctic; one is the only place you can stand near a nesting wandering albatross; and one is for seabirds in flight rather than penguins en masse.

14

Shackleton's island

South Georgia is also where the greatest survival story in exploration ends — and where its protagonist is buried. The whaling stations that processed 54,000 whales at Grytviken alone are now slowly being reclaimed by the fur seals whose ancestors they nearly exterminated.

The whisky traditionVirtually every expedition gathers at Shackleton's grave, faces south toward Antarctica as he was buried to do, and pours a tot — "toasting the Boss." Note that the headstone required restoration after over-saturation by decades of offerings, so pour thoughtfully.

Optional extensions

The three other Antarcticas

These don't fit inside a Peninsula voyage — each requires choosing a longer or entirely different itinerary from the outset. All three are worth knowing about before you book, because two of them are cheaper than you'd guess and one is the most expensive travel on Earth.

Usually bundled with South Georgia

The Falkland Islands (Islas Malvinas)

2 sea days from Ushuaia · adds 2–4 days · or fly in from Santiago for a 10–14 day land-based trip

The counterpoint to the Peninsula's monochrome: green sheep-country hills, painted corrugated-iron cottages, and wildlife that feels intimate rather than extreme. Five penguin species breed across the archipelago, and the islands hold roughly 75% of the world's black-browed albatross — about half a million breeding pairs. Wind is the defining condition: persistent westerlies of 25–40 kph are normal and gusts past 80 are common, but midsummer days run from before 4 am to nearly 10 pm, which is an enormous shooting window.

The critical distinction for photographers is how you visit. A cruise landing gives you a few hours ashore at one to three sites in the middle of the day. Flying in on FIGAS's eight-seat Islanders and staying two to five nights on the outer islands — Sea Lion, Carcass, Saunders, Bleaker — buys you dawn and dusk, weather windows, and unhurried time. It is the single highest-yield upgrade available in the Falklands, and it works either side of an Antarctic voyage: the ship picks you up at Stanley's floating dock.

  1. 1

    Volunteer Point

    The largest king penguin colony outside South Georgia — 1,000–2,000 pairs on a sweeping white beach with gentoos and Magellanics mixed in. Lie on the sand for the eye-level wide-angle shot. Kings breed on a 14-month cycle, so every stage is present whenever you arrive.

    4×4 from Stanley · 2.5–4 hrs each way · £100–160 · full day · book far ahead
  2. 2

    The Neck, Saunders Island

    The consensus finest landing in the Falklands: four penguin species within one short walk — gentoo and Magellanic on the sand spit, king at its base, rockhopper on the clifftop nesting alongside black-browed albatross and imperial shags. A freshwater seep on the north cliffs makes a "shower" where rockhoppers queue to preen.

    Zodiac landing or FIGAS · half day minimum · Nov–Jan for albatross chicks
  3. 3

    Devil's Nose, West Point Island

    Around 2,000 pairs of black-browed albatross on the highest sea cliffs in the islands, nesting on mud pedestals with rockhoppers packed in between them — close enough for wide-angle work as birds land and launch. A rolling 45-minute walk from the jetty, or a Land Rover if the weather's foul.

    Zodiac landing, or by boat from Carcass · half day · Nov–Feb
  4. 4

    Steeple Jason Island

    The largest black-browed albatross colony on Earth — estimates run from 140,000 to 180,000 breeding pairs stretching for miles along the coast. With no high ground to launch from, the birds build "runways" and take a run-up. Uninhabited, managed by the Wildlife Conservation Society, and gloriously hard to reach.

    Zodiac only · approved guide required · allow 3–5 days on Carcass for weather
  5. 5

    Sea Lion Island

    Routinely called the best wildlife destination in the Falklands, and one of very few places on Earth where you can watch orca hunt from dry land. Over 2,000 elephant seals breed here: bulls fight in early October, pups arrive mid-month, and by mid-November the weaned pups are silver and absurdly curious. Resident orca pods work the shallows as those pups first enter the water.

    FIGAS 20 min from Stanley · Sea Lion Lodge, under 20 beds · 2–3 nights · orca peak mid-Nov–mid-Dec
  6. 6

    Carcass Island

    Completely rodent-free, and therefore the best small-bird photography in the archipelago: Cobb's wren (a Falklands endemic), tussac birds, pipits and thrushes, all remarkably tame — as one photographer put it, you can leave the 600 mm at home. Striated caracaras, the "Johnny Rook," hover at head height and will steal anything unguarded.

    FIGAS ~1 hr or Zodiac · 2–3 nights · gateway to West Point and Steeple Jason
  7. 7

    Bleaker Island

    One of the Falklands' largest imperial cormorant (blue-eyed shag) colonies — historically 8,000+ pairs — half a kilometre inland, a four-minute walk from the accommodation, which means easy dawn and evening light. Add a mile-long white-sand beach at Sandy Bay and small rockhopper colonies sheltered along the tussac coast.

    FIGAS 35 min · day trip or overnight · chicks Nov–Jan
  8. 8

    New Island

    The most remote and most dramatic: Cathedral Cliffs and the Settlement Rookery hold albatross, rockhoppers and shags on vertiginous ledges, and golden hour on those cliffs is the best light in the islands. On the north beach a resident male sea lion hunts gentoo penguins — brutal, and unique.

    Zodiac only · hardest outer island to reach · basic hut at North End Beach
  9. 9

    Stanley & the Lady Elizabeth

    A genuine small town of 2,500 with red telephone boxes, painted cottages along Ross Road, a whale-jawbone arch outside Christ Church Cathedral, and the excellent Historic Dockyard Museum — free to expedition passengers. The 1879 barque Lady Elizabeth has been listing in Whalebone Cove since 1936 and is the archipelago's photographic icon at golden hour.

    Walkable from the tender pontoons · half day · museum 1–1.5 hrs

If you only have a cruise dayVolunteer Point books out and the track is rough. Bluff Cove Lagoon — about 1,000 gentoos and 20-plus pairs of kings, half an hour's drive then a 4×4 transfer, with the Sea Cabbage Café on site — is the standard easier alternative, and you pass the Falklands' strange quartzite "stone runs" on the way. Gypsy Cove, four miles from Stanley, has burrow-nesting Magellanics and works best after 1–2 pm when the birds return from fishing. Note that avian influenza has been confirmed in the islands since 2023, with a positive result at Sparrow Cove in November 2025 — check current site closures with your operator before sailing.

A different continent entirely

The Ross Sea & East Antarctica

25–33 days · from Bluff, New Zealand or Hobart · late Dec–Feb only · $20,000–45,000 pp

Antarctica's far side, and a fundamentally different proposition: four to five days of open Southern Ocean each way, no fly-in option, and a total of under a thousand tourists a year. You will rarely see another vessel. There is no scramble for landings and no timetabling around competing ships.

What's here is the continent's densest concentration of heroic-age history — four original expedition huts, standing and stocked — plus the largest ice shelf on Earth, the world's most southerly active volcano, the largest ice-free area in Antarctica, and killer whale ecotypes found nowhere else. The window is tight: winter ice covers up to 85% of the Ross Sea and only clears enough from roughly late December through February. Early January puts you in the Ross Sea for the peak of Adélie chick-rearing and active orca; February opens McMurdo Sound more reliably and lets ships reach the Bay of Whales.

  1. 1

    Cape Adare & Ridley Beach

    The world's largest Adélie colony — around 280,000 pairs, over a million birds at peak — spread across a flat spit, and behind it the oldest building on the continent: Borchgrevink's two huts, put up in February 1899 by the first men ever to overwinter on the Antarctic mainland. An Antarctic Specially Protected Area capped at 2,000 visitors a year; most seasons see far fewer.

    Zodiac cruise or wet landing · half day · ice can cancel the landing outright
  2. 2

    Cape Evans — Scott's Terra Nova hut

    The largest historic building in Antarctica and the emotional centre of any Ross Sea voyage: the hut Scott left for the Pole and never returned to. Inside, preserved by the cold and the New Zealand Antarctic Heritage Trust, almost everything remains as it was in 1911 — tins in the cupboards, reindeer-skin bags on the bunks, chemistry glass on the bench, seal blubber still stacked and dripping oil after a century.

    Zodiac or fast-ice landing · 2–3 hrs · ASPA permit, guided only
  3. 3

    Cape Royds — Shackleton's Nimrod hut

    A 7 × 8.5 m prefabricated hut holding over 6,100 original artefacts, beside the world's southernmost Adélie colony and Pony Lake, with Erebus smoking behind. Visitors consistently describe a warmer feel than Scott's. Climb to the vantage point above the hut for the look-down colony overview.

    Sea-ice landing (~400 m walk) or Zodiac · 2–3 hrs
  4. 4

    The Ross Ice Shelf

    The largest body of floating ice on the planet — over 530,000 km², roughly the size of France — presenting a wall 15–50 m high for some 950 km across the southern Ross Sea. This was the "Great Ice Barrier" that stopped the heroic age from going further south. Late-season ships can push into the narrow inlets near Cape Crozier, where an emperor colony breeds, and on to the Bay of Whales over Amundsen's Framheim camp.

    Full day cruising the shelf front · shoot at midnight sun for gold on the ice cliffs
  5. 5

    Mt Erebus & McMurdo Sound

    The world's most southerly active volcano, 3,794 m, with one of only a handful of permanently active lava lakes on Earth and a steam plume visible from the ship. Photographers rate the sound's late-night light — low, lateral, turning volcanic peaks gold, pink and lavender above pancake ice — as the best of a multi-week voyage.

    Ship deck · around midnight under 24-hour daylight
  6. 6

    Drygalski Ice Tongue & Terra Nova Bay

    A 70 km glacier that flows straight out to sea, its ice cliffs and embayments worked by evening Zodiac cruise — turquoise calved bergs carrying Adélies, fog banks rolling off the tongue, and fogbows. Mount Melbourne, 2,733 m, sits behind it. Nearby Inexpressible Island regularly sees 60-knot katabatic winds, so timing is everything.

    Evening Zodiac cruise · shore walk near the German Gondwana base
  7. 7

    The McMurdo Dry Valleys

    Antarctica's polar desert and the largest ice-free area on the continent — roughly 6,000 km² of frozen lakes, ephemeral streams and bare bedrock that has seen no rainfall in at least two million years. It looks more like Mars than Antarctica. Helicopter access only, restricted to a designated area in Taylor Valley near the Canada Glacier, and far from guaranteed.

    Helicopter from ship · 2–4 hrs · weather and equipment dependent
  8. 8

    The Balleny Islands

    A volcanic archipelago straddling the Antarctic Circle, so hard to reach it has seen fewer than 30 landings ever. You almost certainly won't land — but the sculptural icebergs here are extraordinary, including arches worn clean through their bodies, and one photographer found the northbound return two weeks later gave far better light than the crossing south.

    Ship deck only · gale-force wind common · Type-C orca sightings
  9. 9

    Macquarie Island

    The sub-Antarctic stepping stone, and worth the voyage on its own: all three million of the world's royal penguins breed here and nowhere else. Sandy Bay mixes royals, kings and elephant seals in one chaotic scene; an evening Zodiac cruise off Lusitania Bay passes a quarter of a million king penguins. Pest-free since 2014, with dramatic wildlife recovery since.

    1.5–2 days · Tasmania Parks permit via operator · Jan–Feb peak
  10. 10

    Campbell Island

    New Zealand's southernmost territory and the premier albatross site in the Southern Ocean: over 6,000 pairs of southern royal albatross — the same size as wandering albatross, with the largest wingspan of any bird — nesting on hilltop tussock within metres of the path. Their "gamming" courtship display, all bill-clapping and spread wings, makes them superb subjects.

    Full day, Perseverance Harbour · 1 hr walk uphill each way

Who runs itHeritage Expeditions (Heritage Adventurer, two departures a season, 28 days ex-Bluff, from ~$33,075) is the original and most experienced, operating since 1993. Aurora Expeditions runs the Ross Sea Odyssey and the 33-day Epic Antarctica semi-circumnavigation from Ushuaia to Christchurch — which also passes Peter I Island, stepped on by fewer than 1,000 people ever. Ponant, Hapag-Lloyd, Silversea and Oceanwide run variants. The far rarer extension is Commonwealth Bay in East Antarctica, where Mawson's 1911–14 huts stand in the windiest sea-level place on Earth — one operator scheduled a Christmas Day visit following the region's first ice-free summer in eight years, which tells you how reliable it isn't.

The most expensive travel on Earth

The interior & the South Pole

6–12 days · fly from Punta Arenas · Nov–mid-Jan · $65,000–100,000+ pp

You arrive not by ship but by wheeled Boeing 757, landing on a natural blue-ice runway at Union Glacier, 79°S in the Heritage Range of the Ellsworth Mountains — one of the most surreal moments in travel. From there everything is reached by ski-equipped Twin Otter or Basler. The operator landscape is essentially one company: Antarctic Logistics & Expeditions, running the only full-service private camp on the continent, the only private camp at the Pole, and the only field camp beside an emperor colony.

Expect −10 °C to −25 °C with wind chill at Union Glacier and around −28 °C at the Pole, which sits at 2,835 m. Weather holds delay flights by days, so ALE asks you to arrive in Punta Arenas three nights early. At the Geographic South Pole there are two markers: the striped, flag-ringed Ceremonial Pole for photographs, and a plain metal stake at the true 90°S — re-surveyed and moved every 1 January because the ice sheet drifts about ten metres a year. Amundsen-Scott Station looms behind, and ALE's camp is the only private one there, which buys overnight guests the peculiar silence that day-trippers never get.

Between flights, Union Glacier itself rewards exploration — moraine hikes, the translucent turquoise of the runway ice, and wind-scoured dry valleys like Elephant's Head with fossils in the rock. Add-ons run from tandem skydiving over the Ellsworths to Mount Vinson, the continent's 4,892 m high point and a Seven Summit, 30 minutes away by ski-plane; non-climbers can fly to Branscomb Glacier base camp purely to photograph it. Three Glaciers Retreat, a handful of private suites with Toyostove heaters and private patios at the foot of Mount Sporli, is the most exclusive accommodation on the planet.

Fly-in versus ship, plainlyA ship reaches the Peninsula and the sub-Antarctic islands: six-plus penguin species, whales, seals, seabirds, and vastly more biodiversity per dollar. It does not reach the interior, the Pole, or Gould Bay. A fly-in reaches places no ship can and almost no wildlife beyond the emperors. They are complements, not substitutes — plenty of serious Antarctic travellers do both, in different seasons. If the Pole or the Gould Bay colony is the goal, this is the only path; otherwise a ship wins on every other measure.

Before & after · build in the buffer days

The two gateways

Over 90% of Peninsula voyages sail from Ushuaia; every fly-the-Drake and every interior flight leaves from Punta Arenas. Both cities are routinely treated as transit and both deserve better — they have their own sub-Antarctic wildlife, and one of them serves the best crab you will ever eat. Arrive at least two days early regardless: wind, fog and the occasional strike delay flights into both, and no ship waits.

15

Ushuaia — the end of the world, Argentina

The world's southernmost city sits at 54°S in an amphitheatre of Andean peaks, facing the Beagle Channel that Darwin sailed in 1833. October and November run 2–12 °C with snowmelt mud on the trails and thin crowds; December to February climbs to 10–15 °C with the penguin colony at Isla Martillo at full strength and tours selling out weeks ahead; March brings autumn colour to the lenga beech, king crab at its best, and empty paths. Three days covers the essentials, four lets you add a dedicated birding or photography morning.

16

Ashore — trails, forest and the prison

Inland, the island is sub-Antarctic beech forest, peat bog and glacial lake — and a convict history that built the town. In March the lenga turns copper and the trails empty out, which is the best combination of the year.

17

Eating in Ushuaia

The cuisine here is comida fueguina, and it diverges sharply from the beef-and-Malbec of Buenos Aires. The undisputed star is centolla — southern king crab, hauled from the Beagle Channel by artisanal fishers, kept live in tanks in restaurant windows, and priced by the kilo. Order one whole crab between two and treat it as the meal that defines the trip. The classic preparations are al natural (steamed, served cool with lemon, nothing masking the sweetness) and a la parmesana (warm under a gratinéed parmesan crust).

The dish nearly everyone overlooks is merluza negra, Patagonian toothfish — dense, buttery, and repeatedly described by visitors as among the best fish they've eaten. Multiple sources flag skipping it for the crab as a mistake. Then there's cordero fueguino, lamb slow-roasted over wood, and an Italian-immigrant pasta tradition that produces crab-stuffed ravioli. Ushuaia also has a chocolate culture that outclasses Buenos Aires and rivals Bariloche.

Punta Arenas — the fly-the-Drake hub, Chile

A working port of 130,000 on the Strait of Magellan, built by sheep money and shipping, where wool-baron mansions face the water Magellan's men re-provisioned on. If you're flying the Drake or flying to the interior, everything begins here: briefings, gear checks, boot fitting, and a charter that may or may not leave on schedule. Budget two nights before and one after at minimum; three to four unlocks all three of the region's wildlife excursions — and unlike Ushuaia, this city has king penguins and humpback whales within a day's reach.

19

The city itself

Wool money built this in about thirty years, and it shows: a plaza that looks transplanted from Europe, a cemetery ranked among the most beautiful in the world, and a waterfront where seabirds have colonised the ruins of the old pier.

20

Eating in Punta Arenas

The signature ingredients here are not subtle. Centolla again — the same southern king crab, from the Strait's cold seabed — but the local classic is chupe de centolla: the meat baked in a clay plato de greda with cream and parmesan until gratinated, on nearly every traditional menu in the city. One caveat worth knowing: the legal extraction season runs mid-July to mid-December, with a closed veda from March to June, so crab eaten in the Antarctic high season is often stored or frozen rather than fresh-caught.

The other pillar is cordero magallánico — lamb raised on windswept steppe grass, which gives the meat a mineral, herbaceous flavour found almost nowhere else. Locals will tell you the wind itself seasons it. Its ceremonial form is cordero al palo: a whole lamb butterflied onto a vertical spit and slow-roasted for hours over coals, seasoned with nothing but sea salt and herbs. And because this is the last real city before Antarctica, the restaurants have absorbed a layer of departure ritual — the operator welcome dinner, and a stop at the Shackleton Bar.

Which cabin, and does it matter

Mostly you're outside, so a lower-deck twin with a porthole is genuinely adequate — and it rolls less on the Drake, being closer to the ship's centre of gravity. But for a photographer a balcony is worth the premium for one specific reason: at 3 am, when the light goes extraordinary, you can step out in a base layer instead of dressing and navigating to a public deck. Lindblad's opening floor-to-ceiling windows are the ideal — every cabin becomes a shooting platform. Solo travellers should look at Aurora's Douglas Mawson, which has dedicated single cabins and so avoids the 50–100% supplement charged elsewhere.

Add-ons to pre-book

  • Sea kayaking — $200–600. The activity returning travellers talk about for decades: water level among brash ice, penguins porpoising under the hull, ancient ice fizzing beside you. Book with the voyage; spaces fill before the ship sells out.
  • Camping on the ice — bivvy bag on snow, no tent, under a sun that never quite sets. Limited places.
  • Snowshoeing & mountaineering — best early season when snow cover is deepest; guides teach crampons and axe from scratch.
  • Helicopter flightseeing — Ultramarine, Ortelius, Charcot. Priced separately.
  • The polar plunge — free, about ten seconds, −1.8 °C, and permanent bragging rights.

Gateway nights

Two nights before you sail, one or two after. Pre-voyage, favour walking distance to the port — embarkation morning is not the time to be hunting a taxi in the dark. Post-voyage, go the other way entirely: a hillside or waterfront room with a hot tub and a view, where logistics are somebody else's problem.
