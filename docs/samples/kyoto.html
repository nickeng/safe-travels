Ten days · Late November 2026 · For two
紅葉狩り

Chasing the
Kōyō

Autumn colour does not arrive in Japan. It descends — from the two-thousand-metre larches in early October to the sheltered ravines of southern Kyoto in mid-December. Ride it downhill and you can stand inside peak colour four times in ten days.

The word the Japanese use for this is kōyō (紅葉) — and the ritual of going to look at it, momijigari, literally "maple hunting." Hunting is the right verb. Unlike cherry blossom, which detonates across a city in a single week, kōyō is a slow front that starts on the highest ridges in late September and takes ten weeks to reach the lowlands. Elevation matters more than latitude: the maples above Takayama turn a full month before the maples in Osaka, though Osaka is only 200 km south.

So the answer to your question is yes — emphatically yes, and the mechanism is simple. Start high and inland, finish low and coastal. A route that runs the Hida highlands → Kanazawa → Kyoto → Nara → Osaka moves downhill and southward at almost exactly the speed the colour front does. What you cannot do is catch every band: the front travels faster than a ten-day trip, and the famous alpine names — Kamikōchi, Oku-Nikkō — have already dropped their leaves by the time Kyoto so much as blushes. The chart below is the whole planning problem in one picture.

The one date you cannot look up yet 2026's actual peak. Japan's foliage forecasts publish in September, and Kyoto City's live Autumn Leaves Calendar — which grades 14 named districts on a five-stage Green → Peak → Past Peak scale — goes live around 31 October. Book the route now on the historical bands; re-check both about two weeks out and use the flex built into the plan below. For reference, in 2025 japan-guide's reporters had Kyoto only "approaching peak" on 21 November and at full "peak colour" on 29 November.

What late November actually feels like 晩秋

Crisp, dry and startlingly clear — the humidity that hazes Japanese summers is gone, which is exactly why the maples photograph so well against blue. But the country is at different temperatures at once, and you will move through all of them.

Know before you go

Book now, not soon. Mid-November to early December is the single busiest domestic travel window of the Japanese year. Chris Rowthorn is blunt about it: "Japanese tourists are just as keen about foliage as they are about cherries." Classic Kyoto ryokan (Tawaraya, Hiiragiya, Seikorō) go six-plus months out; Okuhida's private-onsen ryokan the same.

Some gardens now need a reservation just to walk in. Enkō-ji required advance online booking for any visit between 10 Nov and 10 Dec in 2025 — not only the light-up. Check each temple's own page, not an aggregator.

Reserve the buses. The Kanazawa–Shirakawa-gō–Takayama highway bus opens bookings exactly one month ahead and popular autumn dates sell out within minutes. Book both legs at once.

Carry cash. Old-guard kaiseki houses, shinise sweet shops and mountain temples still expect it.

Dates worth building the trip around

Three confirmed nights,
and one crowded weekend

灯り

Most autumn "events" in Japan are really just the season itself. But three light-ups on this route have published 2026 dates — rare, and worth anchoring your calendar to. One of them is free, and it falls on exactly the nights you'd be in town.

Kenroku-en & Kanazawa Castle: the Four Seasons Story 四季物語

This is the single best-timed thing on the whole itinerary, and it costs nothing. On seven nights only, Kanazawa opens Kenroku-en and the castle park after dark and lights the maples — together with the yukitsuri, the conical rope rigs the garden's gardeners begin hanging in early November to hold the pine branches against coming snow. Up to 800 ropes per tree, floodlit from below, standing over foliage that is still crimson. It is a layering of two seasons in one frame that Kyoto simply cannot offer, because Kyoto does not get the snow.

18:00–20:45 (park closes 21:00) · free admission, no reservation · no parking — take the Kanazawa Loop Bus to "Kenrokuen Garden・Kanazawa Castle Park." Eat in Higashi Chaya or Ōmichō first; nothing starts until six.

Why the recommended dates are what they are Two illumination nights — Saturday 21 and Sunday 22 November — fall inside the Kanazawa leg below. That is not a coincidence; the route was built around them.

Midōsuji Illumination, Osaka 御堂筋イルミネーション

Osaka's 4 km central boulevard is lined with more than 800 ginkgo trees that turn hard gold in late November — and the city lights the whole avenue nightly. 3 November – 31 December 2026, roughly 17:00 to 01:00, free. Walk it in late afternoon while the sun is still catching the leaves, then again after dark. It is the last colour of the trip and it is a street, not a garden — which is a good final note.

Kōko-en, Himeji 好古園もみじ会

If you take the Himeji add-on: the garden's ~200 maples are lit from sunset with the ponds doubling them, open to 20:00 (last entry 19:30) against a normal 17:00 close. 15 November – 1 December in the most recent confirmed edition. The Kassui-ken restaurant serves a ¥3,850 momiji gozen autumn set — 20 servings a day, book three days ahead.

The crowd trap: 21–23 November Labour Thanksgiving Day (勤労感謝の日) falls on Monday 23 November 2026, making Sat–Mon a three-day national weekend landing squarely in peak kōyō. How to build a kōyō day 紅葉の一日

Short days are the season's gift, not its cost. Sunset at 16:50 means the illuminations open while you're still out — so one day can hold a near-empty dawn garden, a long lunch, and a lit temple, without ever rushing.

Shifting the window 前後にずらす

These ten days are a compromise, and it's worth knowing which way to bend it.

If Kyoto at absolute peak is non-negotiable, slide everything two to three days later (23 Nov – 2 Dec). Kyoto lands dead centre, Nara and Osaka both improve, and the Kenroku-en illumination on Sat 28 / Sun 29 still catches you. The cost: Takayama will be past colour and into bare-branch-and-first-snow territory. Many people consider that a fair trade — Shirakawa-gō under early snow is its own reward.

If the mountains matter more, go a week earlier (13–22 Nov). Takayama and Shirakawa-gō hit properly, Kōyasan comes into play as a real stop rather than an add-on, Kanazawa is bang on — but Kyoto will only be "colour appearing," and you would want to lean hard on Takao, Ōhara and Kurama, which turn first.

What doesn't work at all is trying to add Nikkō or Kamikōchi. Nikkō's whole season — even its low shrine district — is finished by around the second week of November, and Kamikōchi physically closes on 15 November every year, buses and all. See the add-ons at the end for the version of this trip that does catch them.

Rail & tickets

This route crosses three rail companies and one bus network, so a nationwide JR Pass is poor value — the Takayama↔Shirakawa-gō↔Kanazawa legs are Nōhi/Hokutetsu buses and not JR at all, and the Takayama–Hirayu leg must be ticketed in person rather than online.

Buy point-to-point, and reserve seats for the Thunderbird/Shinkansen leg and the Ltd. Exp. Hida into Takayama. Both run near capacity in kōyō season.

Leg one · 2 nights · Fri 20 – Sat 21 Nov

Takayama &
Shirakawa-gō.

飛騨高山・白川郷

You begin at 573 metres in a basin ringed by the Japanese Alps, in a merchant town so intact it is nicknamed Little Kyoto — and you begin here because this is the stop with the tightest deadline. Hida's valley floor peaks roughly 8–20 November. Arrive on the 20th and you are catching the last week of it: colour still holding on the Higashiyama temple trail and in Shirakawa-gō's valley, the highest ridges already bare and dusted, the pickle vats freshly packed, and the year's first shiboritate sake just pressed.

What isolation did to this place is the whole story. Cut off by mountains, the Hida merchants grew rich on the timber trade to the shogunate and spent it on two things you can still taste: a beef many people rate above Kobe, and an entire pantry of fermented, salted and dried food built to carry a farmhouse through months of snow. Hōba miso reportedly began as a salvage operation — pickles frozen solid in deep winter, laid on a dried magnolia leaf with miso on top and set over the fire.

Two closures to know before you plan a day here Kamikōchi shuts for the season on 15–16 November, every year, regardless of weather — the last bus out leaves at 17:55 on the 15th and it does not reopen until April. The Norikura Skyline closes 31 October. Both are the alpine names travel articles attach to Hida autumn, and both are simply unavailable on these dates. Shinhotaka Ropeway runs year-round and becomes a snow-and-panorama trip instead of a foliage one.

The town, on foot 高山の町

Everything here is walkable and everything closes early — most shops by 16:30, most kitchens taking last orders by 20:00. The town belongs to you before 8am and after 5pm, and to tour buses in between, so plan in those bookends.

Where the colour actually is 飛騨の紅葉

The old town is dark wood and grey tile; the colour sits just above and just outside it. These are the five places to spend your leaf-hunting hours — in descending order of how likely they are to still be holding on the 20th of November.

Shirakawa-gō, a day up the valley 白川郷

Fifty minutes by Nōhi bus, and the natural way to leave — the same highway bus continues to Kanazawa, so you can do the village with luggage in a locker and arrive at your next stop by evening. Roughly a hundred gasshō-zukuri farmhouses, their 45–60° thatched roofs engineered to shed snow load, some over 300 years old and still lived in. Go early: most visitors only get 2.5–3.5 hours between buses, and the tour coaches land mid-morning.

The shot everyone comes for The Ogimachi Castle Ruin Observation Deck — a 20-minute uphill walk from near Wada House, or a ¥300 shuttle — gives the full valley composition. Photographers note a narrow tree-lined side path about 25 m past the main viewpoint entrance that frames it even better. The walking trail closes after heavy snow; in late November you should still be fine, but the shuttle is the fallback. And note: Shirakawa-gō's famous winter illumination is January only (4 nights, reservation-only, booking opens in September) — it is not available on this trip.

Eating in the mountains 飛騨の味

Late November is close to the ideal window: nabe season has just opened, the breweries are releasing their first fresh-pressed sake, and the cold makes hōba miso taste like the reason it was invented.

  • Hida beef 飛騨牛

    Japanese Black cattle raised 14+ months in Gifu — fine marbling, clean sweet fat. Eat it as a ¥350 croquette on the street or as a kaiseki course.

  • Hōba miso 朴葉味噌

    Miso, spring onion, shiitake and often minced beef grilled on a dried magnolia leaf over a tabletop charcoal stove, then stirred into rice.

    Best in the cold
  • Takayama chūka soba 高山ラーメン

    Thin curly noodles in a light soy broth — the base is boiled together with the stock rather than added after, which makes it cleaner than most shoyu ramen.

  • Mitarashi dango みたらし団子

    Grilled rice dumplings on a skewer. Unlike the rest of Japan, Takayama's glaze is unsweetened soy — smoky and savoury. Said to be 600 years old.

  • Akakabu-zuke 赤かぶ漬け

    Fiercely magenta pickled red turnip from the Hida Beni-kabu, a 1918 mutation whose sugars give it its sweet-sour snap. Buy it loose at the morning market.

  • Shiboritate sake しぼりたて

    Seven breweries over 100 years old sit in the old town. From November they release the year's first fresh-pressed batch — look for a bright green sugidama cedar ball newly hung above the door.

    November onward

Where to sleep 泊まる

The strongest play for two nights is to split them: one in the old town, so you have the lanes and the 7am markets to yourself, and one up in Okuhida, where five hot-spring hamlets hold more open-air baths than any other single area in Japan. Okuhida ryokan are effectively half-board — there are almost no restaurants outside them — so don't double-book dinner. Buses there are Nōhi, not JR, and the Takayama–Hirayu leg must be ticketed in person.

Leg two · 2 nights · Sun 22 – Mon 23 Nov

Kanazawa,
on the hinge

金沢

Kanazawa faces the Sea of Japan at the foot of the Hokuriku mountains, and it runs on its own clock — colder and earlier than Kyoto, peaking somewhere in the 15–30 November band. Which puts you here at exactly the right moment, on the hinge between autumn and winter, for the one thing no other city on this route can show you.

From early November, Kenroku-en's gardeners begin rigging yukitsuri — great conical cages of rope, up to 800 strands on a single tree, hung to keep the pines from snapping under snow that has not arrived yet. So for about three weeks the garden holds two seasons at once: snow ropes standing over maples that are still red. Kyoto never gets cold enough for this. It is the single most distinctive image of the whole trip, and on seven nights of November it is floodlit and free.

The other reason to be here now has nothing to do with leaves. Locals date the start of winter by sound rather than sight: buri-okoshi, the thunder that rolls in off the sea in mid-November, is said to announce that the yellowtail have fattened. Days later the crab arrive. The snow-crab season opened on 6 November, and Ōmichō Market's stalls are at their most anticipated stretch of the year.

This is also the leg that absorbs the three-day national weekend. Kanazawa on a holiday Saturday is busy; it is not Higashiyama-in-peak-kōyō busy, and the difference is the difference between a trip you enjoy and a trip you queue through.

Two free openings worth setting an alarm for

Kenroku-en and Kanazawa Castle Park both admit visitors free before the ticket booths open (times shift by month; from 06:00 in Nov–Feb for the castle park). It is a genuine local trick and the only way to have the Kotoji-tōrō lantern and Kasumiga-ike pond to yourselves.

Bring an umbrella, not a shell

November brings 170–265 mm of rain and Kanazawa is nationally famous for sudden shigure squalls out of a clear sky. A folding umbrella is standard local kit here, not a tourist affectation. Photographers actually want the rain: wet stone in the chaya districts throws the lantern light back up.

Sunset ≈ 16:50

Which stacks the day neatly: gardens and market in daylight, a museum or teahouse at dusk, illumination from 18:00.

The garden and the castle 兼六園・金沢城

These two adjoin, and they colour on slightly different schedules — Kenroku-en peaks around mid-November, while the castle park and Gyokusen'inmaru hold into early December. If you arrive at the later end of the window, weight your time toward the castle side.

Teahouse quarters and samurai walls 茶屋街・武家屋敷

Kanazawa was the seat of the Kaga Domain — the second-richest fief in Edo Japan after the shogun's own — and the Maeda lords spent that wealth not on armies but on tea ceremony, lacquer, gold leaf and cooking. The city still reads that way: preserved teahouse districts where geiko still work, and earthen samurai walls draped in straw matting for the winter.

And then the other Kanazawa 現代の金沢

The city is not a museum, and the counterpoint is genuinely good — a circular glass museum with no front door, a shrine gate with Dutch stained glass, a wooden drum gate outside the Shinkansen station, and a concrete box around a still pool. All are ten minutes from the garden.

Kaga cooking, and the week the crab arrive 加賀料理

Aristocratic refinement and unfiltered Sea-of-Japan seafood, both peaking in the same fortnight. That's the case for eating here in late November specifically rather than any other time of year.

  • Kano-gani & kobako-gani 加能ガニ・香箱ガニ

    Ishikawa-branded snow crab: the male, and the smaller roe-packed female prized for her eggs. Locals descend on Ōmichō the day the season opens.

    Opened 6 Nov
  • Kan-buri 寒鰤

    Winter yellowtail, fattened for the cold. Sashimi, shabu-shabu, simmered as buri-daikon, or seared as tataki over rice.

    Season starting
  • Kabura-zushi かぶら寿し

    Not sushi at all but narezushi — salted turnip discs packed with salted yellowtail and fermented in rice malt and amazake into something tangy and almost cheese-like. Appears with the buri-okoshi thunder.

    Winter only
  • Jibuni 治部煮

    The emblem of Kaga cuisine: flour-dredged duck simmered with sudare-fu — a bamboo-mat-textured wheat gluten unique to Kanazawa — in a sweet-savoury dashi thickened by the coating.

  • Kanazawa oden 金沢おでん

    A lighter kombu-and-katsuobushi dashi than the Kantō version, with local touches: wheel-shaped kuruma-fu gluten and shellfish beside the usual daikon and fish cakes.

    Cold weather
  • Kintsuba きんつば

    Pan-fried azuki-paste cakes in a thin batter skin — the city's signature confection, and the reason to sit down with matcha in a garden teahouse while the maples do the work.

The three you book before you book the hotel

Locals will tell you Kanazawa's kaiseki now rivals Kyoto's, and these are the arguments. All three are reservation-only and two are effectively introduction-only — with just two nights here, find out today whether your dates are even possible.

Where to sleep 泊まる

The real decision is atmosphere versus logistics. Higashi Chaya puts you on empty lantern-lit cobbles at night but is furthest from the Shinkansen; Kōrinbō/Katamachi keeps Kenroku-en, Nagamachi and Ōmichō inside a 15-minute walk with the city's only real after-9pm street life; the station is functional but makes the onward Kyoto leg painless. None have a train station except the last — this is a bus-and-walk city.

The reason to come in this exact fortnight

Yoru-momiji.
Kyoto after 17:30

夜もみじ

For a few weeks a year, a dozen of Kyoto's best-known temples stay open after dark and light their maple gardens. This is not a garnish on the daytime trip — it is a second, completely different city, and it is the strongest single argument for timing a visit to these precise dates rather than any other. Sunset at 16:50 means you get both in one day.

The effect is specific and hard to describe until you've stood in it: the gardens are lit from below, so the maple canopy reads as a ceiling of translucent red rather than a tree, and the temple ponds — Eikan-dō's Hōjō Pond, Kōdai-ji's garden pond — go completely black and double everything. Japan-guide's line about Kōdai-ji is the right one: "the sight of the lit up maple leaves, reflecting on the surface of the temple's pond, are particularly breathtaking." One writer described the still water as opening onto a parallel world.

Most of these are pay-on-entry with no reservation — you simply turn up with ¥500–1,000. Two are not: Ruriko-in, whose black lacquer table mirrors the maples so perfectly that the reflection has become the signature image of Kyoto in autumn, may require booking on peak dates; and Enkō-ji went further in 2025, requiring online reservations for any visit, day or night, between 10 November and 10 December.

How to not queue

Arrive right at opening, or — better — in the last 30–45 minutes before closing. The 18:00–20:00 block is the crush; after that the day-trippers have left for dinner and the gardens empty out noticeably.

Kiyomizu-dera and Kōdai-ji are a natural two-temple evening, connected by the lantern-lit stone lanes of Sannenzaka and Ninenzaka. It is also the single most crowded pairing in the city. Doing it on a weekday matters more here than almost anywhere in Japan.

Getting there after dark

Roads and buses through Higashiyama crawl on peak evenings. For Kiyomizu-dera and Kōdai-ji, walking up from Gion-Shijō station is usually faster than the bus once traffic builds. For Eikan-dō and Nanzen-ji use the Tōzai subway (Higashiyama or Keage).

Leg three · 4 nights · Tue 24 – Fri 27 Nov

Kyoto,
in the right order

京都

Here is the thing almost nobody tells you about Kyoto in autumn: the city does not turn all at once. It sits in a basin ringed by hills on three sides, so colour starts on the northern mountain fringe around 25 October and works down and south, reaching the low sheltered gardens of Uji and Fushimi last of all — sometimes holding into mid-December. Which means the same city can show you green leaves in Ōhara and peak crimson in Arashiyama in the same week. Sequence your four days by elevation and you extend your peak from a few days to nearly a fortnight.

The rule is simple and it is the only thing you need to remember here: north and high on your first days, central in the middle, south and low at the end. Get it backwards and you'll find Takao already bare while Tōfuku-ji is still green.

Chris Rowthorn — a Kyoto resident of three decades — puts the city's whole foliage season at "around October 25 to 31 December, with a peak around 10 December," and gives the traveller's escape hatch in one sentence: "If you arrive too early, you can head up to Kurama or Takao… Likewise, if you come too late, you can head to the south, to Uji, or even Nara, to see trees at their peak colour." That sentence is your insurance policy. Whatever 2026's autumn does, some part of the Kyoto basin will be at peak while you are in it.

A four-day order that works

Day 1 (Tue) — arrive midday. Nanzen-ji and the Philosopher's Path in the afternoon, Eikan-dō's illumination in the evening. Both are the same walk.

Day 2 (Wed) — north and high, while it lasts: Takao's three temples, or Ōhara's Sanzen-in, or the Kurama–Kibune walk. Pick one; they compete for the same day.

Day 3 (Thu) — Arashiyama from first light: bridge, bamboo, Tenryū-ji, Jōjakkō-ji. Back east for Kiyomizu-dera and Kōdai-ji after dark.

Day 4 (Fri) — Nara for the day (next section), or hold it for Uji and Tōfuku-ji if the colour is running late.

Tōfuku-ji sits on the Nara line and the Osaka line — catch it on a transfer day rather than spending a slot on it.

First: the mountain fringe 北山・西山

These turn a week to ten days before the city centre, and they are the reason to not spend your first Kyoto day in Gion. All three are half-day trips out of the city and all three are quieter than anything central — Takao because it takes effort to reach, Ōhara because it is a farming village in a mountain bowl, Kurama because you get there on a two-carriage mountain railway that runs through a tunnel of maples.

Higashiyama and the eastern hills 東山

The postcard, and the crush. Everything here rewards being early or being late — Kiyomizu-dera opens at 6am, which is the single best-value piece of information in this guide, and the difference between a 7am visit and a 2pm one is the difference between a temple and a turnstile.

Arashiyama and the west 嵐山・西山

Treat the whole western district as one early-start block rather than several errands: crowds make it genuinely unusable by mid-morning. Be on Togetsukyō Bridge at first light, in the bamboo grove before 8:30, and inside Tenryū-ji's garden when it opens. By eleven you can be finished and eating tofu.

Last: the low, sheltered, latest places 南山城

Save these for your final Kyoto days — and for the scenario where 2026's autumn runs late and central Kyoto hasn't quite arrived. Tōfuku-ji's maple ravine and Uji's riverside are consistently the last colour in the region.

Photography note for Tōfuku-ji The famous view is of and from the Tsūtenkyō Bridge, a 100 m covered walkway over a valley of maples — but photography is banned on the bridge itself, so plan your vantage points from the surrounding paths. Arrive at the 08:30 opening; autumn admission rises to ¥1,000 and it is still crowded at opening.
Eating in Kyoto

A cuisine built
from what it lacked

京料理

Kyoto is the only Japanese city whose food was shaped for a thousand years by having no coast, no meat, and an imperial court obsessed with restraint. Landlocked in a mountain basin, the old capital built its cooking around what a court, a temple network and an aquifer of exceptionally soft water could provide — and turned every one of those constraints into an art form.

The most instructive story is how a city cut off from the sea got its fish. In the Edo period, mackerel caught in Wakasa Bay was salted on the spot and carried on foot over the mountains along a network of routes called the Saba Kaidō — the Mackerel Road — an eighteen-hour walk. By the time it reached Kyoto it had cured to exactly the right salinity. That fish became saba-zushi, and it is still made the same way at a 240-year-old counter in Gion. The same shortage produced nishin soba in 1882, when a soba shop by the kabuki theatre realised that dried herring — another preserved fish walked in from the coast — was perfect in a hot bowl. Both dishes exist only because Kyoto had no fish of its own.

What ties kaiseki, obanzai and temple cooking into one thing is a discipline called shun (旬): catch each ingredient at its precise peak, then do as little to it as possible. The soft, iron-poor groundwater under the basin is the physical reason this works — it draws a clean, pale dashi from kombu, and sets a silkier curd of tofu than anywhere else in Japan. Which is why Kyoto alone built whole restaurant genres around a single humble soy product.

Your dates land on the most theatrical fortnight of the culinary year: the kaiseki calendar has turned fully to matsutake, chestnut and persimmon, plates are garnished with maple-leaf shapes to match the windows, and the Taiza crab season has just opened on the prefecture's own north coast.

Ichigen-san okotowari

"No first-time customers." Kyoto's most exclusive kaiseki houses and ochaya decline bookings from anyone without an introduction from an existing regular — less snobbery than risk management for a six-seat counter where one no-show is ruinous. The workaround is a genuine luxury-hotel concierge, or a platform like Pocket Concierge, Omakase or TableAll. Rowthorn's advice: book weeks or months ahead, and don't cold-call if you don't speak Japanese.

Carry cash. Old kaiseki houses and shinise sweet shops still expect it, whatever the sticker on the door says.

What to eat, in this fortnight 旬のもの

  • Matsutake 松茸

    The wild pine mushroom that cannot be cultivated, and the defining ingredient of autumn kaiseki — grilled, or steamed in a dashi teapot as dobin-mushi.

    Peaking now, tailing off
  • Taiza-gani タイザ蟹

    Snow crab from Kyoto Prefecture's own Tango coast, only five boats licensed — the "phantom crab." Season opens 6 November, so you catch its very start.

    Just opened
  • Yudōfu 湯豆腐

    Blocks of custard-soft tofu warmed tableside in kombu broth, dipped in yuzu ponzu. Tastes almost entirely of good tofu and good water, which is the whole point.

    A cold-weather ritual
  • Yuba 湯葉

    The skin skimmed off heating soy milk, eaten almost like sashimi with wasabi. A by-product of temple tofu-making, elevated into a delicacy.

  • Nishin soba にしんそば

    Hot buckwheat noodles under a fillet of dried herring slow-simmered in sweet soy. Invented in 1882 next door to the kabuki theatre; a winter bowl.

    Winter dish
  • Saba-zushi 鯖寿司

    Vinegar-cured mackerel pressed onto seasoned rice and wrapped in thick kombu — richer and far more assertive than nigiri. Kyoto's signature sushi, born of necessity.

  • Obanzai おばんざい

    Everyday Kyoto home cooking: five or six small dishes of simmered vegetables, tofu and beans. Point at the earthenware bowls on the counter. The best-value way to eat like a local.

  • Momiji wagashi 紅葉の和菓子

    Seasonal confections shaped as maple leaves and chestnuts, designed to offset whisked matcha. Autumn's version of a very old idea.

    Autumn shapes

The tofu temples of Nanzen-ji 南禅寺の豆腐

This is the one food thread genuinely worth planning a day around, and it happens to sit directly beneath the best maples in eastern Kyoto. The temple district around Nanzen-ji has served yudōfu for centuries — Okutan has been at it since 1635 — and the Culinary Backstreets writer Fran Kuzui makes the seasonal case plainly: she and her husband "always make sure to book a meal at one of the city's wonderful selection of tofu-centric eateries" specifically "when visiting Kyoto during November and December's peak leaf-viewing season." Eat here after Nanzen-ji and before Eikan-dō's illumination; it is all one walk.

The high table 懐石

A planning benchmark, since firm prices are hard to pin: reckon ¥10,000–30,000 per person for genuine kyo-kaiseki, with lunch roughly a third to a half of dinner. Reserve the moment your dates are fixed — several of these effectively need a concierge.

Counters, markets and one very famous mochi queue 気軽に

Where you'll actually eat most meals. Nishiki Market — "Kyoto's Kitchen," about 400 years old — is the grazing spine; Demachi Futaba is a takeaway window with a queue of thirty or forty people in the late afternoon and it is worth joining.

Also on the list if you have a spare afternoon: Fushimi, the sake district 15 minutes south of Kyoto Station, where nearly 40 breweries still work the same soft water that makes the dashi and the tofu. The Gekkeikan Ōkura museum's ¥300 entry includes a tasting.

Where to sleep 泊まる

Kyoto is one of the true homes of the ryokan — not the spa-town variety built around a hot spring, but the machiya-rooted inn tied to the city's own aesthetic: tatami rooms facing a private tsubo-niwa garden, a kaiseki dinner keyed to the season, service so quiet it borders on choreography. In November that means maple gardens turning red just past the shoji, which is precisely when they are most booked out.

Most first-timers do one or two nights of ryokan and then move to a hotel — the formality (and the price, since rates are often quoted per person when meals are included) suits a bookend better than a four-night run.

Leg four · one day from your Kyoto base

Nara,
and the deer

奈良

Nara Park sits at about 100 metres on a basin floor 40 km from Kyoto, which means the two cities peak within days of each other — roughly 20 November to 2 December. So you don't have to sequence them; drop Nara wherever it suits, 35–45 minutes each way by JR or Kintetsu, no change of hotel. What you get for the day is a combination nowhere else offers: thirteen-hundred-year-old wooden architecture, pockets of hard crimson maple, and 1,300 free-roaming sacred deer moving between them.

One honest expectation to set, from japan-guide's own first-hand report: "the park's autumn colour is mostly scattered around the grounds in small pockets of deciduous trees, as opposed to being in one, breathtaking wall of colour." Nara is a hunt, not a canopy. You are looking for a stand of momiji framed by a temple roof with a deer asleep under it — and finding it is the pleasure. Get low so the trees sit behind the deer rather than above them.

The exception, and the reason to start your day here, is a pair of walled gardens sharing a fence beside Tōdai-ji's gate. Isui-en uses shakkei to pull Mount Wakakusa and Tōdai-ji's Nandaimon gate into its own composition; next door, Yoshiki-en is free to foreign visitors on production of a passport and its moss garden goes under a blanket of fallen momiji. Two independent first-hand accounts call Yoshiki-en the most colourful thing in Nara. Both are 45–60 minutes and both are missed by most day-trippers.

The dawn move Ukimidō, the hexagonal pavilion that appears to float on Sagi-ike pond, is the park's best single composition — maples, reflection, and deer in one frame. It gets crowded fast on autumn mornings; photographers recommend arriving an hour before sunrise for the west-bank position, with a real chance of mist on the water.

One walkable loop

Kintetsu-Nara → Yoshiki-en & Isui-en → Nandaimon and the Great Buddha → up through the forest to Nigatsu-dō's stilted balcony → Kasuga Taisha's lantern path → Sagi-ike and Ukimidō → Naramachi for the evening. All on foot, roughly 6–7 hours at an unhurried pace.

Timing notes

Nigatsu-dō's balcony faces west over the basin — it is the sunset stop, not a morning one. Nandaimon's old timber glows at the same hour.

Kōfuku-ji's five-storey pagoda was under renovation and hidden from view as of a recent visit — check before planning the Sarusawa-ike reflection shot.

Mount Wakakusa closes seasonally. Confirm the trail is open for your week before counting on the summit view.

The deer

They bow, and they also bite and headbutt once crackers appear. Buy the ¥200 pack knowing what you're starting.

First, the concentrated colour 庭園

Three places where Nara stops being a hunt and simply hands it to you. Do these before the coaches arrive and the rest of the day can be as slow as you like.

Then the park, the halls and the deer 奈良公園

If your dates run early — or late Nara Prefecture's mountains give you a hedge in both directions. Tanzan-jinja in Sakurai — home to Japan's only surviving thirteen-storey wooden pagoda, red lacquer against fiery maple — plus Hase-dera and Muro-ji all peak early-to-mid November, one to two weeks ahead of the city. Yoshino, famous for cherry, is earliest of all: its official foliage tracker had every viewing point already fallen by 12 December, and its illumination and traffic-control season ran 24 Oct – 30 Nov. So if you arrive to find Nara Park still green, go up. If you arrive to find it past, stay down.

The mountain hedges 山の寺

All three are reached on the same rail line through Sakurai, all three peak one to three weeks before Nara Park, and any one of them makes a half-day. Do them early in your stay if you do them at all — by the time the city peaks, these are done.

Leg five · 1 night · Sat 28 Nov → fly out Sun 29

Osaka,
the last colour

大阪

Osaka is lower, warmer and more urban than Kyoto, and it retains its own heat — so its maples and ginkgo peak a few days to about a week after Kyoto's, and hold into December. In 2019 japan-guide's reporters clocked Kyoto at peak on 28 November and Minoh at peak the very next day. Which makes this the perfect closing stop: the trip's insurance policy against an autumn that runs late, and a 40-minute train from the airport.

It is also the sharpest change of register on the route. After nine days of gardens, gravel and quiet, you arrive in the city that spent its money on food while Kyoto spent it on kimonos — the old proverb is Kyōto no kidaore, Ōsaka no kuidaore, roughly "Kyoto dresses itself to ruin, Osaka eats itself to ruin." Osaka Bay once teemed with so much fish it was nicknamed Naniwa, the "fish garden," and by the Edo period the city was the collection point for rice and ingredients from every domain in Japan: Tenka no Daidokoro, the Nation's Kitchen. The result is a place that treats a street cart with the seriousness Kyoto gives a tea ceremony.

Where the leaves are 大阪の紅葉

Two of these are genuinely among the best autumn sites in Japan, not just in Osaka — and both are half an hour from the city centre. Minoh's ginkgo tend to turn slightly before its maples, and Osaka Castle's ginkgo grove goes first of all, so there is a spread here too.

Neon, towers and one very old temple 大阪の街

A practical local tip for the canal: walk Dōtonbori around 17:00–17:30 and you catch both the daylight water and the neon switching on, before the evening crush builds past nine.

Kuidaore 食い倒れ

Early December sits near the peak of the two things Osaka calls the kings of winter flavour — fugu and crab — and the street-food canon is available at any hour. The one dish that ties this stop back to the whole trip, though, is fried on the trail to Minoh Falls.

  • Momiji tempura 紅葉の天ぷら

    Young maple leaves salt-preserved for a full year, then deep-fried in sweet sesame-flecked batter — a crisp sugary frame around a leaf that tastes of almost nothing. Sold on the Minoh Falls path since 1901.

    ¥300–500 a bag
  • Takoyaki たこ焼き

    Dashi batter, diced octopus, tempura scraps and pickled ginger rolled crisp-outside and molten-inside. Invented here in the 1930s by a street vendor named Tomekichi Endō.

  • Okonomiyaki お好み焼き

    "Grilled how you like it" — cabbage-heavy batter on an iron griddle with pork or seafood, sauce, mayonnaise, bonito. Descended from a pre-war penny snack.

  • Kushikatsu 串カツ

    Battered, deep-fried skewers dipped once into a shared vat of sauce — no double-dipping, ever; use the raw cabbage to scoop. Born in Shinsekai around 1929 as fuel for day labourers.

  • Fugu ふぐ

    Pufferfish as paper-thin tessa sashimi, tecchiri hot pot, or grilled milt. Licensed chefs only. The proverb says start when the bitter orange colours — which is now.

    Peak season
  • Hakozushi & battera 箱寿司

    Sushi pressed in a wooden box and sliced into rectangles — older than nigiri, and once Osaka's dominant style. Battera takes its name from the Portuguese for "small boat."

  • Kitsune udon きつねうどん

    Plain udon in clear dashi under a sheet of sweet fried tofu, named for the fox god's fondness for it. Created in 1893 in Semba, when customers started putting the inari pouch straight onto the noodles.

  • Moningu モーニング

    Not a dish but a ritual: order coffee at an old kissaten before a certain hour and a set of thick toast, egg and cabbage salad arrives free. You sit. You linger.

    07:00–10:30

One night, near the leaves or near the train 泊まる

Namba/Shinsaibashi puts Midōsuji's ginkgo tunnel two minutes from your door and Dōtonbori's stalls on the doorstep, with the Nankai Rapi:t running direct to KIX in 38 minutes. Umeda/Kita is calmer and polished, with the JR Haruka to KIX in about 50 minutes and a shorter hop to Osaka Castle. Both are direct to the airport, so pick on vibe.

What the route left out

Four detours,
and one other trip

寄り道

Three of these you can bolt on without breaking the route. The fourth is a different trip entirely — the version of this journey that catches the alpine colour the ten-day plan cannot. All are worth knowing about before you lock your dates.

Kōyasan 高野山

Add 1 night before Kyoto · Nankai line + funicular from Osaka, ~2 hr · Peaks 1–12 November

A monastic town on a forested plateau at 800–900 m, founded in 826 as the headquarters of Shingon Buddhism, and — the catch — its colour arrives two to three weeks before Kyoto's. Japan-guide puts Kōyasan's best viewing at "early–mid November" against "late November–early December" for Kyoto. One travel writer got caught out exactly this way, day-tripping up from Kyoto at Kyoto's peak and finding the mountain already bare. So this is an add-on only if you shift your whole trip earlier, or accept it as an architecture-and-atmosphere stop rather than a foliage one.

It is worth the shift. The approach path to Danjō Garan, the original sacred precinct, is lined solid with maples that — as one first-hand account puts it — "hide visitors' views of the complex until the final moments before arrival." Behind the town lies Okunoin, Japan's largest cemetery: a two-kilometre cedar-lined avenue past more than 200,000 tombstones to the Tōrōdō, the Hall of Lanterns, lit by over ten thousand flames, and beyond it the mausoleum where Kōbō Daishi is believed to rest in eternal meditation rather than to have died. Past the Gobyō bridge, photography, food and drink all stop.

The reason to stay rather than visit is shukubō — temple lodging. About fifty working temples take overnight guests: tatami and futon, a communal bath, shōjin ryōri Buddhist vegetarian dinner and breakfast built on the temple's own sesame tofu, and free participation in the 7am service and the 7:30 goma fire ritual, a Tang-dynasty fire prayer passed down by Kōbō Daishi himself. Rooms are traditionally unheated and November nights approach freezing — pack real layers. Book 6–12 months ahead: autumn is the mountain's most crowded season and late bookings have run past ¥50,000 a night.

Walk up instead of riding The Chōishi Michi is the historic pilgrim approach — 21 km and about 6.5 hours of walking (8 with temple stops) from Kudoyama up to the Daimon gate, following five-sided stone pillars set every 109 m since the medieval period. Start at Jison-in, historically "Women's Kōya," where women banned from the mountain until 1872 worshipped instead. It makes a superb transfer day: hike up in the morning, reach the great vermilion gate for sunset, walk straight into your shukubō. Splittable at Kami-Kosawa or Kii-Hosokawa if you want it gentler. Free, no permits.

Himeji & Kōko-en 姫路城・好古園

A full day from Kyoto or Osaka · Shinkansen 30 min from Shin-Osaka · Kōko-en peaks with Kyoto

Japan's finest surviving original castle keep — never burned, never bombed, never shaken down — six wooden storeys of early-1600s construction nicknamed the White Heron for its plaster walls and wing-like rooflines. In autumn its own colour is modest; what you come for is Kōko-en next door, nine walled gardens in different Edo styles built on the site of the castle's west residence, and the most reliable maple in the region.

The counterintuitive finding, and the reason to read this before you plan: Himeji peaks slightly earlier than Kyoto, not later. The one dated first-hand report available — a 29 November visit — found Mount Shosha already past peak and dropping on the same day Kyoto was only starting to change, while Kōko-en was still at peak. The forested mountaintop colours and drops first; the low in-town gardens track Kyoto. So: if your Himeji day lands in late November, do Engyō-ji on Mount Shosha first — a thousand-year-old complex reached by ropeway, with the cliffside Maniden hall and the three-hall Mitsunodō that stood in for feudal Japan in The Last Samurai. If it slips into December, treat Shosha as landscape and let Kōko-en carry the colour.

Castle admission rose sharply to ¥2,500 for non-residents (¥2,600 combined with Kōko-en; Kōko-en alone is ¥400). Budget 1.5–2 hours for the keep, an hour for the garden, 2–4 for Shosha. Sunset around 16:45 means the Kōko-en illumination (15 Nov – 1 Dec, to 20:00) does real work in filling out the day, and the castle itself is floodlit white from sunset to midnight, free, with 15-minute lighting performances at 20:00 and 21:00.

Kaga Onsen: Natadera & the Kakusenkei gorge 加賀温泉郷

A day or a night out of Kanazawa · Shinkansen 18–20 min, ~¥2,500 · Peaks mid-Nov to early Dec

The easiest add-on on this list, because it is a short hop off a stop you are already making and because it colours later than Kenroku-en — which makes it the right move if you arrive in Kanazawa at the back end of the window and find the garden fading. Since the Hokuriku Shinkansen extension reached Kagaonsen Station, it is under twenty minutes away.

Natadera, founded in 717, is built into a cliff face around a sacred cave that has represented the cycle of life and death since the Jōmon period — walking through it is symbolically an act of rebirth. Koi pond, three-storey pagoda, bell tower, towering rock walls. Bashō wrote about the garden after visiting in the 1600s. Multiple sources call it "especially beautiful in autumn," with a window running mid-November into early December. Allow 45–60 minutes.

Then pick a hot-spring town. Yamanaka Onsen has the Kakusenkei gorge — a 1.3 km riverside path along the Daishōji, framed by the wooden Kōrogi-bashi and the red-steel Ayatori bridge, the best pure foliage walk near Kanazawa, with a night light-up in season and a riverside tea room open 10:00–15:00 in November (closed December–March). Yamashiro Onsen has Kosoyu, a faithfully rebuilt Meiji bathhouse you can soak in or simply look at. Two of Japan's most celebrated ryokan sit here — both listed under Kanazawa's stays above — if you'd rather make it an overnight.

The other trip: two weeks earlier, for the mountains もうひとつの秋

Not an add-on — a different itinerary · Late October to ~10 November

You asked whether the trip could be timed for peak colour in other places too, and the honest answer has a second half: there is a whole tier of famous Japanese autumn that a Kyoto-peak trip structurally cannot reach. Worth knowing, both so you don't build a plan around it and so you know what a future trip looks like.

Nikkō is the clearest case. Its season spans nearly 2,000 m of elevation and lasts two months, but it runs early: Oku-Nikkō's high plateau — Yumoto, Senjōgahara's boardwalks, Ryūzu Falls — peaks early-to-late October; Lake Chūzenji mid-October; the 48-hairpin Irohazaka road late October; and only the low shrine district and the Jizō-lined Kanmangafuchi gorge reach peak in early-to-mid November. Japan-guide's own historical chart for Nikkō ends at "early Nov" and never reaches mid-November. By your dates, both zones are typically bare. Tōshōgū, Kegon Falls and the lake remain magnificent regardless — they just aren't leaf-peeping.

Kamikōchi is even more absolute: the alpine valley peaks early-to-mid October and then physically closes on 15 November, buses and all, until late April. The Norikura Skyline to Tatamidaira at 2,702 m shuts on 31 October. Okuhida's onsen villages at 1,100 m peak in early November and a live 2025 tracker had them recorded as "leaves fallen" by 13 November.

So the mountain version of this journey is a late-October-to-10-November trip: Nikkō or the Japan Alps → Okuhida → Takayama and Shirakawa-gō → Kōyasan → Kanazawa, finishing as Kyoto is merely beginning to turn. Same wave, boarded higher up. You cannot have both in ten days — and if you have to choose, Kyoto at peak is the one that only happens here.

Before you go

What to pack

持ち物

You will cross about 15 °C of temperature range and two weather systems in nine days: a near-freezing mountain valley, the wettest coast in Honshū, and a mild city basin. Pack for the extremes, not the average.

The layers that matter

  • A proper insulated jacket — Hida nights near 0 °C; Kōyasan shukubō rooms are traditionally unheated
  • Thermal base layer — for the 6am temple openings and dawn garden visits
  • Gloves and a hat — the illumination walks are 3+ hours standing still after dark
  • A folding umbrella — Kanazawa gets 170–265 mm in November in sudden shigure squalls; locals carry one, not a shell

Feet and temples

  • Shoes you can take off one-handed — you will remove them a dozen times a day; laces get old fast
  • Warm socks, several pairs — unheated temple corridors are the coldest part of any day
  • Grippy soles — wet stone on Sannenzaka, Ishibei-kōji and Kakusenkei; possible ice in Okuhida
  • A small daypack, not a tote — Higashiyama's steps and Shirakawa-gō's viewpoint climb

For the pictures

  • A fast lens — illuminations are 17:30–21:30 and tripods are widely restricted
  • A polariser — cuts glare on wet leaves and pond reflections; the whole trip is reflections
  • Spare batteries, kept warm — cold plus 10-hour days
  • A microfibre cloth — mist at Sagi-ike and spray at Minoh Falls

Practical

  • Cash, more than feels reasonable — old kaiseki houses, shinise sweet shops, Kōyasan shops; ATMs at post offices and Family Mart
  • Your passport on you in Nara — Yoshiki-en garden is free to foreign visitors who show one
  • Printed temple reservations — Enkō-ji, Saihō-ji and Ruriko-in all gate entry
  • A bear bell — genuinely: bear sightings are reported on Takayama's Shiroyama Park trail

Leave at home

  • A heavy tripod — restricted at most ticketed gardens and useless in illumination crowds
  • A full winter parka — Kyoto, Nara and Osaka run 12–17 °C by day; you'd carry it all afternoon
  • Hiking boots — unless you walk the Chōishi Michi, every trail here is paved or boardwalked
  • Plans for Kamikōchi — it closes 15 November. Genuinely, delete it from the list
