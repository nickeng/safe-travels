Morocco by Colour

المغرب

Twelve unhurried days across five cities — the white one, the green one, the blue one, the saffron one, the red one.

Morocco announces itself by colour before it does anything else. In Casablanca it is bone-white — a city of Art Deco façades bleached by Atlantic salt, with the world's tallest minaret standing over the surf. Four hours north, Chefchaouen has painted every wall, step and flowerpot in the same lime-wash blue, so that the light in its lanes arrives already cooled. Fes runs saffron and indigo: the dye vats of the Chouara tannery, the brass of Place Seffarine, the green-glazed roof tiles above the alleys. And Marrakech is simply al-Hamra, the red — an entire flat city of pisé walls that turn from rose to rust between breakfast and dusk.

Between those colours are the constants. A glass of atay pressed on you within a minute of entering any shop. The five-times-daily call to prayer folding over rooftops. Bread going out to the neighbourhood ferrane in the morning and coming back at noon. Cats everywhere, asleep on warm tile. Charcoal smoke, orange blossom, cedar shavings, and — near the tanneries — a smell you will not forget, which is why the leather shops hand you a sprig of mint at the door.

This route is built for taking that in slowly. It runs south to north to south: land on the Atlantic, work up the coast to the capital, cross into the Rif mountains for the blue city, come down through the imperial cities to Fes, and finish in Marrakech — whose medina, crucially, is flat, which makes it the right place to end rather than begin. Each stop below gets its own colour, its own sights, its own tables and its own beds, and none of the five needs a single early start you did not choose.

The shape of it

  • 12 days, 11 nights Casablanca 2, Rabat 1, Chefchaouen 2, Fes 3, Marrakech 3.
  • Fly in and out of different airports. Land at Casablanca (CMN), fly home from Marrakech (RAK). No backtracking, and it saves a full travelling day.
  • Trains for the flat legs, a private driver for the mountains. Morocco's ONCF trains are comfortable and punctual; the Rif has no railway.
  • Two nights minimum anywhere you sleep. The one exception is Rabat, which works beautifully as a single unhurried night between two train rides.

What to know before you book

The medinas were built for feet, not wheels. This is the single most useful thing to understand about Morocco. The old cities are car-free by design — lanes are cobbled, uneven, occasionally stepped, and no vehicle reaches the door of a medina guesthouse. What happens instead is that your driver stops at the nearest gate and your host sends someone with a handcart for the bags; the last five to fifteen minutes are on foot. It works, and it works well, but it rewards soft-sided luggage over wheeled cases and a phone call ahead to confirm the pickup point.

Hire a licensed guide for your first morning in Fes. Not for the history — for the pacing. Fes el-Bali has thousands of alleys and a real gradient; a good guide picks the flatter route, knows where the shaded benches are, and heads off the "let me show you my cousin's carpet shop" economy before it starts. In Marrakech and Chefchaouen you can manage on your own.

Cash still runs the country. Cards work in hotels, larger restaurants and the modern ville nouvelle districts. Souks, grands taxis, tips, museum tickets and mint tea do not. Withdraw dirhams at an airport ATM on arrival — the currency is closed, so you cannot buy it before you go.

Two kinds of taxi. Petits taxis are the small coloured ones and only work inside the city — insist on the meter, or agree the fare first. Grands taxis are the big estate cars that run between towns and leave when full; for a couple it is worth paying for the whole car rather than waiting for four more passengers.

Dress and the mosque rule. Covered shoulders and knees for both of you keeps things easy everywhere, and is required at the Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca — the only working mosque on this route that non-Muslims may enter, and then only on a guided tour. Elsewhere, admire the minarets from outside.

Fridays and Ramadan change the day. Friday's midday meal is the week's fixed point — couscous, everywhere, after prayers — and many shops shut for two hours around it. During Ramadan, daytime cafés thin out and the evening becomes extraordinary; if you'd rather have normal opening hours, see the calendar below.

Tipping is small and constant. 10–20 MAD for a porter, a mosque guide, someone who steers you out of a dead end, or a photograph of a street performer. Keep a pocket of coins.

When to go

أحسن وقت

You didn't fix dates, which is a luxury — because on this particular route the month matters more than almost any other decision. The problem is that it crosses two climates at once: the Rif mountains, which are wet and cold in winter, and inland Marrakech and Fes, which in high summer are punishing. There are two windows where both are pleasant at the same time, and they are the best twelve days Morocco has to offer.

The short answer

  • Best of all: late April into May, or the second half of September into October. Warm but not fierce inland, dry in the mountains, long soft light, and the Atlas peaks still holding snow behind Marrakech in spring.
  • Also good: March and early November a little cooler, noticeably fewer people, lower riad prices.
  • Avoid July and August for the inland half of this route. If those are your only weeks, invert the trip and give the coast the time.
  • Winter works if you don't mind rain in the Rif and cold nights — Moroccan buildings are built to shed heat, not hold it, so check your riad has proper heating.
If your dates are flexible, aim for a Friday in Fes

Friday couscous is the most reliably wonderful meal in Morocco, and Fes — the country's acknowledged culinary capital — takes it most seriously. The Ruined Garden still hand-rolls its semolina on Friday mornings and you can watch it happen. Build the Fes leg so one of your days there is a Friday and you'll have eaten the single most Moroccan lunch of the trip.

What to pack

Most of this is ordinary. These are the things travellers to Morocco regularly get wrong.

Bring

  • A soft holdall, not a hard wheeled case — everything in the medinas is cobbles and steps, and a porter will carry a duffel far more easily than roll a Samsonite.
  • Closed, grippy, worn-in shoes — cobbles are broken, polished and occasionally slick; this is not sandal terrain.
  • A walking stick or trekking pole each — genuinely useful on Fes's sloped lanes and Chefchaouen's stepped alleys, and no one will look twice.
  • Layers, including one warm one — Chefchaouen at 600 m gets cold after sunset and Marrakech nights drop to 14°C even in October.
  • Light long sleeves and long trousers/skirt — modest cover doubles as sun protection and is required at the Hassan II Mosque.
  • A scarf — dust on the desert roads, sun on the ramparts, and useful at religious sites.
  • A wad of small dirham notes and coins — for porters, tips, taxis, tea.
  • Your own basic medicines — pharmacies are excellent but brands differ; bring what you already know works.

Leave behind

  • An umbrella — in the good months it barely rains; the awnings over the souks handle a shower.
  • Smart evening clothes — even the grandest riad dining rooms are relaxed. One neat outfit each is plenty.
  • A drone — drones are restricted in Morocco and can be confiscated at the airport.
  • Dirhams bought at home — the currency is closed; you can't legally get it abroad and airport ATMs are fine.
  • A big tripod — security at the Hassan II Mosque plaza takes an interest, and in the souks it's simply in the way. A small one folds into a bag.

The route

الطريق

Five stops, one direction, no doubling back. The three flat legs are trains — comfortable, cheap, and far less tiring than a car. The two mountain legs are best done with a private driver, which for a couple costs little more than two seats on a bus and lets you stop when you like.

Nights 1–2

Casablanca الدار البيضاء

Arrive · Mohammed V airport (CMN) · fixed-fare taxi to the centre, ~40 min

Land, sleep off the flight, then give the Hassan II Mosque a full unhurried morning. The Atlantic keeps this city 8–10°C cooler than the interior all year.

Night 3

Rabat الرباط

ONCF train from Casa Port · about 55 min · 90 km

The gentlest stop on the route: wide pavements, an ocean breeze, a small calm medina and Morocco's most photogenic citadel. One night is enough, and it breaks the long haul north.

Nights 4–5

Chefchaouen شفشاون

Private car, about 4 hr · or Al Boraq high-speed train to Tangier (~1 hr 20) then 2 hr by car

Up into the Rif. The train-plus-transfer option costs a little more and is markedly more comfortable — Africa's only high-speed line does the first two-thirds of the journey at 300 km/h.

Nights 6–8

Fes فاس

Private car south through the Rif via Ouezzane · about 4 hr

A genuinely beautiful drive through cork oak and olive terraces. Three nights here: one for the medina, one for Roman Volubilis and imperial Meknes, one spare — you will want it.

Nights 9–11

Marrakech مراكش

ONCF train, about 7 hr in first class · or a short connecting flight via Casablanca

The one long transit day of the trip. Book 1ère classe for reserved, air-conditioned seats and treat it as a rest day with scenery. Fly home from Marrakech Menara (RAK).

Book the trains in advance, in first class

ONCF sells online at oncf-voyages.ma. First class means a reserved seat in a six-person compartment or an air-conditioned coach rather than standing in a corridor, and on the Fes–Marrakech run that distinction matters enormously. It is still inexpensive. For the two mountain legs, ask your riad to arrange the driver — they use people they trust, and the price is usually better than an agency's.

Trimming to ten days

If you only have ten, cut in this order: one night in Marrakech (you lose the Agafay evening — a real loss, but not a structural one), then one night in Fes, which means dropping the Volubilis and Meknes day. That gives you Casablanca 2, Rabat 1, Chefchaouen 2, Fes 2, Marrakech 2 — nine nights, ten days, and nothing essential missing.

Do not cut Chefchaouen to one night. It takes four hours to reach and half its magic is the medina at dawn before the day-trip coaches arrive; a single night means you never see it.

And if you can stretch to fourteen, add Essaouira — two nights of Atlantic wind, ramparts and grilled sardines, three hours west of Marrakech. It is the best extension this route has.

Days worth timing

أيام خاصة

Morocco's calendar mixes fixed civil holidays with lunar religious ones that move roughly eleven days earlier each year. Nothing here is a reason to cancel a plan; two of them are good reasons to shift one by a few days.

Fes Festival of Sufi Culture

17–24 October 2026 · 18th edition · Fes

For eight October evenings, Fes gives itself over to Sufi music. Different brotherhoods take the stage each night at Bab Makina, the walled esplanade beneath the palace walls, and in the Jnan Sbil gardens — chanting, ney flute, hand drums, and Arab-Andalusian orchestras working through mystical poetry until late. Daytimes are conferences, poetry readings and exhibitions; the 2026 theme is La poésie mystique, élixir de guérison — mystical poetry as a healing elixir. Held under the patronage of the King, and much of the programme is free.

It runs alongside normal sightseeing rather than disrupting it — the crowds are in the evenings and concentrated around Bab Makina. The practical catch is beds: late October is already peak shoulder season in Fes, and festival week fills the good riads. Book several months ahead if your dates overlap, and check the programme at festivalculturesoufie.com.

Not to be confused with the far better-known Fes Festival of World Sacred Music, which is a different, larger event held in May and June.

Also on the calendar

  • Friday, every week — الكسكس couscous day. The one date that will definitely fall inside your trip. Shops close for two hours after midday prayers and every good kitchen in the country serves one dish. Book a table.
  • Monday, every week — Tetouan's market day. If you're in Chefchaouen on a Monday, the souk in nearby Tetouan spills out through the medina gates: fishmongers, spice piles, produce on sheets, motorbikes threading the crowd.
  • Ramadan — approx. 8 Feb – 9 Mar 2027. Daytime cafés run thin and some restaurants close until sunset; then the whole country sits down together to harira, dates and chebakia, and the medinas stay awake half the night. Extraordinary to witness; harder to sightsee through. Dates are moon-dependent.
  • 31 October — Unity Day (عيد الوحدة). Morocco's newest public holiday, first observed in 2026. A civic commemoration rather than a street festival: government offices and many shops close, but hotels, restaurants, taxis and mosque tours run as normal. Government-run museums may keep odd hours — worth a check.
  • 6 November — Green March Day. A long-standing national holiday marking the 1975 march into Western Sahara. Same practical effect: offices shut, tourism carries on.
The quiet months are a feature. Outside these dates, Morocco's calendar is genuinely empty for most of autumn — no festival crowds, no closures to plan around, and the big sights at their least congested. If timing around an event isn't your priority, that emptiness is worth more than any programme.

The Moroccan table

المائدة المغربية

Moroccan cooking has one grammatical rule that explains almost everything on it: sweet and savoury belong in the same mouthful. Cinnamon goes on meat. Icing sugar goes on a pigeon pie. Caramelised onions and raisins go on top of a lamb couscous, and preserved lemon cuts across all of it. Once you stop expecting the two to stay in separate courses, the whole repertoire opens up. Learn five dishes and you can eat brilliantly for twelve days.

Friday, and the one dish everybody eats

After the midday jumu'ah prayer on Friday, Morocco stops and eats couscous. Not couscous as a side — seksu as the entire event: one enormous shared platter of hand-rolled semolina, steamed twice over a broth of lamb or chicken, mounded with the "seven vegetables" (carrot, turnip, courgette, pumpkin, cabbage, chickpeas, and whatever the cook fancies — the number is idiomatic, not literal), and crowned with tfaya. Everyone eats from the same dish. A glass of iben buttermilk on the side. Rolling the grain into a neat ball with your right hand marks you out as an old hand; a spoon is entirely acceptable.

The tradition traces to the arrival of Islam in the Maghreb, and it is the most consistently kept food ritual in the country — the closest thing to a Sunday roast, except the whole nation sits down at once. It also varies by where you are: Casablanca's version, couscous bidaoui, leans on the market gardens of the surrounding plain; the Rif version around Chefchaouen runs heavier on chickpeas and mountain roots; in Fes, a handful of kitchens still roll the grain by hand on Friday mornings rather than buying it ready-made.

Ordering couscous on a Tuesday marks you as a tourist. Ordering it on Friday, at a family-run place visibly full of Moroccan families, puts you inside the week's actual rhythm — which is a better souvenir than anything in the souk.

Tanjia, and the fire under the bathhouse

Marrakech has its own dish, and it exists because of the city's plumbing. Before refrigerators and private ovens, a dense medina shared its infrastructure: the neighbourhood ferrane baked everybody's bread, and the wood furnace beneath the hammam heated everybody's bath water. Tanjia uses the spare heat: a cheap cut of beef or lamb shank goes into a sealed clay urn with preserved lemon, garlic, cumin, saffron and olive oil, and the farnachi — the man who tends the fire — buries it in the embers in the morning while its owner goes to work. By evening the meat falls apart at a touch.

It was a working man's lunch, prepared by men, which earned it the nickname "bachelor's stew"; it was already established enough to appear in local scholarly writing by the 17th century. It survives in two forms: the original, collected from the furnace and eaten straight from the urn with torn bread among friends in a garden, and a more decorated restaurant version. Both are real. Only the setting has changed.

Pastilla, and the kitchen that arrived from Spain

The sweet-savoury rule has an address. Fes was the first Moroccan city to formally welcome the Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain, establishing its mellah in the 15th century, and the techniques that arrived with those cooks — pickling, olive-curing, and above all the layering of meat with cinnamon and sugar in one dish — are the reason Fassi food tastes the way it does. Pastilla is the monument: shredded pigeon, spiced egg and toasted almonds sealed inside sheets of warqa pastry so thin it shatters, then dusted white with icing sugar. It takes hours. It is a celebration dish — the thing a Fassi household serves when the occasion genuinely matters — and you should order it in a kitchen with enough staff to do it properly.

1

Casablanca

الدار البيضاء

Nobody comes to Morocco for Casablanca, and everybody should give it a day anyway. The name means the white house — Dar el-Beida in Arabic, which is what the taxi driver will call it — and the city is exactly that: a bleached Atlantic boom town of five million people, the country's engine, with one of the richest Art Deco streetscapes in Africa and a mosque built partly over the sea. It is Morocco's least performed city. Nobody here is selling you a carpet. After a long flight, that turns out to be a mercy.

Give it the arrival evening and one full day. The sights are spread out, so move between them by taxi or tram rather than trying to walk the whole city — but every individual sight is flat, paved and easy underfoot, which makes this the gentlest introduction the route could have.

Arrival, solved

  • Airport → city A fixed-tariff taxi runs 250–300 MAD and takes about 40 minutes, door to door with no stairs. The under-terminal train is cheaper and about 45 minutes, but means luggage on platforms and steps.
  • Where to base The centre around Place des Nations Unies and Casa-Port station — it removes any second transfer on a jet-lagged evening.
  • Onward ONCF from Casa-Port to Rabat, roughly 55 minutes, frequent. Casa-Voyageurs is the mainline station for Fes and Marrakech.
The mosque and the sea

Casablanca's one unmissable thing stands where the city runs out of land. Do it in the morning, then walk the seafront — it is the flattest, breeziest, most bench-lined two hours in the city, and the reason locals come down here at six every evening.

Practicalities at the mosque

Non-Muslims enter only on a guided tour — roughly 45 minutes to an hour, running several times daily except during Friday prayers, around 130–140 MAD each. Shoulders, chest and knees covered for both of you; shoes come off at the door and go into a bag you carry. Reduced-mobility access and equipment are available — worth confirming when you buy tickets. Morning tours are calmer; late afternoon puts better light on the marble. Don't skip the lower level: the vaulted ablutions hall beneath the prayer hall is the thing most visitors remember longest.

The Art Deco century

Between the 1920s and the 1940s, French Protectorate architects built an entire downtown in a style they called Mauresque — Art Deco geometry crossed with Moroccan ornament, wrought-iron balconies, covered arcades, zellige panels on apartment blocks. Much of it is faded and still lived in, which is precisely the pleasure of it. This is a stroll, not a checklist: an hour along Boulevard Mohammed V and its side streets, with café seating under the arcades whenever you want to stop.

The new medina

Casablanca had no old city worth speaking of, so in the 1930s it built one — arcaded, orderly, laid out on a grid, with courtyards and craft workshops and an oven on every corner. Quartier Habous is a planned reinterpretation of a medina rather than the real thing, and for that exact reason it is the easiest "souk experience" in Morocco: wide shaded lanes, no maze, no hustle. It is also the best pastry in the city.

Where to eat

Casablanca has no single native dish — it is a 20th-century melting pot where migrants from every region and colonial-era French settlers converged, so its table is a sampler of the whole country plus an Atlantic seafood tradition and a café culture laid over all of it. Which makes it an unusually good first stop: you can taste half of Morocco here before you've left the coast.

Where to stay

For two nights bracketing a long flight, the answer is the city centre around Place des Nations Unies and Casa Port station. It solves the thing that matters most on arrival day — no second transfer — and puts you a short flat walk from the station you leave from. Casablanca is a working port city, not a riad destination; the honest recommendation here is a proper hotel with a lift, and to ask for a courtyard-facing room away from the boulevard.

2

Rabat

الرباط

Fifty-five minutes up the coast, the volume drops. Rabat is the administrative capital and behaves like one: wide boulevards, functioning pavements, embassies behind hedges, and a compact historic core you can take in over a single unhurried day. Its citadel is washed cobalt and white above the mouth of the Bou Regreg river; its most famous monument is a minaret that was never finished, standing over two hundred columns of a mosque that was never built. And its medina is the one first-timers should meet first — a working old town, calm, small, and refreshingly indifferent to whether you buy anything.

One night is genuinely enough, and it breaks the journey north beautifully. The core — Kasbah, Hassan Tower, medina — is four to six hours at a relaxed pace with café stops. If you arrive early enough, or want a second half-day, Chellah and Salé are both waiting.

The flat route through Rabat

  • Hassan Tower & Mausoleum The single most level major site on the route — an open marble esplanade, a few steps into the mausoleum itself. Free.
  • Bou Regreg promenade Flat, well maintained, about an hour. The low-effort way to end a day.
  • Andalusian Gardens Gravel paths, benches, public toilets, a café. Closed Tuesdays.
  • Care needed The kasbah’s lanes are cobbled and some are steep; Chellah’s ground is uneven, and it is a 10-minute taxi from the centre rather than a walk.
  • Tram Modern, reliable, about 6 dh — and the flattest transport on the whole trip. Across to Salé in a few minutes.
See & do

Two of Rabat's four great sights sit on flat, open ground — the Hassan Tower esplanade and the Mausoleum are described even by accessibility-focused guides as level, bar a few steps up to the tomb itself. The Kasbah's lanes are cobbled and some are steep, but the reward is immediate: through the monumental Almohad gate, and everything turns blue and white and quiet. Below the ramparts, the Andalusian Gardens are flat, shaded, full of orange trees, and often have someone playing the oud under them.

The half-day extra: Salé, across the river

Rabat's sister city sits directly opposite across the Bou Regreg, and the tram gets you there in a few minutes for about 6 dirhams — the easiest, flattest piece of transport on this entire route. Salé's medina is smaller and quieter than Rabat's and sees almost no tourists at all; the 14th-century Abou Hassan Medersa is a miniature of the great Fes schools without a single tour group in it (80 dh; open 9.30–4.30 — and if a staff member offers to guide you, agree the fee at the door). Walk a stretch of the western walls for the view back over a vast cemetery towards the Kasbah, and if you have appetite for one more thing, the Oulja pottery complex gathers ceramics, metalwork and basketry workshops under one roof. Two to four hours, entirely at your own speed. More than one visitor who knows both cities prefers it to Rabat.

Where to eat

Rabat eats like a capital that has never had to perform for anyone. There is no medina food-stall spectacle here — instead a French-inflected café culture laid over a deeply Andalusian kitchen, both built on the same unhurried ritual of a shared plate and a lot of time. The medina was rebuilt in the 17th century by tens of thousands of Moriscos expelled from Spain, and their descendants' surnames are still on the shopfronts: Ronda, Cortobi, Almodóvar, Malgui. You can taste it — the citrus-and-olive edge of a Rbati tagine, the pastry-making, the formal three-course rhythm of a proper lunch.

Street food is genuinely scarce in Rabat compared with Marrakech or Fes — which, for a comfort-first stop, is no loss at all.

Where to stay

For one night between two journeys there is only one sensible base: the Hassan / Centre-Ville grid around Rabat Ville station. Flat streets, wide pavements, walking distance to Hassan Tower and the medina, and the platform you leave from is right there. It quiets down completely after dark — this is the administrative capital, not a nightlife town.

3

Chefchaouen

شفشاون

Four hours north of Rabat the road climbs into the Rif mountains, and then there is a town on a hillside where every wall, every step, every doorframe and every flowerpot has been washed in the same lime blue. Not one blue — dozens of them, freshened by hand at different times, so a single alley runs from powder to ultramarine to something almost violet in shadow. The effect is not photogenic so much as physical: the light in these lanes arrives already cooled, and you notice the temperature drop as you turn a corner into it.

Chefchaouen sits at around 600 metres beneath the twin peaks that give it its name, and it is small — the medina is compact enough that, as one repeat visitor put it, you can't get too lost. That matters here more than anywhere on this route, because the whole town is built up a slope. But it also means the pleasure is entirely unstructured. There is no great monument to queue for. You come to walk slowly, sit down often, and look at a colour.

The one piece of timing advice that changes everything: be out before nine. The day-trip coaches from Tangier and Fes arrive mid-morning and leave by four, so between roughly 10am and 3pm the famous stairways have queues. Before that, and again after five, the town belongs to the people who live in it — women carrying bread to the oven, cats on the warm steps, and almost nobody with a camera.

Reading the gradient

  • Easiest Plaza Uta el-Hammam and the Kasbah gardens — level. Ras el-Maa, the spring at the medina’s edge — a gentle, flat walk, with café tables set right in the shallow water.
  • Manageable The blue lanes generally: flat cobble with occasional short flights. The medina walks end to end in about 15 minutes at an easy pace.
  • Short stairs Derb El Assri’s flowerpot staircase; the Kasbah tower — three floors, and skippable, because the ground-floor garden is a complete visit.
  • Real climb The Spanish Mosque: 20–30 minutes of sustained paved uphill, 45–60 at a slow pace. Fully stepped, no scrambling, but almost nowhere to rest. Optional.
  • Skip The Grand Cascade hike at Akchour — 11 km round trip, 4–5 hours, uneven ground and river crossings on stepping stones.
Read this before you book a room

Chefchaouen is the least easy town on this route to walk, and it's better to know that now than to discover it with a suitcase. One specialist accessible-travel guide puts it bluntly: "the streets are steep, the steps are frequent, and even the flatter sections are rough cobblestone."

None of which means you shouldn't come — a guide written specifically for older travellers is encouraging on exactly this point: if you can manage 15 to 30 minutes on uneven ground at a slow pace, take stairs with a handrail, and rest when you need to, Chefchaouen is well within reach. What it means is plan around the gradient rather than distance: sleep at the medina's edge, don't climb the same hill twice in a day, and bring a stick. Two nights at that pace is genuinely restful. One night at a rush is miserable.

Blue, at ground level

Everything here is flat, or near enough, and all of it sits within about fifteen minutes' walk of the main square. The order that works: the square first for coffee and orientation, then the lanes and the weaving shops at whatever pace suits, then out through the eastern gate to the spring, where the café tables stand in the shallow water.

Worth the steps

Two that cost some climbing. The first is a short flight you can stop halfway up — the Kasbah's ground-floor garden is a complete visit on its own. The second is the only real ascent in town, and it belongs to the end of the day, because there is a sunset at the top of it.

The gentler option, if the climb doesn't appeal

Stop partway. The hillside terraces below the mosque building already look out over the whole blue medina; the last stretch adds height, not a different view. Treat the first bench you like as the summit — and take a torch if you're staying for blue hour, because the path is uneven in the dark.

Or don't climb at all. Book a riad with a rooftop terrace and take the panorama with a glass of tea in your hand as the streetlights come on and the warm dusk tones fight the blue walls. It is, arguably, the better version.

The corners worth finding

Chefchaouen rewards aimlessness more than any town in Morocco, and most of your best photographs will come from a lane you turned down for no reason. But a handful of specific corners are genuinely worth walking to — and one important courtesy applies to all of them: these are people's homes. Residents live behind those doors and use those steps. Move through rather than settling in, step aside for anyone coming or going, and ask before photographing anyone.

Where to eat

Chefchaouen eats like the mountain town it is, not like the Morocco of the guidebooks. Cut off from the coast and from Marrakech's souks by a wall of peaks, it built its table from what the hillsides gave it — goat herds, river trout, dried fava beans, walnuts, olive terraces and dark wild honey — simpler and earthier than the tagines of the imperial cities. The single most useful thing to know: the cafés ringing the postcard-blue square charge for the view and often batch-cook what they serve, while the small family rooms one or two alleys back — several run entirely by local women — are where the honest cooking is. Treat the square as the place for tea, and the alleys as the place for dinner.

The day runs earlier here than in the big cities, and it is worth eating to the local clock. Ftour is bread with olive oil, jben and mint tea between about 6.30 and 9.30, best taken at your guesthouse; the morning stalls in the alleys are packed away by mid-morning. Ghda, roughly one until three, is the real meal, and the hour when the alley kitchens fill with families rather than visitors. Dinner starts around seven and winds down by half nine — noticeably earlier than Fes or Marrakech, and several places shut early, so a late table is a gamble rather than a plan.

The Rif table

  • Jben and honey fresh goat's cheese drained in woven moulds, with bread and dark Rif honey.
  • Bissara fava bean purée from an unsigned pot on a gas ring in the alleys off the square, from about 6am.
  • Rif trout grilled, simply. The best version is at Akchour, where riverside stalls cook it the day it's caught.
  • Tagines heavier and herbier here than further south: lamb with prunes and almonds, chicken with olives and preserved lemon.

Chefchaouen's restaurants are barely photographed by anyone with a camera and a byline, so these are text-only — but they're the four names that come up repeatedly from people who actually ate in them. Rooftop terraces almost always mean a stair climb; ask at the door whether there's ground-floor seating before you commit to a table, especially after dark.

Where to stay

There is really one choice to make, and it is not about décor. The medina's edge, around Plaza Mohammed V and Bab el-Ain, is the seam where normal streets meet the old town gates: taxis and private drivers pull up on tarmac, the larger hotels are here, and your walk in is as short as it gets. The deep medina, up around the square and the Kasbah, is the postcard — and reaching most guesthouses there means several minutes down narrow stepped lanes with no vehicle access at all.

Whichever you pick, ask your host to send someone to meet your taxi at the nearest vehicle point. It is completely standard practice here and it shortens the hard part of the day considerably.

A deliberately short list. Every larger "car-accessible" hotel in town was checked and most failed on inspection — broken lifts, no lift at all, or an address inside the medina walls despite the marketing. These two are the best-evidenced compromises rather than perfect answers; confirm a ground-floor or near-lift room directly with the property before booking.

4

Fes

فاس

If Morocco has a soul, everyone in Morocco agrees it lives here. Fes el-Bali is the largest car-free urban area on earth — a medieval city of thousands of alleys where goods still move by mule and handcart, where the university has been running since 859, and where the tanners are working the same stone vats their ancestors worked in the eleventh century. It is also, by universal Moroccan consent, the country's gastronomic capital: pastilla was perfected here, and a Fassi will tell you so unprompted.

Nothing on this route is as intense. The medina is genuinely disorienting, genuinely crowded, and built on a slope — everything is downhill on the way in and uphill on the way back. Which is why three nights, not two, is the number that makes Fes a pleasure instead of an ordeal. The sights fall into two natural days: one inside the old medina, slow and on foot with a guide; one for the flatter outer city and the great day trip west to Roman Volubilis. The third day is the one you'll be glad of.

Working the medina

  • Bab Boujloud is road-accessible A petit taxi drops you at the gate itself, even though everything beyond is sloped cobble.
  • Plan gate to gate Petits taxis reach every medina gate — Boujloud, Guissa, Ftouh, Rcif. Start and end loops at a gate rather than deep inside.
  • Porters exist Riads inside the walls can send someone with a handcart to meet you. Ask when you book, not on arrival.
  • The rest stop Jnan Sbil gardens — flat, shaded, paved, benches throughout. The best recovery point in the city.
  • Flattest sight The Royal Palace gates on Place des Alaouites: a wide, level plaza, reachable by taxi, no steps.
  • Hilltop viewpoint Take a petit taxi from outside Bab Boujloud up to the Marinid Tombs. The walk up is steep and disorienting.
How to do Fes without being defeated by it

Take a licensed guide for your first morning. Not for the commentary — for the route. A good one picks the flatter lanes, knows where the shaded benches and the clean cafés are, sets a pace to match yours, and quietly deflects the shop-commission economy that follows unaccompanied visitors around. After that first morning you'll have your bearings and can wander alone.

Inside Fes el-Bali

A single day, taken slowly, covers all of this. The classic line runs from the Blue Gate down Talaa Kebira, the medina's main artery, past the madrasas and the specialised souks — separate streets for dyed wool, hammered brass, woven cloth, spices, musical instruments — to the tanneries in the north-east quarter, and there are two beautiful courtyards and one extraordinary rooftop along the way.

A quieter tannery. Chouara is the famous one, and the terraces above it are unavoidably a shop floor — expect a light sales pitch and tip 10–20 MAD if you don't buy. If you'd rather see the same medieval process at a tenth of the scale with almost nobody else there, ask your riad about Ain Azliten, a much smaller tannery off the tourist route. One visiting family called it the highlight of their entire day in Fes.
The easy half — Fes el-Jdid and the gardens

The second day is deliberately gentle, and taxis link the stops. Fes el-Jdid — the "new" Fes, founded in the 13th century — has wider, flatter, calmer streets than the old medina, and the finest thing in it is free: seven vast brass doors set into a tiled façade, kept gleaming with lemon juice. Next door, the historic Jewish quarter is a complete change of key: lower buildings, candy-coloured facades and wooden balconies that look more Andalusian than Moroccan. And between the two cities lies the garden that will save your legs.

The day trip: Volubilis, Moulay Idriss & Meknes

Private car · 6–7 hours door to door · all of it seated between stops

West of Fes, the landscape opens into rolling farmland, and on a ridge above it stand the most complete Roman ruins in Morocco. Pair them with a holy hilltop town and an imperial capital and you have the best full day this route offers outside the cities themselves. Most visitors hire a driver or join a small group; the standard loop runs Fes → Volubilis → Moulay Idriss for lunch → Meknes → Fes.

  1. 1

    Volubilis وليلي

    A Roman provincial capital abandoned to the fields, spread along a ridge with sweeping valley views. Past the entrance and its small museum of bronzes and inscribed tablets, the South Quarter's modest houses give way to a preserved central district holding dozens of intact mosaic floors — dolphins, animals, a zodiac — then the forum and the freestanding triumphal arch. The Villa of Venus near the top exit has the most vivid mosaics of all. Almost no shade: hat, water, sun cover.

    1.5–2.5 hr · uneven ancient paving, gentle uphill, few benches · UNESCO
  2. 2

    Moulay Idriss Zerhoun مولاي إدريس

    Morocco's holiest small town, stacked in white terraces across two hilltops five kilometres from Volubilis, built around the tomb of the man who founded the country's first Arab dynasty. Most tours stop here for lunch on a terrace looking back over the olive valley — which is the right way to use it. The upper lanes are steep; the viewpoint and the restaurants are not.

    Lunch stop · 45–60 min · the view is the point
  3. 3

    Bab Mansour, Meknes باب المنصور

    Sultan Moulay Ismail's late-17th-century imperial capital opens with the most theatrical gate in Morocco: sixteen metres of horseshoe arch flanked by marble columns, carved wood and zellige, facing the wide open Place El-Hedim. Legend has it the sultan executed the architect for admitting it could have been more beautiful still. Level, easy, and utterly worth the stop.

    Flat & open · 30 min · UNESCO
  4. 4

    Heri es-Souani هري السواني

    Just south of the gate, the sultan's ruined granary and royal stables — built to hold twelve thousand horses. Inside, a maze of barrel-vaulted chambers with walls metres thick, kept cool by underground cisterns and a water wheel you can still see. Outside, the collapsed stable roofs have left a forest of open-air arches marching into the distance. It has been used as a film set more than once, and you'll understand why immediately.

    45 min–1 hr · flat but uneven original stonework
Where to eat

This is the best eating on the route, and it repays booking ahead. The riad dining rooms are where the refined end of Fassi cooking lives — pastilla done properly, slow-cooked lamb, courtyards lit by lanterns — while the street stalls of the Achabine market off Talaa Kebira do the other half: potato fritters, harira, kefta sandwiches, fried sardines. Note the geography: medina restaurants are reached on foot down alleys and often have their best seating up stairs, so ask for a ground-floor or courtyard table when you book. The Ville Nouvelle addresses are the sure bet for level, step-free access.

Where to stay

Fes is the one stop on this route where where you sleep genuinely changes the trip. Sleep inside the medina's edge and you step out of your door at seven in the morning into a medieval city with nobody in it — the single best hour of the whole twelve days. Sleep in the new town and you get a lift, a car to the door and a taxi ride to the same experience. There is no wrong answer; there is only which of those two you'd rather have.

5

Marrakech

مراكش

Al-Hamra, the red — an entire city of rammed-earth walls the colour of terracotta, which shift from rose at breakfast to rust at dusk, with the snow line of the High Atlas floating behind them. Marrakech is the loudest, most theatrical, most photographed place in Morocco, and it is also the shrewdest place to finish this trip, for one plain reason: its medina is flat. After the gradients of Chefchaouen and Fes, that is the single biggest comfort win on the route. The lanes are still uneven cobbles with unmarked steps and no dropped kerbs — but they're wider, and the walking is level.

Three nights is comfortable and unhurried at a retiree's pace: one or two main things a day, mornings for sightseeing, the hot part of the afternoon for a rest or a hammam, activity resuming around four or five when the light softens, and one full day kept free for an excursion out of the city.

Pacing the red city

  • The easiest three Jardin Majorelle (level paved paths), Bahia Palace (single-level throughout the main route), La Mamounia’s gardens (taxi to the door).
  • Book Majorelle ahead Timed online entry is effectively required, and the 8 am slot is the single best crowd-avoidance move in the city.
  • The demanding one The souks: narrow, crowded, uneven, with motorbikes moving fast through tight lanes. A guided half-day removes the navigation and sets the pace.
  • Skip the climb Le Jardin Secret’s tower, 40 MAD extra — the ground-level gardens are the real pleasure, and every rooftop restaurant offers the same view.
  • Riads and cars Taxis stop at a medina gate; someone walks you the last 5–15 minutes. Or base yourself in Gueliz or Hivernage, where cars reach the door.
  • Check before you go Bahia Palace’s main courtyard was still partly under earthquake reconstruction as of late 2025.
Palaces and courtyards

Marrakech's great interiors are all within a short walk of each other in the southern medina, and every one of them is essentially a set of rooms around a courtyard — which means they're mostly single-level, mostly shaded, and there is always somewhere to sit. Go at opening. The tour groups arrive in waves from mid-morning and the palaces fill hard between about 10.30 and 2.30.

Gardens, shade and the middle of the day

The genuinely important trick to Marrakech is not seeing more; it is knowing where to go when the sun is high. These four places are the answer — three gardens with water in them and one bathhouse — and treating the hours between noon and four as time to be spent sitting down is what separates a wonderful three days here from an exhausting one.

The hammam, and why to do it on your hottest afternoon

A hammam is a steam-and-scrub ritual, and it is the single best use of a Marrakech afternoon: indoors, cool, lying down, and a genuine rest rather than another walking outing. The sequence is a warm steam room to open the pores, then a full-body scrub with black savon beldi and a coarse kessa glove, then rinsing — an hour to ninety minutes in a spa version. For this trip, choose a private or riad hammam over a communal public one: same ritual, gentler pace, no language barrier, and your own room. Ask your hotel to book a specific one they use.

The square, and the souks behind it

Jemaa el-Fnaa is a UNESCO-listed cultural space rather than a monument, which is a fussy way of saying that what's protected is the crowd itself — the storytellers, musicians, henna painters and juice sellers who have been working this square for centuries. It is also where the city concentrates all of its hustle. The move that solves this completely is to watch it from above first: take a table on one of the café terraces ringing the north side, order a mint tea, and spend half an hour looking down at it before you go in at ground level. Late afternoon into early evening is the sweet spot — the grills fire up around four and the whole square becomes a grid of glowing carts, without the full crush of peak night.

In the souks: haggling is expected and nothing carries a price label — smiling and staying relaxed about it makes the whole thing far more pleasant. If photographing a performer or an artisan, hold a 10–20 MAD note visibly before you take the shot; it sets expectations cleanly and avoids the awkward version. The covered lanes are shaded, which makes the souks a better midday option than the open square.

The day out of the city

Keep one of your three days for this. Two options, both about an hour away in opposite directions — one desert, one mountain. Pick by whether you'd rather watch a sunset or drive up a green valley; you cannot comfortably do both.

The one to book

Agafay Desert أكافاي

Car · 40–60 min · late afternoon to ~11 pm

Not the Sahara — the dunes are eight or nine hours away and unreachable on a three-night stay. Agafay is a rocky, lunar plateau of rolling grey-white hills about 35 km south-west of the city, and what it delivers is the desert evening without the desert drive: gold light raking across empty ridges, the Atlas on the horizon, dinner under canvas with Berber musicians, and your riad by eleven. One traveller who had driven to Merzouga put it exactly right — for a day trip, it delivers the goods "without the 9-hour bus seat imprint on the spine."

The standard late-afternoon run stops first at a women's argan oil cooperative — tea, a short demonstration of nuts being hand-cracked and cold-pressed, no hard sell — then out to a camp for sunset and dinner. Camel rides and quad bikes are usually offered: both are entirely skippable, and getting on and off a camel is abrupt enough that it's worth declining if hips or backs are a consideration. Operators run "sunset and dinner only" versions with hotel pickup, and that is the one to ask for.

💡 Ask for a private transfer rather than a shared minibus. It costs a little more and means you leave when the light is right rather than when the last group is ready — and you're not the couple waiting in a hot van at a pickup point.
The green alternative

Ourika Valley & Setti Fatma أوريكة

Car · 1–1.5 hr each way · full day · 60 km south

The closest real mountain scenery to Marrakech, and a complete change of palette. The road follows the Ourika river up into the High Atlas foothills, and after about the 30 km mark the ochre gives way to genuinely green terraced farmland, orchards and stone Berber villages — the drive itself is the main event, so treat it as scenery rather than transit. Most trips include an argan cooperative stop (interesting, and a soft sell for oil, cosmetics and amlou, which is worth buying), then lunch at Setti Fatma, the valley's main village, on a riverside terrace above the water. If your day falls on a Friday, the village's weekly souk is a working local market rather than a tourist one.

Skip the Seven Waterfalls climb. The trail above Setti Fatma is a scramble over wet, slippery rock — it needs proper footwear and it is the one stop on the day best treated as optional. The first waterfall viewpoint is reachable on an easier path if you want a look. Go on a weekday: at weekends, half of Marrakech has the same idea. A private driver round trip runs roughly 300–450 MAD, versus 20–30 per person in a shared grand taxi — worth it for the timing alone.

Where to eat

Marrakech has the widest range on the route, and it splits usefully in two. In the medina, the pleasure is the setting — hidden gardens behind unmarked doors, riad courtyards, rooftops over the rooftops. In Gueliz, the new town, the pleasure is comfort: level streets, ground-floor rooms, no alleys to navigate, and some of the best traditional cooking in the city, including two kitchens run entirely by women. Book ahead for dinner; nowhere really fills until 8.30 or 9.

In the medina — the setting is the point

Gardens behind unmarked doors, riad courtyards, rooftops above the rooftops. All within the flat old city; a couple have their best seating upstairs, noted where it matters.

In Gueliz — comfort, and two kitchens run by women

Level streets, ground-floor rooms, a taxi to the door — and, as it happens, some of the most serious traditional cooking in Marrakech. If you eat one properly memorable dinner here, make it Al Fassia.

Where to stay

Marrakech is the place on this route to choose comfort over romance. The medina riads are lovely and they are also multi-storey houses at the end of car-free alleys; you've already had that experience in Fes. Here, three car-accessible districts offer gardens, pools, lifts and a driveway your taxi pulls into — which, on the last three nights of a twelve-day trip, is exactly right.

If you have longer

زيادة

Two places didn't make the twelve days but very nearly did. Neither requires restructuring the route — one bolts onto the end, one slots into a day you already have.

Add two days at the end

Essaouira الصويرة

Car or coach from Marrakech · 175 km · 2½–3 hr each way · 2 nights

If you can stretch to fourteen days, this is the best thing to do with them. Essaouira is a whitewashed, blue-shuttered fortified port on the Atlantic, three hours west of Marrakech — and the point is the contrast. You come out of the red city's heat and dust into a place that is cool, breezy and salt-washed, where you will want a light jacket in the evening even when Marrakech is warm.

The medina is UNESCO-listed, small, completely flat and — this matters after twelve days of haggling — noticeably calmer and less pressured than Marrakech's souks. Walk the ramparts of the Skala de la Ville, where the old cannon batteries look straight out at the breakers. Eat at the grills right on the harbour, where you pick your fish off the ice and they cook it in front of you. Then do very little for a day and a half, which is exactly what a twelve-day trip through five cities has earned you.

As a day trip from Marrakech it is a poor idea — five to six hours in the car for a few hours in town. As two nights it's a different trip entirely, and it's easy to arrange: a private transfer out, and Essaouira has its own small airport with seasonal European connections if you'd rather fly home from there.

A Monday from Chefchaouen

Tetouan تطوان

Grand taxi or private driver · 65 km · 1 hr 25 · half a day, ideally a Monday

An hour and a half north of Chefchaouen is a UNESCO-listed medina that almost nobody visits — a working town rather than a stage set, rebuilt at the end of the 15th century by Andalusian refugees fleeing the Reconquista, with a Spanish-Moroccan architectural blend you won't find anywhere else in the country. Its street signs are still bilingual in Spanish.

Go on a Monday. That's market day, and the souk spills out through the old gates into the newer streets: fishmongers, spice piles, produce heaped on sheets spread over the pavement, shoppers and motorbikes competing for the same six feet of road. One veteran travel journalist who caught it put it plainly — "stalls cover the footpaths and shoppers jostle with motorbikes on the streets." It is the least performed market on this whole route.

One caveat: Tetouan is built on a steep slope rising out of a valley, and the upper residential lanes are a maze of dead-end stairways that visitors regularly get lost in. Stay in the lower, flatter market streets near the gates, see the Royal Palace exterior on Place Hassan II, browse the souks, and don't climb. A private driver from Chefchaouen rather than a shared taxi lets you control the pace and get dropped at the right gate.

What this route deliberately leaves out — and why

The Sahara. The great dunes of Erg Chebbi near Merzouga are the image everyone has of Morocco, and they are eight to nine hours' drive from Marrakech in each direction, over the Tizi n'Tichka pass. Doing them properly needs three days of a twelve-day trip and two of them are spent in a car. Agafay gives you the desert evening in one; if the dunes themselves are non-negotiable for you, build a separate two-week trip around the southern circuit rather than bolting them onto this one.

Aït Benhaddou and the Atlas passes. The mud-brick fortified village every film crew uses is 3½ to 4 hours from Marrakech — seven-plus hours of mountain switchbacks round trip. Genuinely spectacular, genuinely a poor fit for a three-night stay at an unhurried pace.

Tangier. You'll pass close to it if you take the high-speed train option into the Rif, and it would be easy to add a night. It's a fascinating, scrappy, literary port city — but it competes with Chefchaouen for the same days, and Chefchaouen wins.

Please check before you go

  • Prices and hours drift. Hotel prices here are approximate calibrations from searches at roughly two months out, not quotes for your dates. Museum fees and opening times change — and several sites on this route close on Tuesdays.
  • Bahia Palace sustained damage to its main courtyard in the 2023 earthquake and sections were still under reconstruction as of late 2025. Worth a check with your hotel before you go.
  • The Marinid Tombs in Fes were reported closed for restoration works in 2025 — the hilltop viewpoint is unaffected, but confirm access to the ruins themselves.
  • Ramadan and Eid dates are lunar and shift each year; the window given is approximate.
  • Accessibility notes throughout are drawn from written accounts, not certifications. If either of you uses a mobility aid, ring the specific riad, hotel or restaurant — Moroccan hosts are used to the question and will tell you honestly.
