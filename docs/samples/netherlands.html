the country

Amsterdam to Haarlem to the bulb fields to Leiden — nine nights down a thirty-mile ribbon of colour, timed to the week the tulips break.

Nothing about the Dutch bulb country is subtle. You come over a low bridge somewhere south of Haarlem, the land flattens into polder, and then the fields begin — not drifts, not borders, but solid rectangles of single-colour bloom laid edge to edge like paint samples, running to a horizon interrupted only by a farmhouse or the stub of a windmill. Purple, then a strip of bare brown soil, then orange, then red. It is agriculture at the scale of a flag, and the effect from a bicycle saddle is close to hallucinatory.

This is de Bollenstreek — the bulb strip, roughly 30 km of sandy coastal soil between Leiden and Haarlem where the Netherlands grows its tulips, hyacinths and daffodils. For eight weeks a year it is the most saturated landscape in Europe, and then the growers cut the heads off and it goes back to being farmland. Your entire trip hangs on those eight weeks.

What follows is a route rather than a base: Amsterdam → Haarlem → Lisse → Leiden, nine nights, four beds, never more than 40 km of travel between them, finishing 17 minutes by train from Schiphol. It is built to put you inside Keukenhof at eight in the morning on a weekday and in an empty field road at golden hour the same evening — and to give you canal cities, hidden courtyard gardens, a 1590 botanical garden where the Dutch tulip industry literally began, and a Japanese garden that opens for six weeks a year, in between.

The one chart that decides your tripwhen the colour actually happens

Bulbs bloom in sequence, not together, and the window shifts a week or two either way with the spring temperature. Mid-April is the only period when the field tulips, Keukenhof at full tilt and the flower parade all coincide.

Read this before you book flights

You probably cannot have both. The Bloemencorso flower parade runs Saturday 17 April 2027. The Clingendael Japanese Garden and King's Day fall in the following ten days (≈23 April and 27 April). A nine-night trip catches one end or the other. Our dated itinerary below picks the parade, because it exists nowhere else on earth and cannot be rescheduled. If formal gardens and Dutch street festivity appeal more, shift the whole thing a week later. If you can stretch to twelve nights — arrive Friday 16 April, leave Wednesday 28 April — you get the parade, Clingendael's opening weekend and King's Day, and that is the completist's answer.

The shape of itfour beds, thirty miles

The whole route fits inside a rectangle about 40 km on its long side. Every leg is a short, frequent train — no car needed, though one buys you the field back-roads at dawn. Sleep in four places rather than day-tripping from Amsterdam: the bulb fields are best at hours when the coaches from the capital have not yet arrived or have already left.

Shorter trip?

Five nights: drop Amsterdam to one night and Leiden to one, keep three in Lisse — the bulb fields are the trip. Seven nights: cut Haarlem to one night. Do not cut the Bollenstreek below three nights: one day is Keukenhof, one is the fields by bike, and one absorbs weather. Dutch April will take a day off you.

What you actually need to knowthe practical floor

Weather. April runs 9–14 °C, May 11–18 °C, with roughly twelve wet days a month. Showers are short and hard and the wind is constant across open polder — a hood beats an umbrella, which will simply invert. The compensation is the light: low, luminous, and long into the evening, which is exactly why Dutch painters were Dutch painters.

Trains. The whole route runs on NS intercity services, every 10–15 minutes, no reservations. Tap in and out with a contactless card. Amsterdam–Haarlem 15 min, Haarlem–Leiden 21 min (and that line runs through the bulb fields — sit on the east side), Leiden–Den Haag 15 min, Leiden–Schiphol 17 min.

Money. The Netherlands is aggressively cashless and, more awkwardly, some smaller places take Dutch debit (pin) but not foreign credit cards. Keukenhof is entirely cashless. Carry a backup card and about €40 in notes for market stalls.

Booking windows. Keukenhof timed-entry tickets go on sale the previous autumn and peak-April slots genuinely sell out — book the moment you fix dates. Anne Frank House releases tickets every Tuesday at 10:00 CEST exactly six weeks ahead and they vanish in hours. Parade grandstand seats and Bollenstreek hotels for parade weekend sell out months out. Restaurant dinner in the Netherlands runs early: kitchens are busiest 18:00–19:30 and a 20:30 table often means an emptying room.

Bicycles. Rent for the field days rather than the whole trip — roughly €10–19/day at Keukenhof, in Lisse, or from hotels in Leiden and the Bollenstreek. The field routes are entirely flat and fully paved.

Field etiquette. This matters and visitors get it wrong constantly. The fields are private working farmland and a trampled row is destroyed bulb stock. Stay on the roadside verge. Never pick. If you want to stand among tulips, that is what the ticketed walk-in farms are for.

02 — Dates that are worth building a trip around

four days in april

Saturday 17 April 2027 · the 80th editionbloemencorso bollenstreek

There are flower parades all over the world. There is only one built out of spring bulbs — tulips, hyacinths and daffodils pressed in one at a time, by hand, by several hundred volunteers over three days — which means it can only exist for one Saturday a year, in this thirty-kilometre strip of the Netherlands, and nowhere else, ever.

Twenty monumental praalwagens and thirty-odd flower-covered cars leave the seafront at Noordwijk at half past nine in the morning and travel 42 km inland through Voorhout, Sassenheim, Lisse — passing directly along the Keukenhof boulevard mid-afternoon — then Hillegom, Bennebroek and Heemstede, reaching Haarlem's Grote Markt around nine at night. More than a million people stand along the route. UNESCO lists the tradition as intangible cultural heritage.

The counter-intuitive advice: the parade is not the best part. Watching a float pass at walking pace for ninety seconds is thrilling for ninety seconds. The two better experiences bracket it — the free illuminated night parade through the village of Noordwijkerhout on the Friday evening, where the floats are lit from inside and you can stand close enough to see individual hyacinth florets; and the Sunday float expo, when everything stands motionless on Haarlem's Grote Markt until about five in the afternoon and you can walk around each one at your own pace. Our itinerary is built to give you all three.

Practical impact — plan for it

Parade Saturday is the single busiest day of the year in the Bollenstreek. Roads along the whole 42 km close for hours (in Lisse, roughly midday to 17:00), parking and buses reach capacity early, and Keukenhof's access roads shut mid-afternoon — if you want the park that day, be inside before noon or don't go. Covered grandstands at Lisse and Sassenheim and open stands at Voorhout, Hillegom and Noordwijk sell out months ahead. Bollenstreek hotels for the Friday and Saturday night go even earlier. Also in the windowthree more worth timing for — or around

  • Koningsdag · King's Day

    Tue 27 April 2027 · nationwide · free

    The whole country turns orange for the King's birthday, and it is less a parade than a national yard sale: every neighbourhood holds a vrijmarkt where anyone may sell anything from a blanket on the pavement, canals fill with decorated boats, and stages appear on the squares. In The Hague the Lange Voorhout hosts a Geranium Market selling potted plants and perennials along one of the finest lime-tree avenues in the country — the most garden-ish King's Day going. Expect closures: most shops and many museums shut or run short hours, and trains are crushed. Walk or cycle.

    I amsterdam · denhaag.com

  • Tulp Festival Amsterdam

    1–30 April 2027 · 13th edition · free

    For the whole of April, roughly 85 locations across central Amsterdam — bridges, squares, shopping streets, neighbourhood gardens and museum forecourts — are planted with curated tulip displays. No ticket, no excursion, no queue: if your Amsterdam days fall in April it is simply there, on the way to everything else. The Rijksmuseum gardens (free to enter) and the floating tulip installation on the Vondelpark pond are the two set-pieces. A self-guided route map is published each year.

    I amsterdam

  • Bevrijdingsdag · Liberation Day

    Wed 5 May 2027 · Haarlem & nationwide · free

    Liberation Day, preceded on the evening of 4 May by a national two minutes' silence at 20:00 — a genuinely striking thing to be present for, when an entire country stops. Haarlem then hosts Bevrijdingspop in the Haarlemmerhout, the oldest public park in the Netherlands and the largest free Liberation Day festival in the country: multiple stages, all day, no ticket. Relevant only if your trip runs into May — by which point the field tulips are past and the late varieties are carrying the show.

    Leg 01 — 2 nights · 16 minutes from Schiphol

    amsterdam

    Two nights, deliberately. Amsterdam in April is at its absolute best and also its most expensive and most crowded — and it is not where the flowers are. Treat it as a beautiful decompression chamber: land, walk the canal belt in the long spring light, eat extremely well, see the two or three things that are genuinely unmissable, and get out to the fields.

    What Amsterdam does have, for a garden tour specifically, is a 1638 botanical garden, the only floating flower market on earth, and — for the whole of April — its own citywide tulip festival, so that the flowers find you between museums rather than the other way around.

    See & do · the floral citygardens, bulbs and a market on barges

    Do this in one loop: the Hortus in the Plantage at opening, tram west to Museumplein for the Rijksmuseum gardens, then Vondelpark, then the Bloemenmarkt on the way back into the centre for the evening. That is an entire day of flowers without leaving the tram network.

    See & do · water and brickthe canal belt, and where to stand in it

    The Grachtengordel is UNESCO-listed and genuinely does reward aimless walking, but it also rewards knowing exactly where to stand. The five spots below are the compositions everybody who photographs this city goes to, and in April the water is still and the trees are in first leaf. Go at blue hour — the half hour after sunset, when the window lights come on warm against a deep blue sky and the canal doubles everything.

    Between them, wander the Jordaan and the Negen Straatjes (Nine Streets), the dense grid of little cross-streets threading the canal ring — vintage shops, cheesemongers, coffee roasters, brown cafés — and drop into the Begijnhof, a medieval almshouse courtyard hidden behind a low wooden gate off the Spui. It is the first of the hofjes you will meet on this trip and a quiet rehearsal for Haarlem and Leiden, which have dozens.

    A boat is worth it. Group canal cruises run about €15–25 each; a small private open boat — the better option for two — starts around €60 an hour and in spring often comes with wine and flowers on board.

    See & do · the unmissable threeif you only book one thing, book this

    All three are online-only, timed-entry, and sell out. The Anne Frank House is the hardest ticket in the Netherlands: it releases every Tuesday at 10:00 CEST, exactly six weeks ahead, and is gone within hours. Set an alarm the day your six-week window opens.

    Eat & drinkwhite gold, brown cafés and one glass greenhouse

    Two things govern eating in the Netherlands in April, and both are good news. The first is that you have landed in aspergeseizoen. Witte asperges — "white gold" — are grown entirely underground so no light reaches them, hand-cut the moment the tip breaks the soil, and treated by the Dutch with total orthodoxy: boiled whole and soft (do not expect al dente; soft is correct), with drawn butter or hollandaise, sliced ham and a halved boiled egg. Every decent kitchen runs an aspergemenu from mid-April. It is the single most seasonal thing you can order.

    The second is borrel, the drinks hour that is genuinely the load-bearing social occasion of Dutch life. From about five, offices empty into the nearest bruine kroeg — brown café, so named for the dark wood or the centuries of tobacco in the walls — for a small beer or a jenever (the juniper spirit British gin descends from) and a plate of bitterballen: molten beef ragout in a crisp breadcrumb sphere, always with mustard, always hotter inside than you expect. Nobody books. You stand.

    The Dutch clock — get this right and everything is easier

    Ontbijt 7–9, bread and cheese, cold, quick. Koffietijd 10–11 and again 15–16, coffee with exactly one biscuit. Lunch 12–13:30 and it is a sandwich — the Dutch working lunch is bread, and a long restaurant lunch is a tourist habit. Borrel 17–19. Avondeten 18:00–19:30 — dinner is early and this is the one that catches visitors out. Book a table at 19:00, not 20:30, or you will eat in an emptying room. And a footnote worth knowing: Hollandse Nieuwe herring is not in season yet. The new catch lands mid-May to mid-June; in April the stalls sell maatjes, last season's matured cure — excellent, but not the thing the whole country queues for.

    The one booking to make in advance: De Kas, which is a restaurant inside a 1926 municipal greenhouse in Frankendael Park, with its own nursery and a "harvested in the morning, on your plate in the afternoon" policy. On a garden tour it is almost too on-the-nose, and it is also very good. One to two weeks ahead.

    Sleeptwo nights in a canal house

    Base yourself in the Jordaan or the inner Canal Belt and nowhere else. Jordaan is the narrower, more residential, more village-like of the two — bakers opening shutters, cats in windows, quiet again by evening. The Canal Belt (Herengracht, Keizersgracht, Singel) is grander and slightly more central, with the museums and the Bloemenmarkt within walking distance. Both are 10–20 minutes on foot or by tram from Centraal.

    The genuine local form is the canal-house hotel: several 17th-century merchant houses stitched together, which means passages, half-landings and no two rooms alike. It also means steep stairs and often no lift — ask before booking if that matters. Note that the Netherlands applies 21% VAT on rooms and Amsterdam adds 12.5% city tax, usually billed at check-in, so headline rates understate the total by roughly a third.     The drive to Haarlem

    Fifteen minutes, every ten minutes, about €5–7. You are barely out of Amsterdam before the buildings stop and the land opens out flat and green. Bring nothing but your bags: you will be in Haarlem's Grote Markt ten minutes after you step off the platform.

    Leg 02 — 2 nights · 15 minutes from Amsterdam

    haarlem

    Haarlem is what people mean when they say they wish they had seen Amsterdam thirty years ago: the same gabled brick and dark water and cobbles, a Gothic cathedral the size of a small mountain, a working windmill on the river — and about a tenth of the crowd. It is also the capital of the bulb region and the flower parade's finish line, so in April the city is dressed for it.

    For a garden tour it has one thing nowhere else in the Netherlands has in this concentration: hofjes. Haarlem has more almshouse courtyards per head than any other Dutch city — roughly twenty-two of them, mostly 14th to 17th century, and here is the thing: they are hidden. You walk down an ordinary shopping street, push a door that looks entirely residential, and step into a silent walled garden ringed by tiny houses that people still live in. It is the most quietly astonishing garden experience in the country and almost nobody does it.

    See & do · the hidden gardenshofjes: how to actually find them

    Rules first, because these are private homes and the residents' tolerance is what keeps them open. Weekday mornings only — most close at weekends and on public holidays, and open roughly 10:00–17:00. Keep your voice down, do not photograph into windows, do not go beyond the courtyard. Pick up the hofjes map at the tourist office in the Town Hall on the Grote Markt; four have confirmed street addresses and the map fills in the rest.

    The Hofje van Bakenes (1395, off Wijde Appelaarsteeg) is the oldest in the Netherlands. Hofje van Oorschot on Kruisstraat you can see straight through the gate from the main shopping street. Teylers Hofje on Koudenhorn is the grandest. And the Frans Hals Museum is itself a 17th-century almshouse built around its own hofje garden, so you get the courtyard and the world's largest collection of Hals in one ticket — including the enormous civic-guard group portraits, which are the reason people come.

    See & do · the old towna square, an organ, a windmill, a river

    Everything is within fifteen minutes' walk. The Grote Markt is the pivot — ten streets converge on it in front of the vast Gothic bulk of Sint-Bavokerk, whose floor is a mosaic of engraved gravestones (Frans Hals is under one of them) and whose 1738 Christian Müller organ was played by Handel and by Mozart at ten years old. On Saturday the whole square fills with a produce, cheese, fish and flower market from 08:30, and in April the flower stalls are stacked with tulip bunches — arrive before ten and build a picnic.

    Then walk east to the Spaarne, the river that made the city rich, past the golden-starred Gravenstenenbrug drawbridge to Molen De Adriaan on the far bank — and don't skip Teylers, the oldest museum in the country (1784) and one of the most intact 18th-century interiors in Europe: a domed Oval Hall of fossils, minerals and brass instruments in their original cases. If you want green air instead, the beach and the protected dune reserve at Zandvoort are twenty minutes by train.

    See & do · past the ringa city gate, a green dome, and the sea

    Three things just outside the medieval core, all worth the ten or twenty minutes it takes to reach them — and all far quieter than the square.

    Eat & drinkbeer brewed in a church, gin poured in a fishmonger's

    Haarlem was one of the great brewing cities of the Low Countries — by the 15th century it was shipping jopen (112-litre barrels) of ale down the Spaarne. The last old Haarlem brewery closed in 1916; in 1994 Jopen revived the tradition from 14th- and 15th-century recipes found in the city archive, and now brews inside a deconsecrated Gothic church, copper kettles under stained glass. Order the Koyt — a medieval ale bittered with herbs instead of hops, earthy and genuinely strange.

    The other local institution is the proeflokaal, a jenever tasting room. In den Uiver has occupied a former fishmonger's on the Riviervischmarkt since 1981 and keeps eighteen jenevers. The form: order a small tulip-shaped glass of oude jenever (aged, mellow), leave it on the bar, bend forward and take the first sip without lifting it. Then bitterballen.

    What to order in spring

    Witte asperges — peak season, on every chalkboard from mid-April. Stamppot with rookworst — mashed potato beaten with kale, sauerkraut or endive; a cold-weather dish that lingers on spring menus and is exactly right after a windy day in the fields. Rijsttafel — the Dutch-Indonesian "rice table" of fifteen-plus small dishes around a mound of rice, a colonial-era invention now recognised as Dutch cultural heritage; Haarlem has one of the oldest dedicated rijsttafel restaurants in the country. Frites with oorlog ("war": mayonnaise, peanut sauce and raw onion). And a fresh stroopwafel from a market griddle, rested on your coffee cup until the caramel gives.

    The drive to Lisse

    Thirty-five minutes and the whole character of the trip changes. Take the seasonal Keukenhof bus straight from Houtplein, or the train to Sassenheim and a short hop. Either way the buildings thin, the land flattens, and then — quite suddenly, usually somewhere around Bennebroek — the first field appears on your left as a solid block of colour and you understand what the fuss is about.

    Leg 03 — 3 nights · the reason you came

    de bollenstreek

    Thirty thousand hectares of bulbs, thirty kilometres of sandy coastal soil, eight weeks a year. This is the part of the trip that cannot be moved, substituted or done badly — and the part where what time of day you go matters more than what you go to see.

    Three nights in Lisse buys you the two things day-trippers from Amsterdam can never have: the eight o'clock gate at Keukenhof before the coaches land, and an empty field road at seven in the evening when the light comes in almost horizontally along the rows. Everything else here is a bonus.

    One agricultural fact, so it doesn't shock you

    Dutch growers cut the flower heads off at peak bloom. The flower is not the crop — the bulb is — and letting a tulip set seed degrades it. So at exactly the moment a field is most spectacular, a machine may come through and behead the lot, and you will see rows of decapitated stems and loose heads blowing across the road like confetti. It is normal, it is the whole economic logic of the place, and it is also why you should not save the fields for the end of the week. Go the day you arrive.

    The one unmissable thingkeukenhof

    Keukenhof is the curated face of the Dutch flower industry — 32 hectares, seven million bulbs replanted every single year, over 800 tulip varieties, designed like an English landscape park with meandering water, mature trees and stepping stones across a pond. It is the counterpart to the raw commercial fields outside, not a substitute for them: the park shows you what a tulip can be, the fields show you what a tulip is.

    How to do it properly. Book the earliest timed slot and be at the gate at 08:00 on a weekday. Walk directly to the 1892 windmill at the north end — everyone else heads for the pavilions first — and climb the platform for the panorama over the park canopy and the fields beyond. Then the Oranje Nassau pond while the water is still glass and the reflections double the beds. Save the indoor pavilions for mid-morning when the crowds move outside; the themed shows under diffuse skylight are the best macro conditions in the park and the rain contingency. Then the whisper-boat — 45 minutes on a silent electric launch through the working fields north of the park, water-level, a view you cannot get from a bike. Tickets are sold at the windmill on the day, not online.

    Come back out at four in the afternoon when the buses leave, and the western beds get an hour of low gold. Fifteen kilometres of paths; most people spend three to five hours; you could spend the day.

    The other half — and the better halfthe fields themselves

    The fields are free, they are everywhere, and they are the thing you will actually remember. They are also working farmland: the rule is stay on the verge, never step between rows, never pick. A trampled row is destroyed stock, many fields are now fenced because of exactly this, and the growers' patience is finite.

    The named roads are Het Laantje and Gerleeweg near Lisse, Loosterweg Zuid, the lanes around Voorhout, and the corner of Nieuwe Bennebroekseweg near Heemstede — but honestly, drive or ride inland from Noordwijk in any direction during peak season and the fields find you. Because growers rotate crops, the field that was purple last year will not be purple this year: check the weekly flower map at bollenstreek.nl or bloemenradar.nl the night before, which is what the local landscape photographers do.

    The proper way to see them is the free, self-guided Tulip Bike Tour — a 10 km half-day loop or a 25 km full day on paved paths and quiet lanes, starting from Keukenhof, Intratuin Lisse or the town centre. A €5.99 audio guide covers the 1637 tulip mania and the practice of topping the fields at twelve stops. And if you want to stand inside the flowers, that is what the ticketed walk-in farms exist for — go to those instead of trespassing.

    Where you're allowed inside the flowersthe walk-in farms, and one museum

    These are the legitimate answer to the urge to stand in a tulip field. All are ticketed, all keep you on paths that don't damage stock, and all book out in peak weeks — reserve before you arrive. Two are working farms where the person explaining the flowers grew them.

    Day trip · 25 minutes north · start at 07:00the biggest flower market on earth

    If Keukenhof is the flowers as art, Royal FloraHolland at Aalsmeer is the flowers as industry, and the contrast is the most interesting thing you will see all week. It is the largest flower and plant marketplace in the world: 775,000 square metres, an 18 km conveyor network, 43 million stems traded every weekday, over 100,000 transactions a day.

    You walk it from a glass-sided gallery bridge suspended above the logistics floor — a space so large the far end disappears — watching motorised trolley trains stream underneath in choreographed lines, sorted by variety, grower, buyer and destination airport. On screens, the descending auction clock: the Dutch auction, where the price falls until someone stops it. Flowers cut in Kenya yesterday will be in a Frankfurt shop tomorrow, and they pass beneath your feet on the way.

    This is an early morning or it is nothing. Trading peaks 07:00–09:00 and the floor visibly empties by ten. The visitor centre opens at 07:00 Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday and Friday, and only until 09:00 on Thursdays. It is closed weekends and Dutch public holidays — including King's Day and Liberation Day — plus a dozen designated non-auction days a year, so check the calendar before you commit a morning. Tickets online in advance, about €9.50. Photography is allowed from the walkway; no tripods, and absolutely no drones.

    Then make a day of it: the Historische Tuin is five minutes' walk away — a living museum of Aalsmeer horticulture from 1700 to 1960, with working demonstration plots tended by volunteers and a terrace for lunch — and it is the dock for the Westeinder boat tour, an hour through a lake landscape of working nursery islands that has barely changed in structure since the 17th century. In May there is a Seringen (lilac) variant that includes a visit to a grower; Aalsmeer built its fortune on lilacs.

    Eat & drinkwhite gold from the soil that grows the bulbs

    The convergence here is real, not marketing: the same sandy, well-drained coastal soil that forces bulbs to bloom early also grows the best white asparagus in the Netherlands, and the North Sea at the western edge has fed Noordwijk and Katwijk for centuries. Come in the second half of April and the fields are in flower, the asparagus harvest is at full yield, and the roadside boerderijwinkels are stacked with local strawberries and farmhouse cheese. Buy asparagus from a farm stall the same day it was cut.

    Otherwise this is unshowy country cooking with two poles. Inland: the pannenkoekenboerderij — pancake farmhouse — where you eat a plate-sized pannenkoek with bacon and syrup in a working farm building with the dunes through the window. On the coast: the strandpaviljoen, a beach pavilion on the Noordwijk boulevard, for kibbeling (battered North Sea fish in a paper cone with garlic sauce) and a beer with the wind off the sea. Between them, Noordwijk holds two Michelin stars' worth of serious cooking — book those four to six weeks out for a Saturday in Keukenhof season.

    Eating around the crowds

    Restaurants on the N208 road to the Keukenhof gates are overwhelmed at lunchtime on busy weekends. Eat before 12:30 or after 14:30, or walk into Lisse village centre where De Engel and Funky Moose take walk-ins. Keukenhof's own food stalls do stroopwafels, poffertjes and filled rolls — fine between flower beds, not a meal. And on parade Saturday the route roads close and everything near them is packed or shut: book dinner for the Friday or the Sunday instead.

    The drive to Leiden

    Twenty minutes, and the last of it is the best rail journey in the country in April: the Haarlem–Leiden line runs straight through the bulb fields. Ninety-two trains a day, about €6.90, no reservation — just sit on the east side and watch the colour bands strobe past the window.

    Leg 04 — 2 nights · 17 minutes from Schiphol

    leiden & den haag

    You have saved the origin story for last. In the 1590s the Flemish botanist Carolus Clusius, newly appointed to the two-year-old botanical garden of Leiden University, planted tulip bulbs that had come west from the Ottoman court. They flowered. Growers copied him. Within forty years the country had talked itself into the first speculative bubble in recorded financial history and out the other side, and the industry you have spent a week driving through was underway. The garden is still there, still open, a five-minute walk from the canal you will be eating dinner beside.

    Leiden is also, simply, a lovely place to end: 28 km of canals, 2,800 monuments, thirty-five courtyard gardens, the oldest university in the Netherlands, Rembrandt's birthplace, and a broad tree-lined canal — the Rapenburg — that is regularly called the most beautiful in the country. Den Haag and Delft are 15 minutes south. Schiphol is 17 minutes north.

    The garden the whole trip descends fromhortus botanicus leiden

    See & do · the canal citya fortress mound, 35 courtyards, and poems on the walls

    Leiden is walkable end to end in twenty minutes and best approached without a plan. Start on the Rapenburg, a broad straight ribbon of water lined with patrician houses, the university's Academy Building and the National Museum of Antiquities — whose atrium contains an actual 1st-century BC Nubian temple, the Temple of Taffeh, given to the Netherlands by Egypt and re-erected indoors. At the southern end is the late-Gothic Pieterskerk, where the Pilgrim Fathers worshipped before sailing for America.

    Then climb De Burcht, a 10th-century circular shell keep on an artificial mound at the confluence of the Old and New Rhine — free, five minutes up, and a full 360° over the rooftops and waterways, with two café terraces at the bottom for afterwards. Leiden's hofjes are its quiet rival to Haarlem's: thirty-five of them, seventeen open to respectful visitors, and there is an official 5 km self-guided Hofjes Walk with a €6.95 map from the tourist office at Stationsweg 2d. Look out too for the wall poems — since 1992 the city has been painting large-format poems onto gable ends, over 120 of them now, in Dutch, English, Arabic, Hebrew and a dozen other languages.

    For one more ride through the fields without the Keukenhof crowds, the Singelpark landscape route is a 30 km loop leaving straight from the city centre through meadow-bird polder, a small ferry crossing of the Kagerplassen lakes, and the bulb fields around Warmond.

    See & do · green Leidencourtyards, poems and polder

    The softer half of the city: thirty-five hidden gardens, a hundred and twenty poems painted on walls, and a flat 30 km loop out through the meadow-bird polder and the bulb fields around Warmond when you want one more ride.

    Day trip · 15 minutes southden haag: a japanese garden that opens for six weeks

    The single best garden in the southern Netherlands is free, and almost nobody outside the country has heard of it, because it is only open for about six weeks a year.

    The Japanese Garden at Clingendael is the only early-20th-century Japanese garden in the Netherlands. The then-owner of the Clingendael estate, Marguérite van Brienen, had stone lanterns, water basins, bridges and an entire pavilion shipped back from Japan and built the garden around them. It is small, dense and extraordinarily intact — winding paths under a continuous blanket of moss, koi ponds, arched bridges — and because the moss and the plantings cannot survive year-round footfall, the gates open only for a short spring window, roughly late April to early June, and briefly again in autumn. Spring is when the azaleas, rhododendrons and Japanese cherries are out. Entry is free, no reservations exist, and there are no dogs, strollers or mobility scooters. Go at the 10:00 opening on a weekday; overcast light suits it far better than sun.

    The surrounding Clingendael estate — meadows, ponds, woodland, rhododendrons — is freely open all year and worth an hour either side. Getting there: bus 18, 20, 24 or 29 from Den Haag Centraal, then a 13-minute walk; or a genuinely pleasant 40-minute walk out through the Haagse Bos.

    Timing warning — check this against your dates

    In the most recent season Clingendael opened on 23 April. Our parade-anchored itinerary reaches Den Haag on 22 April — one day early. If you follow those dates exactly, substitute Delft and the TU Delft botanical greenhouses for the Japanese garden, or shift the whole trip later and trade the parade for the garden. Confirm the 2027 window at denhaag.nl before you fix flights — it is announced each spring.

    The rest of the city rewards half a day. The Mauritshuis is a small 17th-century palace on the court pond holding Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring, Rembrandt's Anatomy Lesson and Fabritius's The Goldfinch — two or three hours, book a slot. Outside, the Hofvijver reflects the medieval towers of the Binnenhof (whose inner courtyard is closed for renovation until around 2028 — the exterior and the free information centre are still worth the walk). The Lange Voorhout is the most elegant avenue in the country, a lime-tree promenade of embassies and mansions, and the working Paleistuin at Noordeinde Palace is free and open daily. Then tram out to Scheveningen for the pier and 4.5 km of North Sea sand — in April it is bracing and empty, which is the point.

    Day trip · or instead of Den Haagdelft, in fifteen minutes

    Delft is the most intact 17th-century city centre in the Netherlands and looks very much as it did when Vermeer painted View of Delft from the Hooikade. It makes a full and completely different day: the Markt between the leaning Gothic tower of the Nieuwe Kerk (William of Orange's mausoleum, and the crypt of the Dutch royal family) and the Renaissance city hall; the Oostpoort, the last surviving medieval water gate, twin-spired and reflected in its canal; and Royal Delft, the last 17th-century Delft Blue factory still working, founded 1653, where you walk through the museum rooms and then into the factory to watch the cobalt painted on by hand. For a garden tour, add the TU Delft botanical garden — a genuinely hidden greenhouse complex of tropical specimens, and the best macro photography in the region.

    Eat & drinkcumin cheese, a stew with a founding myth, and the Indies kitchen

    Leiden is the only city in the world where a stew is a founding myth. When the Spanish siege broke in 1574, a boy is said to have found an abandoned Spanish cooking pot of hutspot — carrots, onions, parsnips and meat — at the city ramparts, and the starving population was then fed herring and white bread by the relieving fleet. Every 3 October the city still hands out herring and white bread in the streets. It is a cold-weather dish and you may have to ask for it in April, but ask.

    The everyday thing to eat is the cheese. Leidse kaas (komijnekaas) is made from partly skimmed milk with whole cumin seeds pressed into the curd as a concentrated seam rather than mixed through, so each slice carries a stripe of spice; the wheel is rubbed with annatto for a brick-red rind and stamped with Leiden's crossed keys. Aged, it goes hard, granular and almost peppery. Buy it by the wedge at the Wednesday or Saturday market along the Nieuwe Rijn, 08:00–17:00 — which is also where the May strawberries, the asparagus stalls and the fresh stroopwafel griddles are, and which is comfortably the best food experience in the city.

    Den Haag adds the other great strand of Dutch food: the Indies kitchen. The city has the country's largest Indonesian-Dutch community and its toko restaurants are the real thing — a full rijsttafel here is a different proposition from the tourist version. Book it; the good ones fill weeks ahead at weekends.

    05 — Worth the detour, if you have the day

    four extensions

    Things that did not make the core nine nights but are genuinely good, in descending order of how much we'd fight for them. The first one is the only place in the country where you can have kilometres of tulips entirely to yourself.

    Add a day from Lisse — or swap it for one of your Bollenstreek afternoons

    goeree-overflakkee: the fields nobody goes to

    Car essential · ~1 hr south of Lisse via the A29 · a half day, best at dawn

    The island of Goeree-Overflakkee, south of Rotterdam, grows roughly ten percent of all the tulips in the Netherlands and is almost entirely unknown to international visitors. The difference from the Bollenstreek is scale and solitude: fields here run for hundreds of metres without interruption, unfenced, punctuated by wind-turbine parks on the flat horizon with the water of the Haringvliet visible at the edges. You can drive these roads on a weekday morning and have entire kilometres of colour to yourself — which, after the Keukenhof car park, is a genuinely different emotional experience.

    Aim for the area around Middelharnis, Nieuwe-Tonge, Oude-Tonge and Dirksland and simply drive toward any of them; the fields reveal themselves. Sunrise to nine in the morning, mid-April. It is also the one part of the tulip region where drone flying is realistically possible — registration with the Dutch authority (RDW) is required, EU Open Category rules apply, and you still need the grower's permission to fly low over a field.

    💡 If you only have one dawn to spend, spend it here rather than on the Lisse field roads — the Bollenstreek is better in the evening anyway, when the low sun rakes along the rows and the day-trippers have gone.
    Half a day from Amsterdam or Haarlem

    zaanse schans: six working windmills

    Train to Zaandam ~15–25 min, then bus 391 · grounds free, mills ticketed · half day

    An open-air village on the river Zaan where genuine 17th- to 19th-century windmills, workshops and merchant houses were relocated from across the region and set working again. Six mills turn: De Huisman grinds spices and mustard, De Kat (1664) still grinds pigment for artists' paint, De Zoeker presses oil, and you can climb Het Jonge Schaap to watch the saw mechanism from inside. There are clog carvers, a cooper, a distillery and a chocolate tasting room, plus the Zaans Museum.

    It is unashamedly a tourist site and heaving at weekends — but on a weekday morning, with light coming across the water and the sails actually turning, it is the postcard Netherlands delivered honestly. Go early, and shoot the mill row with a long lens from the far bank of the Zaan to compress them together.

    A Friday morning from Haarlem — non-floral relief

    alkmaar: the cheese carriers

    Direct train from Haarlem ~30–35 min · free to watch · Fridays 10:00–13:00, late Mar–late Sep

    Every Friday morning the Waagplein in Alkmaar stages the oldest surviving cheese market in the country, and it is far stranger and better than the phrase suggests. Cheese carriers in white uniforms and coloured lacquered hats move 130 kg of Gouda wheels across the square on wooden barrows at a distinctive trotting run, while traders settle prices by handjeklap — a ritual of slapping palms together, one clap per bid, until a hand stays down. The bell goes at ten. Arrive by 09:45 for a viewpoint; a large screen helps when the square is packed.

    Afterwards Alkmaar has 400-odd national monuments and a medieval canal system that rewards an hour's wander, and the market winds up by one, which leaves the afternoon free.

    Half a day from Amsterdam — only if you come in late March

    the blossom park at amstelveen

    Bus or 30 min by bike from central Amsterdam · free · half day with Museum Cobra

    In the Amsterdamse Bos — a municipal forest more than three times the size of Central Park — sits the Bloesempark, a grove of 400 Japanese cherry trees given by the Japan Women's Club in 2000, each one named after a Dutch or Japanese woman. Peak bloom is mid-to-late March, which is earlier than this trip: by mid-April you will get the rhododendrons ringing the grove rather than the cherries, which is pleasant but not a reason to travel. Worth knowing about only if your dates slide early — and worth checking first, because the park is replacing all 400 trees in stages between 2025 and 2029 and sections may be fenced.

    Pair it with Museum Cobra ten minutes away in Amstelveen, the definitive collection of the post-war CoBrA movement — Appel, Constant, Corneille, Jorn — all raw colour and child-like figuration in a purpose-built skylit building. Closed Mondays and on King's Day.

    Before you book — things to reconfirm

    This guide was written ahead of the 2027 season, and a handful of numbers move every year. Specifically: Keukenhof's 2027 ticket prices (the figures here are the previous season's) and its confirmed 18 March – 9 May window; the Bloemencorso's exact 2027 timings and grandstand booking, and the build-day and illuminated-parade dates, which follow the recent pattern here; the Clingendael Japanese Garden's 2027 opening window, announced each spring and the single most likely thing to catch you out; the seasonal Keukenhof bus numbers from Haarlem and Leiden, which change year to year (the old Schiphol 858 was replaced by a dedicated shuttle in 2026); Royal FloraHolland's non-auction days; and hotel rates, which are indicative April figures rather than live quotes and exclude 21% Dutch VAT plus city tax.

    Two seasonal notes worth repeating: Hollandse Nieuwe herring is not in season in April or early May — the new catch lands mid-May to mid-June, and until then the stalls sell matured maatjes. And white asparagus starts thin and expensive in early April, hitting full yield and better prices from late April.
