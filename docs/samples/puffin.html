Iceland · puffin season · 7–8 days

I want to
see a puffin

So go where sixty per cent of the world's Atlantic puffins come ashore — and go in the four months a year they are actually there.

The window
is the whole trip

A puffin spends roughly eight months of the year on the open North Atlantic, sleeping on the swell, never touching land. It comes ashore only to breed. Which means the single most important decision of this trip is not where you go but when — and the answer is a window about four months wide that slams shut in the middle of August.

Iceland holds an estimated 3 million breeding pairs — around 60% of the world's Atlantic puffins. They start arriving at the cliffs in late April, are at full density through June and July, and are gone by mid-to-late August. Between those dates the birds are absurdly easy to find. Outside them there is not a single puffin on an Icelandic cliff, and no amount of driving will produce one.

The other thing that shapes every hour of the trip is the light. In June and July the sun barely sets — over 22 hours of usable daylight, and a golden hour that stretches from roughly 11pm to 1am. That matters practically, not poetically: puffins spend the middle of the day out at sea fishing and return to their burrows in the evening. The best puffin hour and the best light hour are the same hour, and almost nobody else is there for it. The trade-off is absolute — there are no northern lights in puffin season. Pick one.

The state of the season, right now

It is late July 2026, which puts you near the end of this year's window and inside its most frantic stretch: adults are shuttling constantly with beakfuls of sand eels to feed chicks in the burrows. You have roughly three more weeks of reliable adult birds. Two honest options:

Go now (early August). Birds are still on the cliffs, and you arrive in time for Þjóðhátíð and the very start of the pysja season. The catch is that Þjóðhátíð books out Heimaey months ahead — see Special days.

Or aim at mid-June to mid-July 2027. Peak density, longest light, full access at every colony, and you can book the ferry and the good rooms properly instead of scrambling. If you have any flexibility at all, this is the better trip.

Know before you book

  • Rent a car. Every good puffin cliff is a self-drive proposition. All of Route 1 and the South Coast is paved; a 2WD is fine in summer. No F-roads needed for anything in this guide.
  • Download PARKA before you leave Reykjavík — it handles paid parking at Dyrhólaey, Skaftafell and Jökulsárlón, where the mobile signal is patchy.
  • Book the Herjólfur ferry direct at herjolfur.is, not through resellers. Full refund up to 24 hours out; changes ~500 ISK.
  • Iceland is expensive. Budget 3,500–5,000 ISK for a restaurant lunch and 8,000–15,000 ISK for a sit-down dinner. The dagsrétt lunch special (2,000–2,800 ISK) is how locals afford to eat out.
  • Museums close early — typically 5pm. Do indoor things in the morning; save the cliffs for the long evening.
  • Check road.is and vedur.is every morning. South Coast weather has nothing to do with Reykjavík's.

What to pack

Fratercula arctica · Icelandic: lundi · plural lundar

Meet the lundi

A puffin is smaller than you expect — 28 to 39 cm, about the size of a pint glass with opinions. The famous bill is a seasonal costume: it swells and turns coral-and-gold for breeding, then dulls and shrinks once the bird goes back to sea. Everything in this guide is timed around that costume.

The year, and where you are in it

Puffins are creatures of ferocious routine. They return to the same burrow, often with the same mate, lay exactly one egg, and raise exactly one chick. Once the chick fledges, the whole colony empties within days — the cliffs that held a million birds in July are silent by September.

How to behave at a colony

  • Stay on the path, behind the ropes. This is not about the birds' nerves — puffin burrows are dug into the clifftop turf, and a footstep collapses the tunnel and kills the chick inside.
  • Never stand on an undercut edge. At Látrabjarg the turf overhangs the cliff face and is hollowed out by the burrows themselves. Sit, then lie prone to look over. There has been a fatality.
  • Let them come to you. Move slowly, crouch, don't stare directly, don't crowd a burrow entrance. A bird that walks away is telling you something.
  • No flash, no drones. Drone flight over nesting seabird colonies is prohibited.
  • No dogs — and they're banned outright inside the Dyrhólaey reserve during nesting.
On eating lundi Puffin appears on menus in Reykjavík and on Heimaey — smoked, seared rare, occasionally raw — and it is a genuine four-century-old island tradition, not a tourist invention. It is also increasingly hard to defend: the Atlantic puffin is listed as Vulnerable, and Dr Erpur S. Hansen of the South Iceland Nature Research Centre states flatly that "the puffin harvest in Iceland hasn't been biologically sustainable for decades." Eater's Reykjavík guide tells readers to skip it. We agree — read about it, ask about it, and order the humarsúpa instead.

The day, and the one hour that matters

This is the single most useful thing in this guide, and the mistake almost every visitor makes: they drive to a famous colony at two in the afternoon and find an empty cliff. One first-hand account records fewer than five puffins at Látrabjarg at 1pm; another found thousands at Hafnarhólmi at 6pm the same season. The birds are not gone. They are fishing.

Shooting them

  • From a boat: 300–500mm, 1/1000s minimum for the boat's movement, f/5.6–8, continuous AF, burst. Boats don't land, so reach is everything.
  • On land: 70–200mm is genuinely enough — at Látrabjarg one photographer worked at 200mm from three metres. Lie prone near the barrier and let the birds habituate to you.
  • In flight: 1/1600–1/2000s. Puffins fly at around 55 mph and beat their wings absurdly fast.
  • Exposure: they are black-and-white birds — expose to protect the white breast, then lift the shadows. Never shoot them in flight against a white sky; find blue sky or the cliff face behind.
  • Light: low evening sun on the coral bill is the whole shot. Overcast flattens them.
  • ISO 1600–3200 is fine. Noise is recoverable; motion blur is not.

Days worth
timing for

Two of these are among the most singular things that happen anywhere in Iceland, and both fall inside the puffin window. One of them will decide whether you can get a bed on Heimaey at all.

Þjóðhátíð — the National Festival

Heimaey · 31 July – 3 August 2026 · the 152nd

Every year on the first long weekend of August, between ten and fifteen thousand Icelanders — more than triple the island's population — pour into Herjólfsdalur, a grassy volcanic crater on the west side of Heimaey, and camp in it. There are concerts every evening, a vast communal bonfire, flares fired into the hillside, and then the thing everyone actually comes for: Brekkusöngur, the slope-singing, when thousands of people sitting on the crater walls sing together in the small hours under a sky that never fully darkens.

It began in 1874, when Icelanders who wanted to attend the Copenhagen World Exhibition were stranded by weather and threw their own celebration instead. It is emphatically not a tourist music festival — people travel home from across the country for it, and visitors are simply welcome.

The planning consequence Cars are banned in the village during the festival. Ferry slots — vehicle slots especially — go months in advance, and the operator holds back festival-week capacity until a specific release date. Every hotel and guesthouse on the island sells out. If you want quiet puffin cliffs, come the week before or the week after. If you want the festival, book it now and accept that Stórhöfði will have company. Tickets at tix.is; programme at dalurinn.is.

Pysjueftirlitið — the Puffling Patrol

Heimaey · mid-August to mid-September · free · join in

For two or three weeks from around 15 August, Heimaey's puffin chicks — pysjur — leave their burrows for the first time and fly toward the brightest thing they can see. That is supposed to be moonlight on open water. Increasingly it is the streetlights of Vestmannaeyjabær, and every night hundreds of fledglings crash-land in the town's streets, gardens and car parks.

So the island goes out and collects them. Families, kids, grandparents, torches and cardboard boxes, working the streets after dark. The birds are checked in at the Sea Life Trust sanctuary in the morning, weighed and tagged, then carried to a clifftop and thrown gently out toward the sea — their wings work fine; they just need pointing the right way. One family interviewed by Atlas Obscura rescues 200–300 pufflings a season. "It's like Black Friday," they said. "And when the season ends, we start counting down to the next one."

This is not a performance staged for visitors — it is a generations-old community habit that tourists are welcome to join. Ask at the Sea Life Trust visitor centre for the best streets and how to handle a bird. Releases happen down at Klauf beach and other clifftops the following morning.

The timing trade-off The Patrol and peak adult viewing barely overlap. Come in June or July and you get a million adults on the cliffs but no pufflings in the streets. Come after 15 August and you get the Patrol, the first properly dark nights of the year — and mostly empty cliffs, because the adults have gone. Late August is the one moment you might catch a little of both.

The route

Eight days, one rental car, one ferry, and three bases — arranged so that you get a puffin in the bank on day one and then work steadily up to the largest colony on earth. Total driving is modest by Iceland standards: about 500 km, all of it paved.

  1. Reykjavík

    2 nights · arrive KEF · the harbour islands

    Land, sleep, and get straight onto a boat out to Akurey — an hour from the Old Harbour and your wish is already granted. Then the city itself: basalt-column church, the food, a geothermal pool at 10pm.

  2. The South Coast → Vík

    2 nights · ~190 km · waterfalls, then cliffs

    Drive east past Seljalandsfoss and Skógafoss to Vík, which has puffins on the hillside above the church and Dyrhólaey fifteen minutes down the road. Work the evening light here for two nights.

  3. East to the ice — and Ingólfshöfði

    Optional 1 night · ~200 km further east

    If you want the glaciers: continue to Kirkjubæjarklaustur for Fjaðrárgljúfur, Skaftafell and Jökulsárlón — and the tractor ride out to Ingólfshöfði, the best puffin photography on the mainland.

  4. Heimaey

    2 nights · 35-min ferry from Landeyjahöfn

    The main event. A volcano that buried a third of the town in 1973, a harbour entrance through a cleft in the cliffs, and a million puffins on the southern headland. Take the car across.

  5. Back to Reykjavík

    ~2 hr from Landeyjahöfn

    Ferry back, drive west, and if there's an afternoon spare, the Blue Lagoon and Reykjanes sit right beside the airport.

Why this order

  • Puffin insurance first. The Reykjavík boat is cheap, an hour long, and runs up to seven times a day. Do it on arrival and the rest of the trip stops being a gamble.
  • Heimaey last, and overnight. Day-trippers leave on the afternoon ferry; the island goes quiet exactly when the birds come back to the cliffs. A day trip technically works and wastes the best hours.
  • The ferry decides your bases. Landeyjahöfn is 1 hr 45 min from Reykjavík and ~25 min west of Vík, so the South Coast leg and the island leg thread together naturally.
  • Build in a weather buffer. In rough seas the ferry diverts to Þorlákshöfn — a 3-hour crossing instead of 35 minutes. The port in use is announced daily at 6am and 3pm on the operator's Facebook page.
Trimming to 5 days Drop leg 3 entirely. Reykjavík (2) → Vík (1) → Heimaey (2) still delivers three separate colonies and every headline sight on the South Coast.
Leg one · 2 nights

Reykjavík

One of the genuinely strange pleasures of European travel: you step off a transatlantic flight, walk ten minutes to a working fishing harbour, and an hour later you are sitting in a bay full of puffins. Do that first. The city — small enough to cross on foot in half an hour, with a food scene absurdly good for a place of 140,000 people — can wait until you've banked the bird.

Bank the bird: the harbour islands

Two low green islands sit in Faxaflói bay, fifteen minutes out from the Old Harbour: Akurey and Lundey — the second name literally means Puffin Island. Around 30,000 birds nest on them. No boat lands (Akurey became a nature reserve in 2019), so this is a watch-from-the-water experience: the skippers cut the engine at the colony so you can hear it, and the birds porpoise past the hull in rafts. Operators publish a sightings guarantee. Take the earliest or the latest sailing of the day, bring 300mm if you have it, and expect it to be 5–8 °C colder on the water than on the quay.

The city itself

Two days is right. Hallgrímskirkja's stepped concrete façade is a deliberate abstraction of columnar basalt — the same formation you'll be standing on at Reynisfjara — and the tower lift gives you the whole city, the bay, and Snæfellsjökull on the horizon. Beyond that, the best of Reykjavík is low-key: the sundlaug ritual at a local geothermal pool, the Old Harbour at 11pm when the light goes long and gold, the walk out to Grótta lighthouse when the tide lets you.

Hot water, high views, and the long evening

Reykjavík's real evening programme, in a season when the evening doesn't end: a hot pot at 10pm, a viewing deck at 11, a lighthouse at midnight.

Eating

Iceland's food story is preservation turned into craft — cod dried into harðfiskur, lamb smoked over birch, milk strained into skyr — and then, since Dill won the country's first Michelin star in 2017, comprehensively reimagined. Chefs here forage crowberries and angelica off the lava fields and pull seaweed from cold fjords.

What to order

  • Humarsúpa — langoustine soup, sweet and lightly creamy. The version at Sægreifinn on the pier is the most famous cheap eat in the country.
  • Plokkfiskur — cod or haddock mashed with potato, onion and béchamel, baked with cheese. Leftover fish turned into comfort food. Eat it with buttered rúgbrauð.
  • Kjötsúpa — bone-in lamb broth with root vegetables. No cream, no fuss.
  • Fiskipanna — the day's catch cooked in butter and brought to the table sizzling in a copper pan.
  • Pylsa — the lamb-heavy hot dog, ~500 ISK, with fried onion and remoulade.
  • Skyr with berries — crowberries and bilberries ripen in July and August, so summer is the one time you get it at its best. Buy it in Bónus for a fraction of restaurant prices.
  • Harðfiskur — wind-dried cod, torn into strips, eaten with a thick knob of butter. Iceland's original protein bar; every supermarket has it.

Where to stay

Two choices really. 101 Miðborg, the old downtown postcode, puts Laugavegur's cafés, bars and restaurants outside your door and the day-tour pickup at City Hall two minutes away — but weekend nights on lower Laugavegur are loud until 5am, so ask for a back-facing room. Grandi, the reinvented Old Harbour warehouse strip, is quieter, more maritime, and three minutes' walk from the pier the puffin boats leave from — which, for this particular trip, is the decisive fact.

Leg two · 2–3 nights · ~190 km east

The South Coast

Route 1 east of Reykjavík is a highlight reel that refuses to let up: two of the world's most photographed waterfalls, a beach of black basalt columns, glacier tongues coming down almost to the tarmac, and — the reason you're here — four separate, reliable puffin colonies between Vík and Höfn. Vík itself has birds nesting on the hillside above the church, which makes a village of 600 people the most convenient puffin base in the country.

Four ways to meet a puffin on this coast

They run from zero effort to genuine expedition. Dyrhólaey is the one everyone does and it is legitimately good; Ingólfshöfði is the one you'll still be talking about.

The one access rule that will trip you up Dyrhólaey is a protected reserve, and during nesting the upper headland — the lighthouse, the main colony — is open 09:00–19:00 only, roughly mid-May to late June, with a full closure of the upper area for about the first twelve days of May. That window collides precisely with the best evening light. From late June both areas reopen normally. Exact dates shift each year with the birds' arrival: check the signs at the entrance and ust.is. The lower arch viewpoint stays open regardless.

Waterfalls, black sand and ice

Everything below is within a two-hour drive of Vík, and almost all of it is free. Two tips that change the days: go to the famous stops before 8am or after 8pm — under the midnight sun you get golden light and an empty car park at 11pm — and treat Reynisfjara with real respect.

Reynisfjara: read this before you go Sneaker waves have killed six people here since 2007, most recently a child in August 2025, and they arrive without warning out of a sea that looks flat. A physical traffic-light system now controls the entrance: yellow means stay 30 metres back from the waterline, red means the beach is closed — and closed means closed. The Hálsanef basalt cave section has been inaccessible since a 2025–26 landslide. Never turn your back on the water. Check safetravel.is on the day.

This is leg three of the route — the optional extra night at Kirkjubæjarklaustur or Höfn. Ingólfshöfði sits right in the middle of it, between Skaftafell and Jökulsárlón, which is what makes the extra night worth taking.

Eating along the road

This is farm and roadside country, not restaurant country, and the gaps are real: there is almost nothing between Hvolsvöllur and Vík (60 km) or Vík and Kirkjubæjarklaustur (75 km). Eat the guesthouse breakfast, carry snacks, and book dinner before you set out for the day — in villages with three restaurants total, walking in at 8pm in July does not work.

The compensation is that the best food here is inseparable from the geology that makes it. At Laugarvatn Fontana a guide digs a pot of hverabrauð out of the black lakeshore sand where it has been baking at about 95 °C for 24 hours — no crust, dense, faintly sweet, eaten hot with salted butter. At Friðheimar you eat tomato soup among the vines it came from, in a greenhouse lit and heated geothermally through the polar night. Neither is a gimmick; both are how this landscape has always been used.

Where to sleep

Three possible bases, and the choice follows what you want in the morning. Hella / Hvolsvöllur in the west sits 15 minutes from the Landeyjahöfn ferry — the right call if Heimaey is the priority. Vík is the geographic sweet spot and the puffin base: Dyrhólaey and Reynisfjara within 20 minutes, birds on the hill above town, and genuine quiet once the buses leave around 6pm. Kirkjubæjarklaustur, an hour further east in the middle of the Eldhraun lava field, sets you up for Ingólfshöfði, Skaftafell and the glacier lagoons without backtracking.

Vík-area rooms for June–August are typically gone by February. If everything is full, look at Icelandic Farm Holidays (bbl.is) — working sheep farms with guest rooms, excellent value, and rarely listed on the big booking sites.

Leg three · 2 nights · the main event

Heimaey
& the million

Vestmannaeyjar — the Westman Islands — is a chain of fifteen volcanic islands sitting 7 to 10 km off the South Coast, of which exactly one is inhabited. Heimaey is 13 km² and holds about 4,500 people, a volcano that erupted in the middle of town in 1973, and somewhere over a million nesting pairs of Atlantic puffins — the largest colony of the species anywhere on earth. This is the one place where seeing a puffin stops being a hope and becomes a logistics problem.

Getting there

The Herjólfur ferry runs from Landeyjahöfn, a 1 hr 45 min drive from Reykjavík along the South Coast, and crosses in 35–45 minutes. In summer there are seven sailings each way daily: out at 08:15, 10:45, 13:15, 15:45, 18:15, 20:45, 23:15; back at 07:00, 09:30, 12:00, 14:30, 17:00, 19:30, 22:00. The boat takes 65–70 cars and 540 passengers.

Bring the car. Stórhöfði is a 10-minute drive from town but a 40–60 minute walk each way along a road with no footpath, and Eldfell, Heimaklettur and the west coast all open up with wheels. Foot passenger spaces are almost always available last-minute; vehicle slots are not — book those at herjolfur.is days ahead in July, months ahead for Þjóðhátíð. Without a car, a two-hour minibus island tour covers the puffin lookout and the volcano perfectly well, and bikes rent locally.

The approach is one of the great arrivals in Iceland: the ferry threads a narrow gap between sheer cliffs with Heimaklettur rising at the end of it and the 1973 lava field on the other side. Stand on the starboard bow.

If the sea is up In heavy swell the ferry diverts to Þorlákshöfn — roughly three hours, and notoriously rough. Which port is operating is announced daily at 6am and 3pm on the ferry's Facebook page. Check it before you drive to the terminal, and don't book a flight home for the day you sail back.

Two nights, planned around the birds

  • Afternoon 1 — arrive, walk the buried streets in the lava field, then Eldheimar for the eruption story before you climb anything.
  • Evening 1 — up Eldfell for the last two hours of light on the red cinder.
  • Morning 2 — Heimaklettur if you're a confident scrambler, or the harbour, Skansinn and the stave church if not.
  • Afternoon 2 — a RIB or boat tour under the cliffs; sea caves and colonies you cannot reach on foot.
  • Evening 2 — the point of the whole trip. Drive out to Stórhöfði at 8 or 9pm and stay until you're cold. Then walk a little past the hut toward the lighthouse.
  • Wet or blown out? The hut at Stórhöfði is a roofed shelter, the Sea Life Trust has resident rescued puffins, and Eldheimar is indoors.

The cliffs

The colony rings the whole island, but the concentration is on the southern peninsula and the entire western coast. Crucially, all the best of it is free, on foot, and unfenced — no boat ticket, no guide, no barrier. Wind is the only real constraint: check station 6017 (Stórhöfði) on vedur.is and treat anything under about 10 m/s as a good evening.

The island that half-buried itself

At 01:55 on 23 January 1973 a fissure opened in a field on the eastern edge of town without warning. The entire population of about 5,000 was evacuated overnight in the fishing fleet — which happened to be in harbour because of a storm. The eruption ran five months, buried over 400 houses under up to 60 metres of lava, and added 2 km² to the island. The islanders then did something extraordinary: they pumped millions of litres of seawater onto the advancing lava front to cool and stall it, and saved the harbour their entire economy depended on. Walk the lava field and you find wooden signs naming the streets 30 to 60 metres beneath your feet.

Coast, fort and outer islands

Eating on Heimaey

For a town of 4,500 the food is startlingly serious. Gísli Matt's family put the island on the global culinary map with Slippurinn, in a converted shipyard workshop — and then closed it in 2025, on their own terms, after fourteen seasons. Næs, across the street, carries the same forager-fisherman cooking in a looser share-plates register, with a menu that changes weekly according to what the boats landed. Out here the sea isn't scenery, it's the larder: the islands sit on some of the richest cold-water fishing grounds in the North Atlantic, and the volcanic soil is too thin to farm.

Local hours matter on a small island. Dinner is late — around 20:00 — and kitchens may only be opening at 18:00. Reserve Næs and Einsi Kaldi a day or two ahead in peak summer. And ask for eyja bátar at Kráin: "island boats", the archipelago's own submarine sandwich, named for the fishing fleet and not found by that name anywhere else in Iceland.

Where to stay

There are no neighbourhoods here — everything sits in a walkable town about 1.5 km across. What varies is your relationship to Stórhöfði, 4–5 km south. Stay in town for restaurants, the museum and the harbour on foot; stay on the west side near Herjólfsdalur and the coastal walking track to the puffin cliffs starts behind the golf course, no car required. Book far ahead: the whole island has only a handful of properties, and Þjóðhátíð weekend takes all of them.

Further colonies

Iceland has better puffin cliffs than the three legs above — they are just a long way from them. Each of these is genuinely worth the drive for the right traveller, and genuinely not worth it for everyone else, so here is the honest version of each.

Látrabjarg — the closest you will ever stand to a wild puffin

Westfjords · add 2–3 days · gravel roads · evening only

Europe's largest bird cliff: fourteen kilometres of rock rising to 440 metres at the westernmost point of Iceland, with millions of seabirds on it. Park at the Bjargtangar lighthouse at the end of road 612 and walk the clifftop trail roughly 1.2 km east, past the razorbill section, until you reach the puffin ground. There are no fences, no boardwalks and no barriers anywhere. The birds — hunted here for centuries but never shot at from the clifftop — are famously unbothered, and will walk to within one to three metres. One photographer working here filled the frame at 200mm from three metres away.

Two hard conditions. First, timing: come between 9pm and 1am. A midday visit can turn up almost nothing, and travellers have driven six hours for an empty cliff. Second, safety: the turf overhangs the drop and is hollowed out from beneath by the burrows themselves, so the edge that looks solid is not. Do not stand on it — sit down, then lie on your stomach to look over. Gusts arrive without warning. There has been a death here.

Getting there is the cost: road 612 is maintained gravel that hugs cliff edges without barriers, there is no fuel past Patreksfjörður, and the whole point is a late-night return on that surface — so you need a bed at Breiðavík or Patreksfjörður, not a day trip. On the way you get the rusting hulk of the Garðar BA 64, Iceland's oldest steel ship, beached on the shore since 1981; the pink-gold crushed-shell sand of Rauðisandur down a switchbacking mountain pass with no guardrails; and, if you loop out via Arnarfjörður, the hundred-metre tiered cascade of Dynjandi.

Honest verdict Sources who have visited both rate Hafnarhólmi as equally close, more reliable and paved all the way, and Heimaey as an incomparably larger colony reachable in 35 minutes from a road you're already driving. Látrabjarg earns its place if you want the remoteness and the Westfjords themselves as much as the birds — and if you will genuinely commit to the evening timing. Otherwise it's three days for something you can get in an evening on Heimaey.

Hafnarhólmi — the best-built puffin hide in Iceland

Borgarfjörður Eystri, East Iceland · add 1–2 days · paved throughout

At the dead end of Route 94, hemmed in by the jagged rhyolite ridges of Dyrfjöll — the Door Mountains — sits a village of a hundred people and a small rocky headland joined to the shore by a 1970s causeway. About 10,000 pairs of puffins nest in its banks, alongside kittiwakes, fulmars and eiders. What makes it exceptional is not the number: it is that someone built the right thing here. A free car park, a network of elevated wooden boardwalks laid across the nesting slopes, a staircase up to a panoramic level, and — best of all — an enclosed bird hide sitting inside the colony, with chairs and windows you can shut against the wind.

The birds are completely habituated to it and carry on billing, landing and arguing a metre from the boards. 50–100mm is enough for a frame-filling portrait; phone photos work. There are toilets, and the Hafnarhólmi café looks out over the marina and the colony. It is free, with a donation box. Season runs mid-April to around 15 August — the earliest recorded arrival in 2025 was 8 April — and the same daily rule applies as everywhere: before 10am or after 5pm, with 6–10pm the reported sweet spot.

Around it: the elf-hill of Álfaborg, seat of the queen of East Iceland's huldufólk; the turf-roofed Lindarbakki; a village church holding a 1914 Kjarval altarpiece that paints Christ preaching from the top of Álfaborg with the Dyrfjöll behind him; and Stórurð, a 4–6 hour hike into a valley of house-sized boulders and turquoise meltwater pools that is among the best day walks in the country.

Honest verdict A conditional yes. From Reykjavík this is a ~600 km, 14-hour round trip — a serious bite out of eight days — but Route 94 is fully paved and Egilsstaðir is a 45-minute domestic flight from Reykjavík City Airport. If you are going to East Iceland anyway (Stuðlagil, Hengifoss, Seyðisfjörður and Vök Baths make a strong loop), the detour is an easy yes and you should overnight — day-tripping from Egilsstaðir destroys the morning-and-evening timing that makes the place work. Only two small properties and a campsite, so book months out, and avoid Bræðslan weekend unless that's why you came.

Two more, and whether to bother

Snæfellsnes is two and a half hours north-west of Reykjavík and often sold as a puffin day trip. Be sceptical: the land-based sites — the Arnarstapi–Hellnar cliff walk, Lóndrangar, Svörtuloft — are only moderately reliable for puffins, and the last of those needs 15–20 minutes of rough gravel that conditions can close. The one dependable option is the Breiðafjörður boat tour from Stykkishólmur. As a day trip the peninsula is a stretch — three hours each way leaves five or six hours for a place that deserves more, and no room at all for the boat. Give it one night (Arnarstapi, Hellnar, Grundarfjörður or Stykkishólmur) and it becomes a genuinely lovely leg: Kirkjufell, Djúpalónssandur's black pebbles and lifting stones, the Búðakirkja black church, seals at Ytri Tunga, and a glacier-capped volcano at the tip. Go for the peninsula, not for the puffins.

North Iceland — Húsavík and, three hours further by ferry, the Arctic Circle island of Grímsey, where puffins sit at eye level on open land with no fees and no barriers. It's a wonderful place. But our researchers' verdict was blunt and we'll pass it on: the detour does not earn its place on puffins alone. Reykjavík's harbour reaches 30,000 birds in fifteen minutes and Heimaey holds a million a 35-minute ferry from a road you're already on, while Grímsey means a five-weekly ferry, three hours each way and about four hours ashore — or a flight from Akureyri. Go north for Skjálfandi bay's humpbacks, which are the most reliable whale watching in Iceland, for the Diamond Circle, and for the Arctic Circle line itself. Treat the puffins as a bonus.

And if you just have one spare day The Golden Circle — Þingvellir's continental rift and thousand-year-old parliament site, Strokkur erupting every 5–10 minutes, and Gullfoss thundering into its canyon — is a 6–8 hour paved loop from Reykjavík, all three sites free to enter, and it slots neatly onto the drive out to the South Coast. Take it clockwise to stay ahead of the coaches, and stop at Friðheimar or Laugarvatn Fontana for lunch.
