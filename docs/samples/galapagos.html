Plate I · Orientation

A cold current, a grey sky, and no fear at all

Archipiélago de Colón — Islas Galápagos, Ecuador

June is the seam. The warm, rainy months have just closed and the garúa has taken hold: a low grey lid over the highlands, a fine mist that keeps the ferns and Scalesia forests dripping while the coast below stays bone dry. Out at sea the Humboldt Current has swung north and dropped the water into the low seventies, and everything that eats plankton has come to meet it.

What that produces on land is a kind of controlled bedlam. On the malecón at Puerto Baquerizo Moreno, sea lions occupy the public benches, the moored boats and the boat ramp with the unbothered confidence of ratepayers — you step over one asleep on the concrete. At the Puerto Ayora fish market, pelicans and frigatebirds crowd the counter while a resident sea lion positions herself behind it, and the fishmonger works around her. On every black lava shelf in the archipelago, Sally Lightfoot crabs sit like spilled paint. Marine iguanas cross the sand streets of Puerto Villamil at their own pace.

The National Park's rule is that you keep two metres from any animal. In practice that rule protects them from you and does nothing at all in the other direction: the boobies nest beside the trail, the Española mockingbirds come to investigate your water bottle, and a Galápagos hawk will drop onto a post a few feet away and look at you. This is the only place a wide-angle lens is the wildlife lens.

For a photographer, June's grey sky is the quiet gift. Overcast garúa light works like a softbox the size of the sky — no blown highlights on a white booby, no crushed black on a marine iguana, no midday shadow carving up a tortoise's face. The trade is that big landscape frames go flat and the crossings get lumpier. Take the trade.

The one thing that decides your dates

If your June week is still movable, nothing else in the calendar matters as much as this: the waved albatross is on Española from April to December, and nowhere else on the planet. Nearly the entire world population comes ashore at Punta Suárez to court — bill-fencing, honking, that ridiculous swaying walk. Eggs are laid mid-April to late June and the first chicks appear in early June, so a June visit lands you on adults in full display rather than nests full of down. Build the trip around a day on Española and let everything else fall into place behind it.

Getting the shape rightTwo bases, not three

Six days is enough for two islands properly, or three islands badly. Puerto Ayora is the ferry hub, so any three-island loop means backtracking through it — and there is no direct lancha from Isabela to San Cristóbal at all. Every leg you add costs a dawn start and a half day at sea, which on a six-day trip is the difference between photographing an island and glimpsing it.

So the guide's core route is Santa Cruz → San Cristóbal, flying into Baltra and out of San Cristóbal so you never retrace a mile. That pairing buys you the archipelago's two best June wildlife days — North Seymour for booby courtship and frigatebird display, Española for the albatross — plus wild highland tortoises, in-town sea lion colonies, and Kicker Rock. Isabela then sits below as a genuine third leg: add two or three days for it, or swap it in for San Cristóbal if penguins and a volcano rim matter more to you than albatrosses. Both are good trips. They are not the same trip.

Plate II · The June brief

Shooting the garúa

What is happening in June, and how to photograph it

Two things make June exceptional and one thing makes it awkward. The exceptional: the archipelago's whole courtship calendar peaks at once, and the sky turns into a softbox. The awkward: the same weather system flattens your landscapes and roughens your crossings. Here is the whole picture, on one axis.

The rules that shape every frameFive that actually change your photography

These are the Park's own, and they are not decorative — guides enforce most of them and the archipelago's survival depends on them.

Two metres. "Maintain a distance of at least six feet (two meters) from wildlife… even if they approach you." Read that carefully: the animal may close the gap and that's fine. You may not. This single rule is why you need reach for behaviour — by the time you have legally repositioned for a pair of mating iguanas, it's over — and why a wide lens works for anything sitting still.

No flash on wildlife. Some guides look the other way. Don't take the offer. Carry a small white reflector and lean on stabilisation and ISO instead.

A naturalist guide, always, outside the port towns. Your guide sets the route and the pace, and photographers are habitually the slowest part of any group. Plan on their clock, not yours.

Stay on the marked trail. There is no stepping off for a better angle. Compose from where the path puts you — which is why lying prone on the trail, camera braced on a folded towel, is the single most useful Galápagos technique.

No drones, at all. Aerial tourism is prohibited across the National Park and Marine Reserve. You can carry one in your luggage and you cannot fly it. Research and commercial permits exist and take at least fifteen working days.

GlassBring both ends. Skip the monster.

The received wisdom cuts both ways here and both halves are wrong. "The animals are tame so any lens will do" is wrong — one normal zoom immediately limits what you can shoot, and the middle focal lengths produce the least interesting Galápagos pictures, because there are really only two things worth going for: environment or detail.

But "you won't need a long lens" is wrong too. You need reach not for distance but for timing — the 2-metre rule means you cannot close in fast enough to catch action. A useful counterweight: one professional shooting Española and Punta Suárez with a 300mm f/2.8 found it "one of the only locations where I've struggled to get the shot because the animals were too close," and never once needed his 2× teleconverter.

So: a genuine wide (16–35mm class) and a 100–400mm class tele, both focusing close — under 3 m, preferably closer — and a focus limiter for birds in flight. One favourite Galápagos frame of a blue-footed booby nesting on a cliff edge was taken at 24mm from about a foot away. Add a macro for what everyone ignores: iguana scales, lava lizards, Sally Lightfoot crabs, the plants.

Two of you is the second body. On a panga there is no room to change lenses and no time. One camera wears the wide, one wears the tele, and neither of you swaps mid-encounter.

Sitting on the equator, the Galápagos gets about twelve hours of light every day of the year — sunrise near 06:00, sunset near 18:00. But the sun's path is close to vertical, so it doesn't rake in at a low angle: golden hour and twilight both compress into a sliver. There is no warning ramp. Be in position before the window opens.

Keeping the gear aliveHumidity, salt, lava, and one rogue wave

The archipelago is unusually hard on cameras, and there is no professional repair anywhere in Ecuador that can help you. What you bring is what you have. One workshop leader has watched six cameras die across sixty photographers. His two absolute rules: if the body is more than a year old, have it professionally cleaned and checked before you go — hot sun, high humidity and blowing salt push any lingering fault over the edge — and if it's new or newly serviced, practise with it at home first.

The condensation cycle is the specific Galápagos failure. An aggressively air-conditioned hotel room to humid garúa air fogs lenses and viewfinders and, across a week, encourages fungus inside the optics. Store the bag somewhere unconditioned; let gear reach ambient temperature before you use it; never seal a cold camera into a warm, damp dry bag.

Clean nightly, not at the end. Blot salt off the body with a soft towel; cotton buds and a large brush for the crevices; lens fluid for spray. Salt left on seals and metal overnight corrodes. Volcanic dust and fine beach sand work into the same gaps.

Lava eats plastic bodies. Never set a camera down bare on rock — a towel, a beanbag, or your own t-shirt underneath, which doubles as your brace for prone, ground-level frames.

The panga is where kit dies. One first-timer was advised to keep a backup body on the boat and work out of a dry bag; his panga then took a rogue wave and swamped everyone aboard. Several cameras were ruined and their owners had nothing for the rest of the trip. He was fine. Carry the cheaper body on panga-only excursions — if you're more likely to lose it, lose the backup.

Shooting from the panga has its own etiquette and physics. Clear it with the guide first; they don't like surprises and may judge it unsafe. Only one person stands at a time — if everyone stands the boat is far likelier to capsize. Stand across the keel, feet spread over the boat's width, never along it, or you have no support at all. Move slowly: hand your gear to someone, stand, get stable, take it back. Then go wider, raise ISO — a little noise beats a blurred frame — and let stabilisation do the work. A tripod is useless on water; a monopod is the good compromise ashore.

In the water, hover still and stop kicking and curious animals close the distance themselves. Shoot turtles, sea lions and penguins near the surface where the light is. No lights or strobes on skittish subjects. And treat the waterproof compact as genuinely disposable — wade in holding it overhead, drag it down dusty trails, accept that one day it dies, and count the pictures you'd never otherwise have risked.

Plate IV · Leg one · 2 nights

Santa Cruz & Puerto Ayora

Isla Santa Cruz — the working heart of the archipelago

Puerto Ayora is a real port town, not a resort strip — twenty minutes end to end on foot, every dive shop and tour agency in one stretch, and sea lions asleep on the public benches. It is also where the boats leave from, which is the actual reason you're here.

June on Santa Cruz splits in two. The coast stays dry and mostly overcast at 21–26°C; the highlands, twenty minutes uphill, sit in permanent mist that keeps the Scalesia forest dripping and the pastures green. Pack a fleece for the tortoise reserves even when town is warm. That mist is why the highlands are the best place on the island to photograph giant tortoises in June: they stay out grazing all day instead of retreating into shade.

On foot from townWhat you can reach without a boat or a guide

An unusual amount, as it turns out. These are all free-access or near-free sites — no naturalist guide required — which makes them your weather buffer and your early-morning shooting ground while everyone else is queuing for a day boat.

Twenty minutes uphillThe highlands, in the mist

A single road climbs from Puerto Ayora through Bellavista to Santa Rosa and on to the Itabaca Channel, and almost everything worth seeing inland is strung along it. Do the whole lot as one half-day by taxi — around $40 return, with the driver waiting — or fold it into your arrival or departure transfer, which is the smarter play on a six-day trip.

Plate V · The day boat · from Puerto Ayora

North Seymour — the courtship island

Isla Seymour Norte · Fregata magnificens · Sula nebouxii

A flat, dry, one-square-kilometre slab of uplifted seabed with a single two-kilometre loop trail through it — and the highest concentration of seabird theatre in the Galápagos. In June, both headline species are performing at once.

The magic of North Seymour is its scale. There is no hill to climb, no long approach, no searching. The panga puts you on rocky shore where sea lions are body-surfing the break, and within a hundred metres the trail is threading between nesting birds on both sides. The whole loop takes a guided ninety minutes to two and a half hours, and you will not stop shooting.

Magnificent frigatebirds hold the largest colony in the archipelago here, and their display season runs roughly March to June — you arrive at the back end of the peak. The males sit low in the brown, twiggy scrub and inflate a scarlet gular pouch the size of a grapefruit, throwing their heads back and drumming. Because they perch low and in shade, this is where the garúa overcast earns its keep — flat light on a red pouch against dull scrub instead of blown highlights and black holes.

The blue-footed boobies nest on both sides of the path, and June sits at the front edge of their courtship peak (June–August). You want the foot-lifting dance: one bird high-stepping to show off the feet, the other arching back and sky-pointing with lifted wings. They favour rocks — as though they know they want a pedestal.

Then the interior: dry Opuntia cactus forest holding Galápagos land iguanas, the males a startling amber-yellow. Several visitors report North Seymour as the only place they saw one. On the way back the coastal stretch delivers marine iguanas in profile against the ocean, swallow-tailed gulls — the world's only fully nocturnal seabird, sitting obligingly still by day — brown pelicans working low over the water, and a sea lion colony with nursing pups.

Book it early, and book it mid-trip

The Park caps daily visitors and these boats sell out. Reserve weeks ahead in June, not days. And don't schedule it for your arrival day — go once you've found your sea legs, because June crossings are the choppier kind.

Plate VI · Eating on Santa Cruz

The street they close for dinner

Puerto Ayora — la calle de los kioskos

Galápagos food is defined by one hard fact: you are 972 km from mainland Ecuador, and almost everything except fish and a few highland crops arrives by boat or plane. What the sea gives, though, is some of the best-regulated seafood in South America.

The defining ritual is simple. At 5pm the National Park closes Charles Binford to traffic, and a row of open-front kiosks drags plastic tables straight out onto the road. They grill the day's catch over open flame — often guava wood — a few feet from where you sit. It is emphatically not fine dining: cramped shared seating, menus painted directly onto the wall, owner-chefs like William cooking your fish themselves under a mural of their own face. Tourists and locals eat side by side. No reservations; walk the street and pick a kiosk, early, before it fills.

Sustainability here isn't a marketing line — it's enforced by the Park as a condition of the Marine Reserve's survival. Only licensed artisanal fishermen may fish, using selective methods, and several species run on strict government-set quotas that open and close by date once population monitoring allows it. Which means you eat on nature's calendar, not a menu's. For your June trip, the practical translation is: lobster is out. The season runs roughly September to December. Lean into yellowfin tuna, brujo, and the odd mollusc instead. All of them are better in June anyway.

Plate VII · Sleeping on Santa Cruz

Two nights in town

Puerto Ayora — and the case against the highlands

There is only one sensible base for this itinerary, and it's Puerto Ayora. Everything you need is in a twenty-minute walk: the tour operators, the dive shops, the kiosk street, and the pier your San Cristóbal lancha leaves from. The highlands are a genuinely different Galápagos — misty, green, tortoises on the lawn — but they're 25–30 minutes by taxi from the harbour and the pier, and on a ferry-heavy six-day trip that friction is real. Treat the highlands as a place you drive through, or as a one-night add-on if you extend.

Plate VIII · Leg two · 3 nights

San Cristóbal & Puerto Baquerizo Moreno

Isla San Cristóbal — Zalophus wollebaeki, in numbers

The provincial capital, and the island where the sea lions simply took the town. They are on the benches, across the storefront steps, draped over moored boats, asleep on the boat ramp — as though they pay rent. It is the easiest serious wildlife photography anywhere in the archipelago, and it costs nothing.

The cold Humboldt water that arrives in June is what makes this leg work. The nutrient bloom pulls in dense fish schools, rays and sharks; it is the first month locals start telling you to rent a wetsuit. And it puts you within a two-hour boat ride of Española while the albatross colony is in full courtship.

One caution the sources are unanimous about: the equatorial sun burns straight through the garúa cloud. A grey sky here is not a mild sky. Long sleeves, a brimmed hat and high-factor cream, even on the overcast days — especially on Española, where there is no shade for two hours.

Free, and on footThe walkable island

San Cristóbal's real luxury is how much needs no boat, no guide and no ticket. A single loop from the malecón — Interpretation Centre, up Cerro Tijeretas, down to Darwin's Cove for a snorkel, along to Punta Carola for sunset — is one of the best half-days in the Galápagos and entirely free. Do it on your last morning, or your first evening, or both.

Up in the greenThe highlands trio, in one taxi

Three sites strung along one highland road, bundled by every driver in town into a single circuit for around $60 per group — no naturalist guide required. Go early: El Junco is often fogged in at dawn and clears as the morning runs on, and the frigatebirds bathe in the lake in the cool hours.

Plate IX · The day that decides your dates

Española — the albatross colony

Phoebastria irrorata · nests in one place on earth, and this is it

Ten to twelve hours door to door, two and a half of them across open, choppy water each way, on a boat you must book weeks in advance. It is the single richest wildlife day achievable in the Galápagos without a cruise, and in June it is at its absolute peak.

Española is the oldest and most southerly of the main islands, uninhabited, and reachable only by licensed boat. Not every boat runs it and not every day — the National Park caps landings to control visitor numbers, and which vessel you get is determined by your travel dates, because the operators are assigned by day under a rotation set by the Ministry of Tourism. In practice: fix your Española day first, book it before anything else, and build the rest of the week around it.

Why June specifically. The albatross come ashore in April and stay through December. Eggs are laid from mid-April to late June, and the first chicks appear in early June — so a June visit puts you on adults in full courtship and incubating pairs, before the colony fills with down. Come in late July instead and you trade the dance for chicks. Come in February and there is nothing here at all.

Take this seriously

Punta Suárez is a two-hour walk with no shade at all under a near-vertical equatorial sun. One first-hand account records a member of their tour group having to be evacuated by the crew for heat exhaustion. Water, hat, long sleeves, and pace yourself — you cannot leave the trail and you cannot leave early.

Stop one · morning · ~2 hours on footPunta Suárez

A dry landing, then a trail through saltbush and grassy stretches along the island's rocky south shore, and it starts before you're ready: sea lions block the path and you step over or around them. Then, in sequence and all within a couple of metres of the trail —

Nesting blue-footed and Nazca boobies right beside the path. June is inside the blue-footed courtship window, so watch for the sky-point: one bird stamping its feet like a slow-motion step show while the other arches back and lifts its wings. Nazca booby nesting on Española runs closer to November–February, so expect them present but less concentrated.

Española's "Christmas iguanas" — the only marine iguana population on earth that stays brightly red-and-green all year round, elsewhere a seasonal breeding colour. They huddle en masse on the rare patches of ground not covered in boulders. Get low; this is the macro and wide-angle subject of the day.

Galápagos hawks that will land within a few feet of you, plus the endemic Española mockingbird and Española lava lizard, and swallow-tailed gulls. Partway along, the trail reaches a blowhole that fires spray 45 to 90 feet up through the lava; sit on the cliff edge there and watch albatrosses use the updraft to launch.

And then the colony itself, walked through at close range — birds on open ground, bill-fencing, honking, and stretching two-and-a-half-metre wings along the cliff edge before dropping over it. The cliff top functions as their runway: a strip of a metre or three, and they need all of it.

Stop two · after lunch · beach & snorkelGardner Bay

A wet landing onto one of the longest white coral-sand beaches in the Galápagos — hundreds of sea lions stretching as far as you can see, entirely indifferent to visitors. If your boat is an early one it matters: one account landed at 07:00 and beat the other boats by ten or fifteen minutes, which is the difference between clean beach frames and other people's tripods.

The bird to watch for here is the Española mockingbird, endemic and famously bold. It will hop right up to you, look directly into your face, investigate your bag and dig in the sand at your feet. Guides discourage giving them water, however hard they ask. It's the one Galápagos subject where the problem is that a 400mm won't focus close enough.

Then the boat repositions offshore to snorkel a rock formation about 50–75 metres out — some crews call it Turtle Rock. Schools of fish, green turtles, resting Galápagos sharks, and near-guaranteed curious young sea lions doing barrel rolls a hand's width from your mask. Currents here are genuinely strong; this suits confident swimmers, and the crew keep a support panga close for anyone who tires.

Plate XI · Eating on San Cristóbal

A fishing town with a farm-to-table problem

Puerto Baquerizo Moreno

Everything green on your plate arrived by boat or plane; everything from the sea came off a skiff this morning. That tension runs through every menu in town, from the $5 family lunch counter to the tasting menu on the waterfront.

Chefs here talk about sourcing the way chefs elsewhere talk about technique. Around 40% of what a Galápagos kitchen uses is bought locally, and when something runs out there is no shop to run to — you call other boats to see who can help. One highland restaurant, Otoy, grows 90% of its own produce, which in this archipelago is close to a miracle. Meanwhile the local catch — brujo, bacalao, canchalagua, and in season the red spiny lobster — is what locals point to as genuinely Galapagan rather than simply Ecuadorian-coastal.

Plate XIII · The third leg · add 2–3 nights, or swap it in

Isabela — penguins and a caldera

Isla Isabela · Spheniscus mendiculus — the only penguin north of the equator

The seahorse-shaped giant: bigger than every other island in the archipelago combined, six volcanoes along its spine, and a single sand-street village of about two thousand people wrapped around a four-kilometre beach.

Isabela is the leg the core six days can't quite hold, and the one you'll most regret leaving out. In June its case is specific and strong: the Cromwell Current upwelling is at its most active, pushing cold, nutrient-rich water onto Isabela's shores, and that makes this the most reliable month of the year to find Galápagos penguins hauled out in the lava crevices at Las Tintoreras — nesting season runs May to January. It's also the start of the best whale-watching on the west coast.

How to fit it. Two honest options. Add it: Santa Cruz 2 nights → Isabela 2–3 → back through Santa Cruz → San Cristóbal 2–3, which is an eight or nine-day trip, not six. Or swap it: Santa Cruz 2–3 nights → Isabela 3, then fly home out of Baltra. That version trades the waved albatross for penguins, a volcano rim and flamingos — a completely legitimate trade, just a different trip. What doesn't work is squeezing all three into six days: there is no direct Isabela–San Cristóbal boat, so every hop routes back through Puerto Ayora.

The crossing from Santa Cruz is roughly 2–2.5 hours by speedboat, and it gets rougher through the garúa season as the cold currents strengthen. Take the tablets. Draw all your cash before you board: Isabela is effectively cash-only, and the one ATM in town is not something to rely on.

Be clear-eyed about the west coast

The flightless cormorant, the Mola mola, and the famous western sites — Tagus Cove, Punta Vicente Roca, Elizabeth Bay, Urbina Bay — are cruise-only and simply cannot be reached from Puerto Villamil on a day trip. If the cormorant is a must-have shot, that alone is the argument for adding a short liveaboard leg. Everything below is genuinely reachable from a bed in town.

Three days, well spent

  • Day ASierra Negra & Volcán Chico — the one big inland day, best in the morning
  • Day BLos Túneles — weather-dependent, so keep it flexible
  • Day CLas Tintoreras half-day, then the free town-side sites on foot and by bike

The geography does the planning for you: the free sites — Concha de Perla, the tortoise centre, the wetlands, the Wall of Tears road — all cluster along one route out of town, and cost nothing but a $2–3/hour bike.

A hard constraint

Puerto Villamil is the only place to stay on Isabela. The highlands, the Sierra Negra area and the rest of the island have no lodging at all. The choice isn't which town — it's which few blocks.

The island's four big daysLava channels, tunnels, a caldera and a free lagoon

And the road out of townFree, on foot or on two wheels

One sandy track runs west out of Puerto Villamil past the wetlands and the tortoise centre to the Wall of Tears, five kilometres out. Rent a bike for a couple of dollars an hour and give it a morning: giant tortoises cross the path, marine iguanas cover the rocks at Playa del Amor, flamingos work the salt pans, and mangrove estuaries open up along the way. The wall itself — built by the prisoners of Isabela's 1946–59 penal colony, sixty-five feet of stacked lava block — takes fifteen minutes and is grim rather than scenic. The ride there is the experience.

Eating in Puerto VillamilA fishing village with a beach-town layer on top

There is no fine dining here and, given the remoteness, there may never be. What you get instead is real working-port cooking built around whatever the Pacific gave up that morning — tuna, wahoo, swordfish, and brujo with patacones.

Two layers matter. The restaurant row around the main square serves the tourist trade — pizza, shawarma, seafood platters. A few blocks inland, the municipal market and the workers' lunch counters serve the town's own rhythm: an empanada con queso and a glass of warm, cinnamon-spiced morocho standing up at midday, or a big almuerzo at shared tables with off-duty fishermen. Eat in both.

The two hours that catch everyone out

"Lunch clock starts right at 12 noon in the Galápagos — arrive somewhere after 12:30 and you may not get a seat, or some dishes will already be sold out." Then most kitchens close for siesta and only reopen around 5pm. Lunch after 1pm is a genuine challenge, and a lot of places don't open at all on Sundays. Plan dinner for 6pm onwards, and don't assume a place serving lunch will still be serving mid-afternoon.

Plate XIV · If you have another day

The boats you didn't take

Everything below leaves from Puerto Ayora and competes directly with North Seymour for a day of your Santa Cruz leg. None of them beats it for June wildlife — but two of them are the best landscapes in the archipelago, and one is the best story.

The most photographed view in the Galápagos

Bartolomé & Pinnacle Rock

Bus + boat · 10–12 hr door to door · ~$190–350 pp · book 1–2 weeks ahead

A barren volcanic islet off Santiago, and the one postcard everyone comes home with. The day runs long: hotel pickup between 5:30 and 7:00 depending on whether you're on a sailboat or a yacht, forty-five minutes across the highlands to the Itabaca Channel, then a two-to-two-and-a-half hour crossing north (currents can stretch it to three). Breakfast is served on board and dolphins are common on the way.

Ashore, a wooden boardwalk and staircase — somewhere between 370 and 600 steps, depending who's counting — climbs thirty to forty-five minutes through spatter cones and lava cactus to the summit viewpoint: twin bays split by a narrow isthmus, the arrowhead of Pinnacle Rock spearing out of the water, and Santiago's lava fields stretching behind. Your guide will point out Tiquilia nesiotica and, frequently, a Galápagos hawk.

Then the part that matters more for wildlife: a fifteen-minute panga ride along the base of Pinnacle Rock specifically to find Galápagos penguins — sunning on the rock, or flying through the water — and blue-footed boobies on the shoreline, both of which are in season in June. One visitor watched a penguin dodge a hunting sea lion, and found sea lions lying in holes partway up the rock itself. Afterwards, a snorkel where you have a real chance of swimming with penguins, alongside white-tip reef sharks, turtles and rays.

Two things to ask before you book

  • Which snorkel site?The Park rotates day boats between Pinnacle Rock and the calmer, shallower Sullivan Bay. Only Pinnacle Rock gives you the deep-water penguin and shark swim
  • What time is the summit?Day tours climb mid-morning to early afternoon. The golden-hour Pinnacle Rock shot belongs to multi-day cruises — don't plan on it

June's overcast actually helps the summit panorama — it softens what is otherwise brutal volcanic contrast. It also means flat white sky, so book the clearest morning you're offered.

Flamingos, an olivine beach, and a barrel of post

Floreana

Speedboat · ~9 hr round trip · from ~$210 pp · book 24 hr ahead

The quietest of the four inhabited islands — about a hundred people at Puerto Velasco Ibarra — and the one with the best story. A day tour from Puerto Ayora is roughly ninety minutes' sailing each way, and the Park reshuffles the stops on the day.

The reliable highlight is Punta Cormorant: a wet landing onto a beach tinted gold-green by olivine crystals, then a flat two-kilometre loop past a brackish lagoon that is one of the most dependable flamingo sites in the archipelago. Note the June timing honestly — flamingo courtship displays peak in July and August, so in June expect normal feeding and wading rather than dancing. The trail carries on to a white coral-sand beach where green turtles nest (December to May, so you're just past it, but turtles still feed in the shallows) and rays and reef sharks patrol.

Post Office Bay is a wooden barrel that has been an honour-system mail relay since 1793, set up by whalers as South America's first postal service. Leave an unstamped postcard; sift the pile for one addressed near home and hand-deliver it. A short trail runs to a lava tube you descend by ladder. Most itineraries also fit Enderby or Champion Islet — the last refuge of the Critically Endangered Floreana mockingbird, fewer than 200 birds left on earth.

Two things a day-tripper cannot have. Devil's Crown — the semi-submerged cone widely rated among the best snorkels anywhere — is cruise-access only; a Santa Cruz day boat won't reach it. And February 2026 saw 158 Floreana-lineage giant tortoises returned to the island for the first time in over 180 years, but they were released into remote highland habitat and are GPS-tracked, not positioned for visitors. The breeding corral at Asilo de la Paz is where you actually photograph tortoises here.

Or stay the night

For a photographer this is the better version. An overnight at the Wittmer family's lodge or a village homestay buys you golden hour at black-sand Playa Negra, unhurried time with the sea lions and marine iguanas that haul out at the pier, and a walk-in snorkel bay where green turtles graze within arm's reach.

The catch: the public inter-island ferry runs only some days of the week — the scheduled lancha is Tuesday and Thursday, out 08:00–09:45 and back 15:00–17:00 — and both the lodge and the homestays fill. Confirm the timetable at the Puerto Ayora dock before you commit.

Two more, if the boats are full

South Plaza & Santa Fé

Full-day boat from Puerto Ayora · both under an hour out

Neither is a headline, and both are genuinely good — worth knowing because North Seymour and Bartolomé sell out first. South Plaza is the better one for a photographer: a tiny cliff-edge island where you shoot seabirds in flight from above, which you can't do anywhere else on this itinerary.
