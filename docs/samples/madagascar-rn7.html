The Red Road

Route Nationale 7 — Antananarivo to Toliara

Nine hundred and fifty kilometres from a highland capital at 1,280 metres to a Vezo fishing beach on the Mozambique Channel — through rice terraces, rainforest, granite, sandstone canyon and spiny forest, in the clearest month of the year.

You will smell the road before you see it. Laterite dust, eucalyptus smoke, charcoal, and the sweet-sour steam of vary being turned in a pot behind a chalkboard menu. The RN7 leaves Antananarivo through a canyon of red-brick houses with wooden balconies, climbs onto a plateau quilted with rice terraces, and then just keeps going south — past hand-pulled rickshaws in Antsirabe, woodcarvers' alleys in Ambositra, a rainforest where a lemur was discovered in 1986, a Wednesday market where two thousand zebu change hands, and a sandstone massif eroded into towers, before it finally runs out of altitude and hits the sea at Toliara.

It is Madagascar's one great overland route, and September is the month to drive it. This is the tail of the cool dry season: minimal rain, firm tracks, clear skies for the Isalo sunset, active lemurs, and — this year and every year — the closing weeks of famadihana, the highland ancestor ceremony that by custom and law happens only between June and September.

Two weeks is exactly right for it. Not because the distance demands it — you could sprint Tana to Toliara in three long days — but because the RN7 is a road of stops, and the driving days are slower than the map suggests. Below is a fourteen-night shape that gives every stop enough time to stop being a place you drove through.

You will be cold before you are hot

The single practical fact that shapes packing: the RN7 descends 1,280 metres over its length, and September nights at altitude are genuinely cold while the coastal finish is genuinely hot. Antsirabe can touch freezing before dawn; on the Andringitra plateau above 2,000 m, the national park's own guidance warns that April–September nights drop well below zero. Four days later you'll be swimming in a 28°C lagoon.

Six things to settle before you go

Hire a car and driver, not a car. Every source on this route converges on it: self-driving isn't advised, the RN7's condition varies from decent tarmac to potholed crawl, and the good detours — Antoetra, Tsaranoro, Namoly, Salary — need a 4x4 and someone who knows the fords. A driver-guide also solves your hardest daily problem, which is finding a kitchen that can cook without meat.

Park guides are compulsory and cash-only. Ranomafana, Isalo, Andringitra and Zombitse all require a licensed local guide, booked at the park office — in Ranohira, book the evening before, because in high season the guides go early. Isalo's current published rates run 65,000 Ar per person for a day's entry (110,000 for two days) plus a ~5,000 Ar community charge and 80,000–120,000 Ar for the guide depending on trek length. Madagascar National Parks announced a doubling of entry fees for its "exceptional" parks, then postponed the rollout to November 2026 — a September trip should fall under the lower schedule, but reconfirm locally.

Distances lie. Antananarivo to Antsirabe is 170 km and takes most of a morning. Ambalavao to Ranohira is a long day. Ranohira to Toliara is a full transit day. Build the driving into the itinerary rather than treating it as the gap between days.

Photograph people only after asking. This matters more here than in most places: tombs, sacred stones, the zebu market, artisans at work, and above all a famadihana are not backdrops. Fady vary village to village; your guide is the interpreter.

Time the week, not just the month. Two things run on a weekly clock and are worth routing around: Ambalavao's zebu market (Wednesday is the main day, with a smaller Thursday morning) and the Fianarantsoa–Manakara train, which leaves only on Tuesdays and Saturdays.

Book the small lodges early. June–September is high season and the best places on this road are tiny — Setam Lodge, Tsara Guest House, Betsileo Country Lodge and Camp Catta's bungalows all have a handful of rooms between them.

The road's vocabulary

  • vary — rice; also "a meal". Mihinam-bary, "to eat rice", means to eat.
  • laoka — whatever accompanies the rice. The key word of your trip.
  • hotely — a small sit-down eatery with a chalkboard, not a hotel.
  • gargotte — an informal roadside food stall.
  • zebu — the humped cattle that are wealth, status, ceremony and dinner.
  • pousse-pousse — hand-pulled rickshaw; Antsirabe runs on them.
  • fady — ancestral taboo. Local, specific, not negotiable.
  • famadihana — the turning of the bones; June–September only.
  • lambda / lamba — the woven cloth; lamba arindrano is Ambalavao's silk.
  • mora mora — slowly, slowly. Also the national transport timetable.
  • vazaha — foreigner. You'll hear it, cheerfully, from every child.

Why this month

September is the month every window is still open

Nothing about the RN7 in September is a compromise. It is the driest, clearest, firmest month on the road — and it is also the closing weeks of four separate seasonal windows, three of which shut behind you in October.

Ring-tailed lemur babies

August – October · births cluster in the austral spring

Ring-tailed lemur births cluster in the austral spring, August into September, which means a September drive puts infants on their mothers' backs at the roadside reserves you are stopping at anyway. A visiting primatologist's advice for Isalo was blunt: come in September or October to raise your odds of seeing babies. At Anja, thirteen kilometres south of Ambalavao and directly on the RN7, mothers with twins clinging on are photographed regularly in this window.

The hour matters as much as the month. Lemurs shelter from the midday heat, so troops are up and moving across the granite early-to-mid morning and again mid-to-late afternoon — which happens to be when the light on those domes is worth having. Come October the infants are already riding less and exploring more.

June → September, by law

Famadihana, the turning of the bones

A family exhumes its ancestors, rewraps them in silk lamba and dances them around the tomb — brass band, rum, a feast. A joyful reunion, not a funeral. Dates are set family by family by an mpanandro, an astrologer, so none reach a calendar. Never photograph one uninvited; a well-connected guide can sometimes arrange a visit.

Mid-June → end September

Humpback whales, close the door behind them

The Anakao–Salary stretch is one of Madagascar's primary humpback breeding grounds. September is explicitly the closing month — go out by pirogue in your first days on the coast, not your last. A Vezo community association in Anakao trains the guides and runs the trips.

Wednesday, every week

Ambalavao's zebu market

Up to two thousand cattle, walked in from villages days away, traded on a fenced hillside from around 10:00 to 16:00, with a smaller Thursday-morning session. Sources differ on the exact rhythm — confirm with your driver, because there is no second chance until next week.

September → November, peaks October

Jacaranda, but only just

Tana's purple-flowered avenues and the shore of Lac Anosy are the city's most-photographed sight — and they peak in October. In September, treat blossom as a bonus rather than the picture you came for.

The question you asked

Is vegetarian survivable in Madagascar? Yes — but it is managed, not assumed

Every first-hand account of this road converges on the same verdict. A filling, genuinely good meat-free diet is achievable at every single stop on the RN7 — but menus rarely spell it out, meat signals hospitality, and the traps are in the broth rather than on the plate. Learn six dishes and one sentence and you will eat well for two weeks.

The unlock is one Malagasy word: laoka

A Malagasy plate is not a main course with sides. Rice — vary — is the meal; the verb for eating a meal, mihinam-bary, literally means "to eat rice." Everything else on the table is laoka: the accompaniment. And on the highland plateau a laoka is defined by its sauce — a tomato-onion-ginger-garlic base — not by its protein. The same base without the meat is a vegetable laoka.

That is why asking for "the laoka without meat" is an everyday request here rather than a special favour, and why the countryside is often on your side: in rural areas, meals are frequently vegetable-only by tradition and economics, before anyone asks. As one Madagascar destination expert put it on a travel forum, you will not walk in and find a printed vegetarian menu — but "commonly available nearly everywhere are the sort of things you've mentioned, like sautéed vegetables with rice." A driver-guide quoted on the same forum was blunter: "there is always a vegetable-based accompaniment to go with rice and lots of eggs available, so being a vegetarian (or even vegan) in Madagascar isn't a problem."

Both are true. The difference between them is effort — and a two-week road trip gives you plenty of chances to get good at it.

The rhythm that actually works on this road

Fend for yourself at lunch, eat at the lodge at night. Buy avocados, bananas, peanuts, tomatoes and fresh bread at each morning's market — fruit on this road is startlingly cheap — and treat roadside lunch as a bonus rather than a requirement. Then have your driver phone ahead to that night's lodge before you arrive. Ranomafana, Tsaranoro, Namoly, Anakao and Salary all run fixed set-menu dinners with no alternative kitchen within reach, and every one of them will cook around you given notice. Notice is the whole trick.

Seven ways meat gets in without appearing on the plate

  1. The rice itself. Rice is not automatically vegetarian. Vary sosoa, the standard breakfast porridge, is traditionally eaten with dried meat and can be cooked in its rendered fat or a meat broth. Ask how the rice was made — especially at breakfast.
  2. Bouillon cubes. Malagasy home and hotely cooking leans hard on Maggi/Knorr-style cubes for depth. A plate of plainly sautéed vegetables may still be seasoned with a meat-based cube. « Est-ce que c'est cuit avec un bouillon cube ? »
  3. Kitoza as a garnish. Smoked, dried zebu strips get diced small into fried or vegetable rice and are often not filed mentally under "meat" by the cook. Ask even about dishes that read as vegetable-forward.
  4. Broth as cooking liquid. Ron'akoho (chicken broth) and other ro are used to simmer beans, greens and rice for flavour. A bean stew that looks vegetarian may have been built on one.
  5. Lard as the default fat. Ravitoto's silkiness usually comes from pork fat rendered into the leaves, not oil; ramanonaka, a fried rice cake, is fried in lard. « Sans graisse de porc. »
  6. Shared-plate bleed. At a hotely serving from one big platter, the meat sauce soaks into everything. Ask for your rice and vegetables plated separately.
  7. Dried shrimp and fish, filed under "not meat." Patsa (tiny dried shrimp) turns up inside leafy-green dishes — sweet-potato leaves stewed with dried shrimp is a documented highland staple. If you avoid fish too, say so explicitly and separately, every time.

Order these — they work almost everywhere

  • Lasary lasaryMarinated or pickled vegetable salad — tomato, carrot, cabbage, cucumber. The single most reliable vegetarian dish in the country. Ask for it even when it isn't on the board; it usually arrives anyway.
  • Ravitoto tsy misy hena ravitotoPounded cassava leaves, dark and almost creamy, sometimes finished in coconut milk. Genuinely vegetarian when made without the pork — but you must specify, because the default isn't.
  • Voanjobory / tsaramasoBambara-groundnut and bean stews. Filling, protein-rich, and traditionally cooked plain in a great many households. The best actual dinner on this list.
  • Minsao légumes min saoWok-fried noodles with vegetables — a legacy of Chinese railway migration. The safest order on any menu in any town of any size.
  • Vegetable omeletteAvailable at even the most basic hotely, and the protein fallback for every lunch stop between towns.
  • Mofo gasy & kobaBreakfast: sweet rice-flour fritters off a charcoal griddle (morning only, gone by mid-morning) and dense peanut-banana rice cake sold in slices from market baskets. Both vegetarian — except ramanonaka, which is fried in lard.

Leg 1 · 2 nights · Antananarivo

The city of a thousand stairs

Tana is built on twelve sacred hills and behaves like it: everything worth seeing is up a staircase, and the staircase is the point. Give it two nights — one to recover from the flight, one to actually see it — because this is also the last place for two weeks where a vegetarian can order literally anything.

The shape of the day that works: start high. Walk the Haute-Ville from the Rova down through the presidential quarter to Andohalo, take the long stone flight known as the thousand stairs of Tana, and land — slightly stunned — in the roar of Analakely market at the bottom. That single descent is the most photogenic sequence in the city and it orients you completely. Then flatten out: Lac Anosy and its gold WWI monument, a coffee on a balcony over Avenue de l'Indépendance, and an hour in the craft market before dinner.

Save the second day for Ambohimanga, the sacred royal hill 21 km north-east — the pre-colonial Merina capital, and unlike the fire-gutted Rova, substantially original 18th-century buildings. Go in the morning: the traffic out of Tana can eat two hours in each direction if you leave it late. If you want a lemur head-start before the parks, Lemurs' Park at Katsaoka, 22 km south-west on the RN1, keeps nine species semi-free across five hectares of rescued animals; the guided walk runs 90 minutes and about 70,000 Ar, and it slots into the same day as Ambohimanga if you start early.

Up the hill and down the stairs

The lower town, and the royal hill north

Where to eat — and stock up

Tana eats early by international standards: sakafo antoandro, lunch, is the day's main event and hotely fill by noon, while local dinner runs roughly 18:30–20:30. Reservations are pointless at a hotely and expected at the city's sit-down French kitchens. Somewhere in these two days, do all three of: a proper Malagasy plate (romazava with its faintly tingling brèdes mafana greens, ravitoto, voanjobory), a market breakfast of hot mofo gasy off the griddle with condensed-milk coffee, and one dinner that counts as a honeymoon dinner.

Where to sleep

The choice is really between charm and logistics. Haute-Ville and Isoraka put you inside the colonial stone-and-jacaranda quarter within walking distance of dinner and the old town — the atmospheric option, at the cost of Tana's notorious traffic. Ivato, out by the airport, is where most RN7 trips actually stage from, because it turns a dawn departure into something humane. Two nights here is the classic split: one in town, one by the airport.

170km

Leg 2 · 2 nights · Antsirabe & Ambositra

Rickshaws, crater lakes and carved wood

The RN7's opening act is the coolest, most European-feeling stretch of the whole route: 1,500-metre air, wide colonial avenues, thermal springs, volcanic lakes, and the two towns where Madagascar's most famous craft traditions are made by hand in back rooms.

The drive: 170 km to Antsirabe, paved but potholed in stretches, and most of a morning. Ask your driver to pull over — properly pull over, not slow down — where the terraced rice paddies open out on both sides; this is Madagascar's defining landscape and shooting it through a windscreen wastes it. Ambositra is a further 90 km south.

Antsirabe was built around its hot springs and still calls itself the Ville d'Eau. It has one of the highest concentrations of hand-pulled pousse-pousse in the country, hand-painted and built in dedicated workshops, and a slow open-air ride through the grid of 1920s avenues is genuinely the most romantic hour on this leg. Agree the fare before you set off. Between rides, the town's small ateliers are worth an hour as a set: a zebu-horn workshop where horn is heated over a fire and polished into bowls and jewellery — one family business calls itself Les six frères — and a miniature workshop building model rickshaws and bush taxis out of tin cans.

Watch for, don't book

Around Ambositra you may see young men clinging to the humps of zebu bulls at the roadside. That is savika, a Betsileo rite of passage rather than a spectacle — organised bouts cluster in the October–December rice-planting season, so in September you'll catch informal practice and cattle being driven, which is the better version anyway.

The craft towns

Crater lakes and the volcanic country west of town

Antsirabe sits at the edge of the Vakinankaratra, a landscape of extinct craters, hot springs and terraces. The standard day loop runs Betafo for a rice-field walk, Lake Tritriva for the crater rim, then the gentler Lake Andraikiba on the way home — where locals picnic on Sundays, stalls sell gemstones, and there's a silk weaver to call in on. A certified local guide is now required at Tritriva rather than optional; reckon on roughly 15,000–20,000 Ar for the guide plus about 10,000 Ar entry, both visitor-reported rather than official.

Eating and sleeping on this leg

Both towns are one-night drive-breaks rather than bases — Ambositra especially. Antsirabe has the leg's only real restaurant choice, and it is also where a vegetarian should eat properly before Ambositra's thinner options; the stop-by-stop table above has the specifics. Souimanga's kitchen leans organic and sustainable; Hotel Artisan in Ambositra runs the town's best table and organises the Zafimaniry treks itself.

Leg 3 · 2 nights · Ranomafana National Park

Where the highlands fall into rainforest

Turn east off the RN7 and the plateau simply stops. Ranomafana is 41,600 hectares of moss-hung submontane cloud forest on the seam between Betsileo rice country and Tanala forest country — a park that exists because a primatologist found a lemur here in 1986 that science didn't know about.

This is the stop that most rewards a second night, and the reason is timing rather than distance. Lemurs are most active at dawn; the nocturnal species — mouse lemurs, leaf-tailed geckos and a startling number of chameleons — only appear after dark, on a separate guided walk along the road bordering the park (you are not permitted inside at night). One night gets you one of those. Two gets you both, plus the hot spring the town is named for.

Everything here runs on cash and on guides. Guides are mandatory, booked at the park office, and paid separately from entry — in ariary only, and there is no ATM in the village, so arrive with the whole stay's money. Trails are steep and slick even in the dry months: budget more time than the distances suggest and wear real shoes.

Where to sleep — and the one call to make first

Ranomafana's properties trade polish for position: expect simple rooms with extraordinary views rather than five-star finish. They also serve fixed set-menu dinners with little choice, which is precisely why this is the stop to phone ahead about. Hôtel Thermal states outright that its chef adapts each day's menu to dietary restrictions if you mention it at booking — so mention it at booking.

410km

Leg 4 · 1 night · Fianarantsoa & Sahambavy

A hill town, a jungle railway and a tea lake

"Fianar" is the cultural capital of Betsileo country and the best-preserved old town on the road: some five hundred houses built between 1870 and 1900 still lining cobbled streets on a hill, with roughly fifty churches among them. One night, and make the evening walk the point of it.

The drive: two hours back west from Ranomafana on the RN25 — well surfaced, extremely curvy — and you rejoin the RN7. Fianarantsoa is built in three tiers and only the top one matters for an evening: the Haute-Ville, a warren of steep cobbled lanes and brick houses with wooden balconies, recognised by the World Monuments Fund, and best walked around Sunday morning when the churchgoing crowds come down the stone stairways in their best clothes.

Two things make this stop more than a stopover. The first is the FCE railway, a metre-gauge line hacked through the escarpment between 1926 and 1936, which drops 163 km to the Indian Ocean at Manakara across 67 bridges and 48 tunnels, stopping at villages with no road at all. The full ride is a full day at best and a genuine expedition at worst — it lives in the add-ons below. But the yellow-and-red station is worth twenty minutes on its own, and the line's first real stop is the second thing: Sahambavy, Madagascar's only tea plantation, twenty minutes out of town, where the red laterite turns abruptly to rolling green terraces around a small lake. Entry to the estate is about 5,000 Ar; you can walk the terraces, tour the withering-and-rolling factory, and taste at the end. If you want one extra-romantic night on this half of the road, sleep out over the water at the Lac Hôtel rather than in town.

A glass of Betsileo wine, and dinner

Fianarantsoa is, improbably, Madagascar's wine capital — French colonists planted vines in the hills here in the 1920s, and the Lazan'i Betsileo cooperative, founded in 1971, now controls roughly half the country's vine acreage; a Cistercian monastery at Maromby north of town has made wine since 1958. The quality is rustic rather than refined and visiting arrangements are informal, so ask your guide who is currently receiving visitors. The most concrete option is Clos Malaza, a working 30-hectare estate reached by 11 km of RN7 and then 20 km of dirt track through Betsileo villages.

465km

Leg 5 · 2–3 nights · Ambalavao, Anja & the Tsaranoro Valley

Zebu, paper, ring-tails, and 800 metres of granite

South of Fianarantsoa the plateau tips into the dry south, and this is the densest, most varied leg on the road: a cattle market out of another century, two crafts you can watch being made, the easiest wild lemurs in Madagascar, and — an hour's rough track further — one of the great mountain landscapes on the island.

Ambalavao itself is a one-morning town with two things in it worth going out of your way for. The zebu market is the more famous: Madagascar's second-largest, up to two thousand cattle walked in from villages days away and traded on a fenced hillside on Wednesdays, with a smaller Thursday-morning session and impromptu zebu rodeos between trades. It is loud, dusty, thoroughly untouristy, and it only happens on its day — route the leg around it. The Antemoro paper workshop behind the Aux Bougainvillées hotel is quieter and, in its way, more remarkable: bark from the wild avoha mulberry boiled for six hours, pounded to pulp, spread by hand on cloth-covered frames and pressed with dried wildflowers while still wet. It's free to visit, tips appreciated, and it's the surface this guide is designed to look like. Ambalavao also weaves the lamba arindrano, a large hand-spun silk cloth patterned with geometric motifs drawn from southern funerary monuments; the same shops sell it.

Ambalavao and the ring-tails

The detour worth two nights: Tsaranoro and Andringitra

Thirty-five kilometres south of Ambalavao a signed turnoff leaves the RN7, and twenty kilometres of track later you are in a broad valley of rice paddies ringed by sheer granite walls up to 800 metres high — Tsaranoro, Chameleon and Karambony. Climbers come from all over the world for these faces. You don't have to climb anything: the valley walks, village visits, and the half-day hike up Chameleon Mountain for the view over the whole massif are the reason to come, and ring-tailed lemurs live around the camps.

Behind it rises Andringitra National Park — 31,160 hectares of granite domes and three ecozones stacked into one massif, with the highest climbable peak in Madagascar, Pic Boby (renamed Imarivolanitra, "close to the sky," by locals who found the colonial name — after a dog — disrespectful of a sacred mountain) at 2,658 m. Hilary Bradt called the walk here one of the most beautiful mountain walks she had ever done, and noted that at 2,000 metres "the landscape takes on a Disneyland quality, with rock-gardens of colourful succulents against a backdrop of craggy granite peaks."

The honest verdict for a honeymoon. The full Pic Boby trek is three days and two nights from Ambalavao, with a 4am summit start and — in September — sub-zero nights at altitude, which the park authority says outright requires an overnight up high. It is magnificent and it is not restful. The version we'd actually plan for this trip: two nights at Camp Catta or Tsarasoa in the Tsaranoro Valley, one gentle day walk to the Chameleon viewpoint, the plunge pool with the cliff behind it, and Anja on the way in or out. You get the granite, the lemurs, the Betsileo villages and the solitude — both Bradt and a first-hand trekking account independently remark on how few other travellers are here — without the cold and the 4am.

Andringitra, if you do want the summit

  • Two bases, not connected by road. Namoly (~47 km / 3 hrs of 4WD track from Ambalavao) for Pic Boby; Tsaranoro (~2.5 hrs) for the cliffs and easier walking.
  • Six official circuits. Asaramanitra, 6 km / ~4 hrs, moderate, to the sacred twin waterfalls Riandahy and Riambavy — the best half-day. Diavolana, 8 km / ~10 hrs, the "lunar" high plateau with cold-adapted ring-tails. Imarivolanitra, 28 km round trip over two walking days.
  • A guide is mandatory and arranged at the Namoly or Morarano park office, or through an Ambalavao operator. Porters and a cook can be added.
  • Rough guide-fee benchmark: ~€15 half-day / ~€33 full-day for a group of up to four, plus ~€12 for porters — an operator's figure, not the official rate card. Get park fees from the office.
  • September means below freezing above 1,900 m. One August visitor packed "two layers of fleece, a beanie for the nights and thermal underwear" — and T-shirts and swimming trunks for the days.
  • Access closes when the rains start in November; the tracks in can become impassable.

Where to sleep on this leg

A genuine fork. Stay on the RN7 near Ambalavao if you're treating this as a one-night market-and-lemurs stop, or go into the Tsaranoro Valley for two nights and make the mountains the point. The valley camps are rustic — bungalows and comfort tents rather than luxury, with reed-filtered pools and resident lemurs — and both ask you to pre-order meals a day ahead, which for once works entirely in a vegetarian's favour.

700km

Leg 6 · 2 nights · Isalo National Park

A Jurassic massif eroded into towers

A hundred kilometres of Jurassic sandstone rising out of the southern plains, cut into canyons and ruiniforme pinnacles and hiding palm-shaded pools in its clefts. One first-hand visitor compared its canyon directly to Zion's Narrows and gave Isalo the edge: "larger canyons, more vegetation and lemurs. Zion National Park does not have lemurs."

The drive: long. Ambalavao to Ranohira via Ihosy is a full day, and the landscape changes completely on the way — terraces give out to savannah, cactus and the great pale flank of the Andringitra massif off to the east. Ranohira itself is a one-street RN7 truck-stop: buy phone credit, change money, and stay outside it. The good lodges are all a few kilometres out, tucked into the rock.

Two nights lets you split the park's classic seven-hour circuit into two comfortable half-days instead of one long push — ask your guide directly about doing that, because a honeymoon does not need a 7-hour day in 35°C heat. Morning one: the plateau viewpoints, the crest above Ranohira, and the descent to the Piscine Naturelle. Morning two: Canyon des Makis or the Namaza gorge with its black and blue pools and the Cascade des Nymphes. Both evenings: the Window.

Two things your guide will tell you, worth knowing first

Book the guide the evening before. In high season the Ranohira park office runs out of guides by morning, and everything inside Isalo requires one. And the canyons are not trails. There is no path in Canyon des Makis — it is rock-to-rock scrambling beside a river, which is exactly why it's good. Bring water; Isalo's heat, not its gradient, is what makes the hiking moderate.

Inside the massif

Sandstone, sapphires and the drive out

Three roadside stops bookend the park, and all three are free or nearly so. The Window and the Queen of Isalo — an anthropomorphic rock that looks like a crowned woman, and appears on the 1,000-ariary banknote — both sit outside the fee zone on the road south, 12 and 17 km from Ranohira. Then, 26 km on, Ilakaka: a village that went from a handful of houses to over 30,000 people after sapphires were found here in 1998, and now supplies something close to half the world's sapphires. And on the last stretch toward Toliara, the forest-corridor park at Zombitse-Vohibasia — one of the very few places on Earth where baobabs and octopus-limbed Alluaudia grow side by side.

Eating and sleeping at Isalo

Unexpectedly, this is the best-prepared stop on the road for a vegetarian — three separate kitchens here are specifically recommended for it. The lodges are also the best on the RN7 full stop: stone architecture built into the massif, pools, spas, horse riding, and dark skies. One lodge's own pitch mentions "some of the clearest astronomical views on the island" from its terrace; September is dry and clear, so bring a tripod. Night walks inside the park are not permitted, so stargazing happens from the lodge — which is no hardship at all.

950km

Leg 7 · 3 nights · Toliara, Ifaty & Anakao

The road runs out of altitude

This is the payoff leg, and the instruction is simple: stop moving. Behind the beach lies the world's fifth-largest coral reef — 450 km of fringing, patch and barrier reef running from Morombe to Itampolo — creating a lagoon so sheltered you can snorkel it off the sand. In front of it, humpbacks in their closing month of the season.

The drive: a full transit day from Ranohira. Toliara, when you reach it, is a hot, sun-bleached working port — the RN7's terminus rather than its reward. Almost nobody stays: you transfer straight out to the beach. That gives you a genuine three-way choice for the last three nights.

Ifaty and Mangily, 25 km north by road, are the easy option — a merged strip of beachfront bungalow resorts right behind the barrier reef, with the most dining choice, the dive shops, and pirogues heading out at dawn. Anakao, south, is the quiet one and reachable only by speedboat from Toliara port (roughly 45–90 minutes, around 70,000 Ar each way, and you're carried the last stretch ashore over the sandbar by tractor platform or ox-cart). No road, no vehicles, no ATM, solar power on a schedule, bucket showers even at the comfortable lodges — and, in exchange, a real Vezo fishing village on seven kilometres of white sand facing an uninhabited island. Salary, 100 km north over three hours of sand track, is the end-of-the-line option for total disconnection.

Our pick for a two-week honeymoon finish: one night Ifaty, two nights Anakao. Ifaty gets you onto the reef the afternoon you arrive with no boat transfer to organise; Anakao gets you the sunset, the pirogues, Nosy Ve and the whales. And do the whale trip early in your stay — September is the last month of the season, and open-water pirogue trips are weather-dependent, so you want a spare day in hand.

The reef, the spiny forest and the whales

Baobabs, pirogues and the culture of this coast

The Vezo are the texture of the whole coast: semi-nomadic, outrigger-sailing, free-diving, building pirogues whose sails are now often stitched from rice sacks. The fleet goes out around dawn and is back by midday, and the catch is sold straight off the sand. The best way to meet that culture isn't a tour — it's staying somewhere small and eating what the family cooks.

Inland, the landscape flips to spiny forest: baobabs and cactus-like endemics found nowhere else. Note one expectation to reset — the famous Avenue of the Baobabs is near Morondava on the west coast, on an entirely different route (it's in the add-ons). What you get here is a genuine but visually different baobab landscape, and it's arguably the better photographic subject for being less photographed.

On the final RN7 approach into Toliara, slow down for the tombs. The Mahafaly and Antandroy build monumental painted stone tombs topped with real zebu horns denoting the wealth of the dead, and carved wooden aloalo posts that narrate a life — traditionally ibis and zebu motifs, but modern ones depict bush taxis, aeroplanes and contemporary life. These are roadside sights, best seen from a slowing car rather than approached.

Where to eat

Toliara has the cosmopolitan kitchens — French and Italian-run, with the best vegetarian handling on the coast — while Ifaty's are beachfront and seafood-led and Anakao's are family front rooms. The local specialty to know about is lobster in a vanilla-infused sauce, which is the one thing on this coast worth breaking a diet for if only one of you is vegetarian.

Add-ons · not in the fourteen nights

Four things the core route leaves out

The RN7 is a spine, and four genuinely major places hang off it. None fits inside a two-week drive without cutting something — but you should see them before you decide what you're skipping. They're on the map below as dashed side-branches.

Add one day · from Ambositra

Antoetra and the Zafimaniry villages

4x4 + guide · 1.5–2 hrs each way, then on foot · full day

Everything carved that you see for sale in Ambositra is actually made up here. Beyond the roadhead village of Antoetra — 40 km south-east, the last 20 on dirt — lies a scatter of roughly a hundred villages in forested hills, reachable only on foot, where houses, tombs and furniture are assembled entirely by mortise-and-tenon joinery, no nails, and finished with geometric carved shutters called sokitra. UNESCO inscribed the Zafimaniry's woodcrafting knowledge on the Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2003. It's the pattern running down the edge of this page.

A two-hour uphill walk from Antoetra reaches Ifasina, which is the honest day-trip version; multi-day treks continue to Sakaivo and beyond. On arrival, custom requires that you visit the village elder first to ask permission — often identifiable by the one carved door with a bird motif — and small courtesy offerings are customary at the stone ancestor stelae (tsangambato) along the trail. A guide is not optional here: they handle the protocol, the translation and the elder's entry fee. One first-hand account describes the drive out as "a kaleidoscope of greens in the perfectly carved terraces," and the arrival as "total immersion in a true artistic culture."

💡 A caution worth carrying: the widely-repeated claim that each carved motif has a fixed symbolic meaning — spider-web for family ties, honeycomb for community — traces to a single carpenter's tourist-facing commentary rather than older ethnography, which records the Zafimaniry's own explanation as simply that it is beautiful, and that this is how the ancestors did it. Enjoy the pattern; hold the symbolism lightly.

Add three to four days · from Fianarantsoa

The FCE railway down to the Indian Ocean

Tue & Sat, 07:00 departure · 8–24 hrs · 70,000 Ar first class

The most-loved and least-reliable journey in Madagascar. The Fianarantsoa–Côte Est line drops 163 km from the highland plateau to the coast at Manakara across 67 bridges and 48 tunnels and down one of the steepest railway inclines in the world, through jungle valleys and terraced paddies, stopping at villages with no road access at all — which is why every station becomes a market as the train pulls in, with bananas, crayfish, persimmons and samosas passed up through the windows. The Guardian described the route as teetering "along viaducts… through steep-sided jungle valleys." One traveller's account is more to the point: they left Fianarantsoa at 7am and reached Manakara at 3am the following morning.

Two hard constraints. It runs only on Tuesdays and Saturdays from Fianarantsoa (returning Wednesdays and Sundays from Manakara), so your whole RN7 schedule has to land you in Fianarantsoa the night before one of those. And it is genuinely unpredictable: three secondhand ex-Renfe locomotives from 1964, a history of months-long closures after cyclone damage, and delays measured in hours. Confirm with your hotel that it isn't en panne a day or two ahead.

How we'd do it: ride it one-way, then drive back. A full rail round trip costs four days of a fourteen-day trip in pure transit; riding down on a Tuesday or Saturday, spending a night or two at Manakara — where local guides run pirogue-and-bivouac trips on the Canal des Pangalanes, the 600 km colonial waterway running parallel to the coast — and then returning to Fianarantsoa by road on the paved RN25/RN27 in five to seven hours costs you three. Book first class; it's the difference between an adventure and an endurance test.

💡 For the vegetarian: there is no dining car, and the vendor food at stops skews toward fish and meat snacks. Buy bread, avocados, peanuts and fruit in Fianarantsoa the evening before, and take more water than you think you need. Two detailed 2026 reviews also report serious foodborne illness after multi-day Pangalanes bivouac trips with the best-known local guide — if you do the canal, prefer a one-night version and drink only sealed bottled water.

Add two days · east of Antananarivo, on the RN2

Andasibe-Mantadia — the loudest lemur on Earth

3–4 hrs on paved RN2 · 2 nights · bolt onto either end of the RN7

The one add-on we'd argue for hardest, because it gives you an animal the RN7 cannot. The indri is the largest living lemur, and its call — an eerie, rising wail somewhere between whale song and a siren — carries up to three kilometres through the forest. Analamazaotra's boardwalk trails are the most reliable place on the island to hear and see them, and they are gentle, short walks rather than a trek. Indris call mainly between dawn and 10am, so go out with the first guided group of the day; this is non-negotiable, and it's the whole reason to sleep here rather than day-trip.

September is also, as it happens, the start of orchid season: the forest holds over a hundred species blooming September through November, and the community association Mitsinjo runs an eleven-hectare orchid park for exactly this window. Mitsinjo is also one of the only places locally still permitted to run night walks — no longer allowed inside the national park itself — which is where the mouse lemurs, sleeping chameleons, leaf-tailed geckos and a hundred-odd frog species live.

    Analamazaotra Special Reserve (Andasibe / Périnet)

    Easy boardwalk circuits through montane forest; indri family groups, sifakas, chameleons, heavy birdlife. The dawn walk is the trip.

    1–4 hrs · guide mandatory · go with the first group

    Mantadia National Park

    The wilder, steeper primary-forest half, 20 km north — old growth, buttress roots, waterfalls, and a real chance at diademed sifaka and black-and-white ruffed lemur. One guide tracked a family for four hours before a sighting.

    Full day · separate vehicle + guide hire · not a boardwalk

    Peyrieras Nature Farm, Marozevo

    On the RN2 you're already driving. A curated close-up of dozens of chameleon species, long-nosed Langaha snakes, comet moths and semi-wild sifakas — travellers use it as a study session before spotting the real thing.

    ~2 hrs guided · at the foot of the Mandraka pass

💡 Sequence it after the RN7 rather than before, if you can. Two gentle forest days and a lodge with a pool make a much better decompression from 950 km of road than they do a warm-up for it.

Add six to seven days · the west coast

The Avenue of the Baobabs and the Tsingy de Bemaraha

Fly Tana–Morondava ~1h10 · then 4x4 only · 6–7 days minimum

The two most photographed things in Madagascar are both out here, and neither is on the RN7 — which is the single most important expectation to correct before you plan. This is a full expedition bolted onto an expedition, and in a two-week trip it replaces the RN7 rather than supplementing it. In a three-week trip, it's extraordinary.

The Allée des Baobabs is a 260-metre stretch of the RN8 twenty kilometres from Morondava, lined with twenty-odd Adansonia grandidieri up to 30 metres tall and, by some accounts, 800 to 2,800 years old. Locally they're Renala, "mother of the forest," and offerings of rum and honey are still left at a nearby sacred baobab where ancestor spirits are believed to live. It's free, and everyone goes twice — sunrise around 5am and sunset around 5pm — because in the dry season the trees are leafless and the trunks turn orange-red against a clean sky. Four kilometres off and five down a side track stand the Baobabs Amoureux, two trunks grown twisted around each other: two young lovers from rival villages, forbidden to marry, who asked to stay together forever. It is, unavoidably, the most on-theme twenty minutes in the country for a honeymoon.

Then the hard part. Tsingy de Bemaraha — a UNESCO limestone karst "stone forest" whose name means walking on tiptoes — is 8 to 10 hours of dirt track, stream fords and two vehicle ferries north of Morondava, and the ferries leave only when enough vehicles have gathered. Once there, the Grand Tsingy circuit is a harnessed via ferrata through razor pinnacles, narrow rock corridors and a one-person-at-a-time suspension bridge; the gentler Petit Tsingy is approached by pirogue up the Manambolo past a limestone cave. Two full days in Bekopaka lets you do both unrushed. Break the drive at Kirindy, the dry forest where the fossa — Madagascar's largest carnivore — is close to guaranteed, and where a torch-lit night walk turns up Madame Berthe's mouse lemur, the world's smallest primate.

💡 Fly the Tana–Morondava leg rather than driving it. The overland alternative from Antananarivo is 700 km and reportedly a gruelling 15-hour-plus ride; the flight is about 1h10 and buys back days for the Tsingy itself. Bekopaka has no ATM and basic-but-comfortable lodges — set romantic expectations at Morondava's beachfront resorts on either end, not in the middle.
