The full loop · February 2027 · two adults, two kids (10 & 12)

ශ්‍රී ලංකාSri Lanka

Thirteen days, one island, and the whole shape of it: cave temples and a rock fortress in the dry-zone north, tea terraces and a train through the clouds, elephants at a shrinking waterhole, and a beginner's wave on a warm southern bay.

Sri Lanka is small enough to circle in a fortnight and varied enough that you will not believe you have stayed in one country. You land into wet heat and the smell of frangipani and diesel, and by the fourth morning you are barefoot on stone that has been walked on for two thousand years, climbing past a wall of fifth-century painted damsels with a queue of Sri Lankan schoolchildren. Three days later you are pulling a jumper on at dawn because you are nearly two kilometres up, in a town with a mock-Tudor post office and a racecourse, drinking tea grown on the slope below the window. Then the road tips you down through waterfalls to a dry-zone plain where a jeep idles at a waterhole while an elephant family drinks — and finally to a warm bay where the waves come in slow and shoulder-high and break onto sand, which is exactly what you want when a ten-year-old is standing up on a board for the first time.

February is the right month for almost all of it. The northeast monsoon has emptied itself out and this side of the island is in its dry season: brilliant skies over the Cultural Triangle, the clearest views of the year off the hill-country escarpments, and calm, 28°C water on the south coast. It is also peak blue-whale season off Mirissa and the tail of the best window for climbing rock in cool air. The trade-off is that this is high season — Sigiriya's staircases, the Nine Arch Bridge and Ella's viewpoints fill from mid-morning, and February hotel rates are the year's highest. Almost everything in this guide gets better if you are out by seven.

Two things about February will actually shape your plan. The first is which coast is in season: Sri Lanka has two monsoons running on opposite clocks, so in February the south and west are perfect and the east — Trincomalee, Arugam Bay — is emphatically not. That is why this loop runs down the middle and out to the south coast, and why nobody sensible drives to the east in February.

The second thing is altitude. This loop climbs from sea level to nearly 1,900 m and back down again in under a week, and the temperature swing catches families out. Nobody packs a fleece for Sri Lanka. In Nuwara Eliya in February you will want one, and Horton Plains at 2,100 m before dawn is genuinely cold.

Six things worth knowing before you book

Get a car and a driver. This is the normal way to do Sri Lanka and it is not a luxury: distances look tiny and take three times as long as you'd expect, the A-roads are slow, and with two kids the alternative is a lot of bus changes. Your driver becomes the trip's fixer — he books the jeeps, finds the lunch, knows which park has water in it.

The hill-country train is a real question mark. Cyclone Ditwah damaged most of the network in November 2025; as of the latest reporting the Nanu Oya–Ella–Badulla stretch is running again but the Kandy end is still cut. This changes how you route the hill country — the train section deals with it properly, including what to do instead.

Temples need covering up — all four of you. Shoulders and knees, at every Buddhist site including Sigiriya's summit temple, Dambulla and the Temple of the Tooth. Shoes come off, and the stone gets hot enough by ten to genuinely hurt: bring socks for everyone. Sarongs are rentable at the gates if you forget.

Book the big-ticket stuff early. February is peak: reserved train seats open 30 days ahead and go, dinner tables in Galle Fort and Ahangama need booking, and safari jeeps and whale boats want a day or two's notice.

Cash first, card second. Rupees for tuk-tuks, temple tickets, tips and small eateries; cards increasingly fine in Galle and at hotels. ATMs in every town on this route.

Don't drink the tap water — bottled or filtered throughout, and be a little careful with ice at the most casual roadside stalls.

What to pack

Days worth timing for

February is a quiet month in the Sri Lankan calendar — no Esala Perahera, no Sinhala New Year crush. But one night in it is arguably the best show in the country, and one date closes the banks.

Navam Maha Perahera, Colombo

Colombo's answer to Kandy's great procession, and the capital's biggest night of the year: two consecutive evenings when caparisoned elephants, Kandyan drummers, whip-crackers, dancers and fire performers parade around Gangaramaya Temple and Beira Lake, timed to the Navam full moon. If your dates can be nudged to catch it, do — for a ten- and twelve-year-old, elephants in gold cloth moving through a crowd at night is the kind of thing that outlasts every temple on the itinerary.

Timing it: the perahera runs the nights around Navam Poya, which for 2027 is expected on Saturday 20 February (the exact date is set by lunar calendar and gazetted closer to the time — verify with the temple before you build a trip around it; in 2026 it fell on 31 January–1 February). Expect heavy crowds and road closures around Beira Lake on both evenings; arrive early for a spot. Since Colombo is your arrival and departure city anyway, the cheap version of this plan is simply to put your city night on the right date.

The Poya rule Every full moon is a national public holiday, and alcohol is not sold anywhere in the country — hotels included. Temples fill with families dressed in white, which makes Dambulla or the Temple of the Tooth more atmospheric rather than less. Some small local restaurants shut; tourist-facing places carry on as normal. Plan the wine, not the sightseeing, around it.

The loop, anticlockwise

Thirteen nights, eight bases, one big clockface: north into the dry-zone ruins, up into the tea, down the escarpment to the safari plains, then west along the coast to Galle and back to the airport. Anticlockwise is the right direction — it puts the hard climbing early while you're fresh and jet-lagged-early-waking, and saves the beach for the end, when everybody needs it.

At the Sri Lankan table

Everything here is called "rice and curry," which tells you almost nothing. What arrives is a mound of rice ringed by five to eight small bowls — a dal, a coconut sambol, sautéed greens, a pumpkin or jackfruit curry, poppadoms, maybe fish — and it changes completely as you move round the loop. On the coast it is fired with chilli and built on the morning's catch; up in the tea country it turns mild and green, full of leeks and beetroot and carrots that only grow above 1,800 m because British planters put a kitchen garden on the mountainside. Eating your way round this island is a legitimate reason to do the loop in this order.

What to eat

Rice and curry බත්

The daily backbone: rice plus a rotating cast of small curries, dal, pol sambol and mallung. Eaten by hand, mixed together.

Kottu roti කොත්තු රොටි

Shredded flatbread chopped and griddled with egg, veg and meat by two metal blades — the tak-a-tak soundtrack of every Sri Lankan evening. The kids' gateway dish.

Hoppers ආප්ප

Bowl-shaped fermented rice-flour pancakes with lacy crisp edges, often with an egg dropped in the middle. String hoppers are the noodle-nest cousin.

Breakfast & dinner
Kiribath කිරිබත්

Rice cooked down in coconut milk, cut into diamonds, eaten with fiery lunu miris. A dish of auspicious beginnings — traditionally the 1st of the month.

Lamprais

The great Dutch-Burgher dish: rice cooked in stock with meat curry, brinjal moju, frikkadels and a boiled egg, all bundled into a banana leaf and baked so the flavours fuse.

Isso vade

A deep-fried lentil patty crowned with a whole prawn, invented by hill-country Tamil communities and now sold at every station and seafront on the island. Order it from a hawker, not a menu.

Highland vegetable curries

Leeks dry-fried with curry leaves and Maldive fish; carrot and cauliflower simmered in coconut milk with turmeric and cinnamon. Milder, greener, unmistakably hill country.

Kandy → Ella
Crab curry

Lagoon-caught mud crab in a coconut gravy — fierce and roasted-spiced in the Jaffna style, gentler in the south. The one splurge dish worth planning a dinner around.

Nuwara Eliya strawberries

Sri Lanka's only real strawberry belt, sold at roadside farms as punnets, milkshakes and waffles. An easy, unanswerable win with kids.

Nov–Mar
Ceylon tea

Planted in 1852 after coffee rust wiped out the previous crop; now the island's signature. Locals drink it milky and very sweet, many times a day.

When Sri Lankans actually eat

The single most useful thing to internalise: lunch, not dinner, is the main meal. Local "hotels" — which means casual eateries, not places to sleep — do their best trade between noon and one, and in smaller hill towns much of the town has shut its kitchen by half eight. Plan your big rice-and-curry for the middle of the day and eat lightly in the evening, and you'll both eat better and spend less.

Short eats: a colonial mash-up that became a national habit

Few countries have built an entire eating occasion — and an entire vocabulary — around what you nibble beside a cup of tea. "Short eats" is the umbrella term, and pulling it apart is a decent history of the island: the rolls descend from Chinese settler cooking (and don't exist in China), samosas and vadai came with South Indian labourers brought over to work the tea estates, pies and patties from the British and Dutch, and the cutlet is probably a descendant of the Portuguese bolinho, a salt-cod-and-potato croquette. Even the idea of a snack-with-tea rather than a meal is borrowed English afternoon tea.

What made them Sri Lankan was the 1940s: Colombo bakeries expanded their savoury counters to court wartime European clientele, Perera & Sons was selling a full range by the 1960s, and as the economy opened up in the 1980s short eats became the fast food of a busier urban island. The preference is still fiercely local — samosas and vadai rule in Pettah, where a chicken-and-mushroom pie reportedly "doesn't work as well." Order a mixed plate at a bakery at four o'clock and let everyone pick.

Eating with a 10- and 12-year-old

  • Spice is adjustable — "less spicy, please" is completely standard at every rice-and-curry buffet and nobody minds.
  • Hill-country curries are naturally milder than coastal ones. Ella and Nuwara Eliya are the easy landing.
  • Reliable safe harbours all round the loop: dosa and pure-veg South Indian, wood-fired pizza in Ella and Ahangama, brunch cafés on the south coast, gelato.
  • Sweet hoppers with jaggery, and plain rice with dal, are the two things that work for even the fussiest eater.
  • Guesthouse dinners in Ella and the Cultural Triangle usually need ordering by midday — and are often the best meal of the day.
Leg one

Negombo & Colombo

The bookends. Negombo is fifteen minutes from the airport and exists in this itinerary for one reason: a pool and a bed at the end of a long-haul flight, with a fishing town's worth of atmosphere thrown in for free. Colombo you save for the end, when you've earned a proper city dinner and the kids have stopped needing an early night.

The arrival evening, done properly

Do not schedule anything. What actually works with jet-lagged kids is a swim, then a slow walk on Negombo's beach at sunset with a bag of isso vade from a promenade vendor, then bed. Save the good stuff for the morning, when everyone is awake at five anyway and the town is at its best.

That good stuff is the Lellama fish market, where up to four thousand boats land their catch: outrigger canoes hauled up on the sand, wholesale auctions shouted across the concrete, and fish drying in sheets in the open air. The real frenzy is around five in the morning, but a nine o'clock visit still finds a working market, and it is the single most effective "we are definitely not at home" moment you can hand a child in the first twelve hours of a trip. It's closed on Sundays — most of Negombo's fishing families are Catholic and at church. Afterwards, the Hamilton Canal, dug by the Dutch, runs a gentle boat trip out into the lagoon and its mangroves — herons, monitor lizards, outriggers — and drops you at the blue-and-white St Mary's Church, one of the reasons the town gets called Little Rome.

One Colombo afternoon

If the last day gives you a half-day in the city, spend it on the one temple that will genuinely hold a twelve-year-old's attention. Gangaramaya is a working monastic school and a magpie's museum at the same time — gilded Buddhas and Thai and Burmese carving, and then a treasure-room of donated oddities including the world's smallest Buddha statue, which you need a magnifying glass to see. Next door, Geoffrey Bawa's Seema Malaka floats on Beira Lake, three open pavilions on a platform, silent after the temple's bustle.

Then go to Galle Face Green at five o'clock, which is not a sight at all but a 500 m strip of grass by the sea where the whole city comes to fly kites, eat off carts and watch the sun go down. It is the best free hour in Colombo.

Arrival vs departure Split them. Negombo for the arrival night — 15–20 minutes from the terminal, beachfront pools, nothing to organise. Colombo for the departure night if you want the city afternoon (45–60 minutes back to the airport on the E03 expressway), or Negombo again if you have a dawn flight. Mount Lavinia is the third option: 20 minutes south of the city and the one place in greater Colombo with a real swimming beach.
A note on elephant "orphanages" Pinnawala, near the Kandy road, is the most-suggested family day trip in the country and we have deliberately left it out. Independent accounts are genuinely split on welfare grounds — chained elephants, prods, breeding for tourism. You will see far more, far better, from a jeep at Minneriya or from the viewing platform at the Elephant Transit Home at Udawalawe, which is a government rehabilitation centre where calves are bottle-fed and later released, with no touching and no rides.

Negombo eats seafood, straight off the Lellama boats. Colombo is where the island's food layers are stacked closest together — Burgher lamprais, a 1884 bakery, and one of Asia's most famous crab restaurants, all within a few streets.

Where to stay

Leg two

Sigiriya, Dambulla & the ruined capitals

Three and a half hours north of the airport the air dries out, the palms give way to scrub and tanks, and the country's deep past starts sticking out of the jungle: a fifth-century palace on top of a two-hundred-metre plug of magma, a cave temple that has been in continuous use since the third century BC, and two abandoned capitals big enough to explore by bicycle. Give this three nights. It is the single densest, most extraordinary stretch of the loop, and it is also brutally hot by ten in the morning — which is the whole reason three nights beats two.

The two rocks

Everybody comes for Sigiriya. Almost everybody ends up saying the better morning was the other one. They are twenty minutes apart and you should do both — Pidurangala for sunrise on one day, Sigiriya at opening on another — because doing them back to back means a thousand steps twice before lunch with two children.

Look, don't shoot Halfway up Sigiriya a spiral staircase leads to the fresco gallery — the famous Sigiri Apsaras, painted damsels who have been on that rock face for fifteen hundred years — and the Mirror Wall below them, polished plaster covered in graffiti-poetry scratched by visitors from the eighth century onwards. Photography is prohibited at the gallery. It's the best five minutes of the climb anyway.
Two practical hazards Sigiriya has an occasional hornet problem serious enough to have closed the summit in the past — follow staff instructions, and don't shout near the nests on the upper staircases. And the monkeys at both Dambulla and Sigiriya will take food straight out of an open daypack; zip everything.

Temples, tanks and village life

Between the two climbs, the days here fall into an easy rhythm: something cultural in the cool of the morning, the pool in the middle of the day, and something out on the water or in a jeep in the late afternoon. Dambulla is the unmissable one — five caves under a granite overhang, 150-odd Buddhas, and 2,100 square metres of painted ceiling. It is a living pilgrimage site rather than a monument, so on a Poya day you'll climb alongside families dressed in white carrying lotus flowers, which is the version to see if you can time it.

February and the elephants — set expectations Minneriya and Kaudulla are famous for "The Gathering," when up to three hundred elephants converge on the shrinking reservoir. That peaks roughly June to October, not February. In February you will still very likely see elephants — resident herds are around both tanks year-round, and the access road itself regularly has them standing in it — but expect family groups of a dozen, not a hundred. Ask your driver or lodge on the day which park has water in it; they alternate, and Hurulu Eco Park is the third option. Go on the afternoon slot: cooler, better light, and the animals come out as the heat drops.

Two abandoned capitals

This is the reason to give the Triangle three nights rather than two: within easy driving distance are two of the great archaeological cities of South Asia, and they are completely different experiences. Do one of them properly. If your family likes bicycles and wants the more compact, more photogenic ruins, take Polonnaruwa. If you want the deeper, stranger, still-worshipped one, take Anuradhapura.

Polonnaruwa — the one to do by bike

Car or tuk-tuk · 50–90 min from Sigiriya · Full day

Sri Lanka's second capital, built between the 10th and 12th centuries, and a green, walled archaeological park rather than a single monument. The trick — and the thing that turns it from a hot trudge into the kids' favourite day of the trip — is to rent bicycles at the entrance for about LKR 400 and ride the loop, which takes four or five hours at a browsing pace. Be at the gate by eight or nine: the crowds build at ten and there is very little shade.

  1. 1

    Royal Palace & Council Chamber

    Thick brick walls of what was a seven-storey, thousand-chamber palace, and beside it a three-tiered stone platform carved with elephants, lions and dwarfs. Climbable, dramatic, easy to picture.

    First stop past the museum · 45 min
  2. 2

    The Sacred Quadrangle (Dalada Maluva)

    A dozen structures packed onto one raised platform, including the circular Vatadage that once housed the Buddha's tooth relic before it moved to Kandy, and the odd seven-stepped pyramid of the Sathmahal Prasadaya. Shoes off to climb it.

    The dense highlight · 1 hr
  3. 3

    Rankoth Vehera

    The largest dagoba here and the fourth largest in the country — a vast brick dome in excellent condition. Shoes come off on sand that is scorching by midday; this is a "get here early" stop.

    Free with site ticket · 30 min
  4. 4

    Lankatilaka

    Seventeen-metre brick walls, four metres thick, standing roofless around the remains of what was a thirteen-metre Buddha — it reads uncannily like a Gothic nave. Stand on the pillar slabs directly in front for the shot.

    The sleeper favourite · 30 min
  5. 5

    Gal Vihara — the finale

    Four colossal Buddhas — seated, standing, reclining, and one in a cut cavern — carved out of a single granite face, and widely held to be the finest rock sculpture in Sri Lanka. Save it for last. Shoes off, socks on, and don't turn your back on the Buddha for a photo.

    Unmissable · 45 min

Anuradhapura — the one still being worshipped

Car · ~1 hr from Habarana · Full day

The island's first capital, founded in the fourth century BC, and one of the most sacred places in the Buddhist world — but also forty square kilometres of it, hotter and far more spread out than Polonnaruwa. Do not try to walk it. Take a car and have the driver drop you at one end of each precinct and collect you at the other; that one decision is the difference between a great day and a miserable one. Go anticlockwise, start at sunrise or after four.

  1. 1

    Ruwanweli Maha Seya

    A blinding white stupa and the spiritual centre of the city, its courtyard full of barefoot, white-clad worshippers carrying lotus flowers. The most alive place in either ancient city.

    Barefoot · bring socks
  2. 2

    Lovamahapaya & the Sri Maha Bodhi

    1,600 stone pillars in a bare grid — all that's left of a nine-storey monastery roofed in bronze. Alongside it grows the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi, raised from a cutting of the tree the Buddha sat under, and tended for over two thousand years.

    Small separate fee at the Bodhi
  3. 3

    Jetavanaramaya

    Once among the tallest structures on earth, and still an astonishing amount of brick. Best in late-afternoon light, when the whole dome turns burnt orange.

    Late afternoon
  4. 4

    Abhayagiriya & the Samadhi Buddha

    A monastic complex swallowed by jungle — shaded, cool, good for kids — with the fourth-century Samadhi Buddha seated in meditation at the heart of it. This is where to be when the heat peaks.

    The midday move
  5. 5

    Kuttam Pokuna, then Tissa Wewa at sunset

    Two perfectly symmetrical stone bathing pools fed by an ancient filtration system — the quietest corner of the city. Finish with a stroll along the Tissa Wewa reservoir as the light goes.

    Free · end of the loop
💡 If you can only face one and want maximum payoff per hour of driving, take Polonnaruwa — it's closer, more compact, rideable, and Gal Vihara alone justifies the day. Anuradhapura rewards the family that likes atmosphere over monuments. Serious ruin-lovers can add Mihintale, 16 km east of Anuradhapura, where Buddhism landed on the island — 1,000-odd worn stone steps, barefoot, to a rock-top Buddha and the whole dry-zone plain laid out below.

Leg three

Kandy & the last kingdom

Kandy held out against the Portuguese and the Dutch and did not fall until 1815, which is why it still feels like the cultural capital of the island rather than a provincial city. It is built round a lake a king dug in 1807 and called the Milky Ocean, and on the lake's edge sits the single most important building in Sri Lankan Buddhism. February is kind here: 28°C, mostly clear, the beginning of the cool air.

The Temple of the Sacred Tooth Relic

Sri Dalada Maligawa — a sixteenth-century temple inside the old royal palace complex, holding what Buddhists believe is a tooth taken from the Buddha's funeral pyre and brought to the island in the fourth century. You will not see the relic: it sits in a gold casket behind a series of locked doors, and only a handful of people ever lay eyes on it. That is not the point.

The point is the puja. Three times a day the inner doors are opened for worship, and for those minutes the temple fills with hewisi drumming and horns, tables heaped with lotus and jasmine, and a slow press of white-clad worshippers kneeling in front of the shrine. Time your visit to a door-opening or you will have visited a building rather than a religion. The morning one, 9:30–11:00, is the easiest with children.

Afterwards, walk out of the gate onto the lake path — a three-kilometre shaded loop with the Queen's Bathing Pavilion on its edge, big monitor lizards on the banks and the white "Cloud Wall" running along one side. It is the best free hour in the city, and doing it at sunrise, with the temple chants carrying across the water, is better still.

Immediately behind the temple, Udawattakele was the private forest of the Kandyan kings, off limits to commoners on pain of death; today it is 104 hectares of genuine rainforest with trails, giant lianas, toque macaques and giant squirrels, five minutes from a city centre. And for the view of the whole valley, the 88-foot white Buddha at Bahirawakanda is a short steep climb or a two-minute tuk-tuk, best at sunset when the lake and temple light up below.

February in Kandy Kandy's own great festival, the Esala Perahera, is in July and August — so you miss it, but you also miss its prices and its crowds. What February gives you instead is the clearest hill-viewpoint weather of the year, and a temple busy with local worshippers rather than tour groups.

And when you want out of the city

The Three Temple Loop

If the family's interest in temples runs deeper than the headline act, this is the half-day that rewards it — three fourteenth-century Kandyan temples strung along a walkable loop of paddy fields and villages about thirty minutes south-west of the city, and almost nobody there. Go by tuk-tuk or car to Pilimathalawa and walk between them; entry is around LKR 300 each.

Gadaladeniya is the smallest and quickest — a stone temple with a whitewashed stupa perched on a rock. Lankatilaka Vihara (confusingly, it shares its name with the very different brick temple at Polonnaruwa) is the winner of the three: a whitewashed two-storey shrine standing on a bare rock outcrop with a panorama over the hills and rice fields, and an old painted wooden door worth the trip on its own. And Embekke Devale has the thing you'll remember — a long tile-roofed colonnade of wooden pillars carved with dancers, wrestlers, swans and double-headed eagles, some of the finest woodcarving in Sri Lanka, in even shaded light all day.

Where to eat

Kandy eats early — much of the city has closed its kitchen by eight — so make lunch the big meal here, as locals do. The two unmissable ones are cheap: a women-run food hall full of schoolchildren, and a rice-and-curry place one blogger called the most memorable meal of her Sri Lankan trip.

Where to stay

Two real choices. On the lake means walking to the temple, the puja and the dance shows, and skipping the daily tuk-tuk juggle with two kids — but most lake properties are boutique townhouses without much space to run around. Up in the hills (Heerassagala, Aniwatta, Nittawela) buys cooler air, valley views and proper pools for the afternoons, at the price of a 10–20 minute ride into town each way.

Leg four

Up into the tea

The A5 out of Kandy is one of the great drives: three to four hours of switchbacks climbing through waterfalls and then through tea, until the whole landscape is combed into terraces and the temperature has dropped fifteen degrees. At the top is Nuwara Eliya, founded by British colonists in the 1820s as a hill station and still doing a passable impression of Surrey — a mock-Tudor post office, a racecourse, a golf club, log fires, and a gentlemen's club that requires a jacket and tie for dinner.

The drive is the day

Don't rush this leg — the stops on the way up are the reason to drive rather than train it. About two hours out of Kandy the road passes Ramboda Falls, a 109-metre cascade you can see for free from a rooftop above a roadside shop (or pay a token amount at the hotel below for closer access to the base pool). Half an hour further, Damro Labookellie sits at around 1,900 m and runs a free guided walk through withering, rolling, fermenting and sorting, ending at a tasting table — twenty minutes long, exactly right for children, and the machinery is genuinely gripping. Closer to town, Pedro Tea Estate does the same thing on the Lover's Leap slope for about LKR 500, with pluckers working the rows outside.

In town, the two things worth doing are both easy. Gregory Lake, dammed in 1873, has a flat promenade, pedal boats and pony rides, and is where local families spend a Sunday — the February racing season hasn't started, so it's calm. And the red brick post office, built in 1894 with a Tudor gable and a clock spire, still works: buy a stamp and send the grandparents a postcard from Little England.

Then there's the strawberries. This is the only part of Sri Lanka cool enough to grow them, and the farms along the Hakgala–Ambewela road sell them by the punnet with milkshakes and waffles on the side. February is in season. It is the single most reliable bribe on the entire loop.

Horton Plains & World's End

The big one, and the reason to consider a second night here. Horton Plains is a 2,100–2,300 m plateau of montane grassland and cloud forest that stops dead at an escarpment dropping around 880 m into the valley — World's End. The loop trail is about 9 km and takes three to four hours, passing Mini World's End and Baker's Falls, on ground that is uneven with roots, stone steps and boggy patches. Sambar deer graze near the path.

Two hard rules: be at the gate by 6:30–7:00 am, because the view is reliably clouded in by mid-morning and you will have driven an hour for nothing; and dress for cold — this is the one place on the loop where a fleece, hat and long trousers are not optional. Ten- and twelve-year-olds who walk regularly will manage the loop fine, but treat it as the day's only event.

It gets properly cold Nuwara Eliya sits at 1,868 m and drops into single figures overnight in February. Plenty of guesthouses here don't heat well — confirm your room has a working fireplace or heater before you book, and if you can, pick a place with a heated pool, which turns a cold evening into the kids' highlight.
💡 The colonial-hotel high tea is a genuine only-here ritual: three tiers, Ceylon tea, silver, and a room that hasn't changed since the planters left. The Grand Hotel's Tea Lounge takes children happily. The Hill Club, founded by British planters in 1876, still enforces jackets and ties in its main dining room — book its more relaxed restaurant if you want the building without the dress code.

The hill country is the one part of Sri Lanka where the colonial tea-planter's bungalow is the genuine article rather than a pastiche — thick stone walls, high ceilings, fireplaces that get used, bed tea brought by a butler at dawn. The reason people build a trip around this island

The hill-country train

Doors open, feet out, a tea plantation dropping away below you at fifteen miles an hour. The line the British cut through the Central Highlands to get tea down to Colombo is now routinely called one of the great railway journeys on earth, and the stretch your children will remember is the one that climbs through Haputale and crosses the Nine Arch Bridge into Ella. It is loud, slow, chaotic, endlessly delayed and completely worth it.

What you actually get is not a scenic railway in the polished sense. It is a working train: vendors moving through the carriages selling wade and corn and sweet tea, schoolchildren getting on and off at halts with flower-lined platforms, whole families in the vestibules, and the doors simply left open so that people sit in them with their legs hanging over the drop. The scenery escalates as you climb — after Hatton the tea estates spill down both sides of the valley in combed green terraces, and between Nanu Oya and Ella the line runs a long series of ridge-edge S-curves, through short black tunnels and out over viaducts.

Second class reserved is the sweet spot. Windows that open, a guaranteed seat for four together, and access to the doorways. Third class is the classic experience and the most fun, but in February you may well be standing. First class is air-conditioned with sealed windows, which defeats the entire object.

But: the line is broken, and you need to plan around it

In November 2025 Cyclone Ditwah tore through Sri Lanka and wrecked the upcountry railway — 159 landslide sites and 95 washouts on the hill line alone, with a reported 70% of the national network affected. Repairs have been running ever since, and the position as of the most recent reporting is:

  • Nanu Oya → Haputale → Ella → Badulla: reopened 20 June 2026. The most scenic stretch of the whole route, including the Nine Arch Bridge, is running again — two departures a day in each direction.
  • Kandy → Peradeniya → Nanu Oya: still closed. And it got worse: on 3 August 2026 flash flooding on the Mahaweli swept away the machinery repairing the Black Bridge near Peradeniya, with no revised completion date issued. Engineers had been targeting end-2026 for full restoration; whether that survives is genuinely unknown.
  • And the boarding station itself is disputed. The June 2026 reopening was reported as Nanu Oya–Badulla, but other 2026 sources describe services starting further east at Ambewela and put full restoration at early 2027. The plan is the same either way — drive up and join the line wherever it currently begins — but settle which station before you reserve seats, because it moves your transfer by an hour.
What to do about it Plan on driving Kandy → Nuwara Eliya (3–4 hr up the A5, which is a lovely day in its own right) and boarding at Nanu Oya — or Ambewela, if that is where services currently begin, riding the confirmed-open leg through to Ella. You lose the Kandy-to-Hatton section; you keep the tea-terrace switchbacks, the open doors, the vendors and the Nine Arch crossing — which is to say, you keep everything the ride is famous for. Then re-check the live position 4–6 weeks out at railway.gov.lk or through a local agent, and be sceptical of any operator or blog telling you the full line is "back to normal" — several are, and the reporting says otherwise.

And the bridge

Four kilometres out of Ella, the line runs across the Nine Arch Bridge — ninety-one metres long, twenty-four high, and built in 1921 entirely out of brick, stone and cement with no steel in it at all, reputedly because the outbreak of war made steel impossible to get. The local engineer credited with building it was P.K. Appuhamy. It is free, it is a flat twenty-minute walk from the road, and watching a train come over it is the picture the whole country puts on its posters. Trains cross roughly twice a day on the current schedule, so ask at your guesthouse for the timings the night before and get there before nine, both for the light and because by ten it is shoulder to shoulder.

Leg five

Ella & the walking

Ella sits in a notch in the escarpment at about 1,000 m, and the whole town is arranged around the view out through it — the Ella Gap, a V of hills that drops away south towards the plains and, on a clear morning, the sea. This is the most straightforwardly enjoyable base on the loop for a family: everything is close, most of it is free, the food is good and cheap, and the walking is the right size for a ten-year-old. Two nights minimum, three if you want Lipton's Seat as well.

The morning that makes the trip

Do this on your first full day and do it early. Start up Little Adam's Peak — a well-graded path through tea to a 1,141 m ridge with a 360° view over the Gap and Ella Rock, twenty to fifty minutes up depending on legs. One family with a seven- and nine-year-old did the whole round trip from their guesthouse in eighty minutes and reported "enough steps to make kids complain" and genuine delight at the top, which is about the most honest review a hike can get. There is almost no shade, so this is a before-nine or after-four activity, never midday.

Then come down the marked trail past the Ravana Pool Club — a good juice-and-snack stop with the same view, and where the kids can watch people launch off the Flying Ravana zip line, half a kilometre of cable across the tea at up to 80 km/h, which is the single best-value adrenaline purchase of the trip at about US$20 — and carry on down to the Nine Arch Bridge. The two connect by a signed path, which is why they're one morning and not two.

The tougher option is Ella Rock: roughly two hours each way, partly along the railway track, and notoriously badly signed — locals have been known to obscure the real path in order to sell guiding. Take an offline map, go early, and treat it as the twelve-year-old's day rather than a whole-family stroll.

Waterfalls, tea and a kitchen

Ravana Falls is the easy one — a 25 m multi-tiered cascade directly beside the road six kilometres south of town, no walking required, langur monkeys in the trees and vendors selling king coconut and boiled corn. Diyaluma is the ambitious one: at 220 m it's the island's second-tallest waterfall, and the trick is to approach from above, via Poonagala, which is a half-hour downhill walk rather than a brutal climb from below. At the top are natural infinity pools you can swim in — with no barriers whatsoever at the cliff edge, so this is a hold-hands, eyes-on-them stop. February's dry conditions are the right window; still ask locally on the day.

For a change of pace, Lipton's Seat is the viewpoint where Sir Thomas Lipton is said to have sat and surveyed his empire — on a clear morning you can see across to Udawalawe and the Hambantota coast — best paired with the Dambethenne Tea Factory he built below it, where a LKR 250 tour walks you through the entire process and ends in a tasting. It's shut on Sundays. And an evening cooking class at a family home, clay pots over a wood fire, is the thing families consistently name as their favourite non-view activity here.

Leg six

Down to the leopard country

Three hours down off the escarpment and you are in the dry zone: thorn scrub, brick-red tracks, lagoons and a heat that sits at 33°C. February is the right end of the year to be here. The northeast rains have gone, the bush has thinned out, and the waterholes have shrunk — which means the animals stop being scattered through impenetrable scrub and start concentrating, visibly, around what water is left.

Yala or Udawalawe? Both, if you can

They are two genuinely different days and they are 65 km — about ninety minutes — apart, which is too far to base once and do both casually.

Yala is the famous one, and Block One has one of the highest recorded leopard densities anywhere on earth — roughly one leopard per square kilometre in places. It also has, by the same logic, the jeeps: when a leopard is spotted, vehicles converge, and the resulting jams are a documented problem rather than a rumour. Elephants, sloth bears, crocodiles and 200-plus bird species are near-certain; the leopard is not. And one honest caveat for February: several sources note leopards are actually more visible in the drier mid-year months, when they hunt more in the open. Build in two drives, not one — an afternoon and a dawn — and you meaningfully change your odds. If you'd rather trade a little probability for a lot of calm, ask your operator about the quieter Block Five via the Galge gate, which is what one family travelling with a five-year-old chose precisely for that reason.

Udawalawe is the one to take the children to. It's smaller, wetter and far more open — grassland and reservoir instead of dense scrub — and it holds around 600 wild elephants, which makes a sighting close to a certainty, often within the first twenty minutes and often a whole family herd with calves. There are perhaps a dozen leopards in the entire park, so nobody comes here for those. What you get instead is elephants at close range in good light, crocodiles, water buffalo, and a reservoir edge thick with birds including the lesser adjutant stork, which is the largest bird in Sri Lanka and impossible to miss.

The 4:45 am alarm is real Gates open at 6:00 and the drive that matters starts before sunrise, which means leaving your lodge in the dark. One travel writer described her family's Yala morning as being up at "sparrow's fart" — accurate. The consolation is that dawn in the bush, waiting in a line of jeeps for the gate with the sky going from grey to fire, is one of the trip's best half-hours. Afternoon drives run roughly 1:30 to 6:00 pm; every vehicle must be out by six.

Temples in the thorn scrub

The overlooked half of this leg, and the one that pays off if the family's interest in temples is real. Twenty-odd minutes from Tissamaharama, Kataragama is one of very few places on earth venerated simultaneously by four traditions — Buddhists, Hindus, Muslims and the indigenous Vedda people all hold it sacred, each with a different story about the deity who lives there. The main devale is deliberately plain and unornamented, and it runs three pujas a day (around 5 am, 11 am and 7 pm). Go to the evening one: drumming, oil lamps, pilgrims washing in the river afterwards. Beside it stands Kiri Vehera, one of the sixteen places the Buddha is said to have visited in person.

Deeper in, inside Yala's own buffer zone, Sithulpawwa Rajamaha Viharaya is a 2,200-year-old monastery built into and on top of a 400-foot rock — founded in the second century BC and once home to something like 12,000 monks, with rock caves, Brahmi inscriptions and fragments of second-century wall painting on the way up to the summit dagoba. It pairs naturally with Kataragama into one half-day loop, and it is the best possible antidote to a wildlife-only visit. Add Kirinda if you want sea air: a clifftop stupa above the Indian Ocean with a legend attached — the queen said to have washed ashore here went on to bear Dutugemunu, the island's most celebrated king. Rough water, so treat it as a walk and a view rather than a swim.

Where to stay

The genuine article here is the mobile tented safari camp. Operators — Mahoora has been doing it since 1998 — pitch a full canvas camp in the buffer zone days before you arrive and strike it completely afterwards, leaving no trace. It is not glamping-as-decor: you get an exclusive naturalist and jeep for your group, a proper multi-course dinner cooked in front of you under the trees, a night walk looking for owls and civets, and a 5:30 wake-up to peacocks. Expect rustic-but-comfortable — real bed, ensuite, hot water, mosquito netting — rather than five-star polish; canvas ages fast in the tropics.

Beyond that, the choice is Palatupana (minutes from Yala's main gate, where the design-led lodges cluster, isolated after dark and priciest), Tissamaharama (a lakeside town with ATMs, restaurants and the mid-range hotels, 20–30 minutes from the gate) or Kataragama (plain, local, temple-first, very limited lodging).

Most lodges here are full board or all-inclusive and there is very little independent dining — which is why this leg has no separate restaurant list. Tissamaharama town has the options if you want them.

Where the kids learn to stand up

Weligama, Ahangama & the whale coast

Weligama Bay is a wide crescent of sand with a gently shelving sandy bottom, no reef, no rocks, and small consistent whitewash waves — which is precisely why it turns up on every list of the best places in the world to learn to surf. Add 28–29°C water that needs no wetsuit and February's calmest conditions of the year, and you have the closest thing to a guarantee that a ten- and a twelve-year-old will stand up on a board this holiday.

The surf lesson

The bay is lined with schools and the good ones are explicitly beginner factories. Chill & Surf, founded by a Weligama local, runs ISA-certified instructors, includes all gear and transport within 2 km, offers a stand-up guarantee — don't stand, next lesson free — and charges from about €17 for a sixty-minute beginner lesson. Kuma Surf Camp is the other established option, at roughly $30 for a group hour or $50 for a ninety-minute private, with multi-day packages and board hire from about $2. Both open around six in the morning, and that is when to go: the bay is glassy, empty and cool, and by mid-morning it is a genuine traffic jam of foam boards.

Confirm the ages when you book. Neither school publishes a minimum age and neither should be assumed — a quick WhatsApp message settles it, and a private lesson for two kids is usually the better answer than dropping them into a group of adults anyway.

If the kids catch the bug and outgrow the whitewash within two days — which happens — the graduation step is a short tuk-tuk east to Kabalana in Ahangama, a wide golden beach with a reef A-frame called The Rock and several schools on the sand; one guide calls it the best option on the coast for young families. Beyond that, Midigama's named reef breaks (Lazy Left, Rams Right, Coconuts, Plantations) are for confident intermediates on sharp reef — great to watch, not to learn on.

Blue whales, and the honest version of the stilt fishermen

February is the peak of blue-whale season off Mirissa — one of the best land-accessible places on the planet to see the largest animal that has ever lived, with sperm whales, spinner dolphins and occasionally orcas in the mix. Boats leave Mirissa harbour between about six and seven in the morning and are back by late morning; February also gives you the calmest sea of the year, which matters enormously if anyone in the family is prone to seasickness (eat before, not on board; the boats carry almost no food). Prices run roughly $30–95 depending on the boat. Choose the operator on ethics, not price — look for ones that keep to the 100-yard international approach distance and limit time near the animals; some work with university marine-research programmes.

And the stilt fishermen: the single most reproduced image of Sri Lanka, men perched on a crossbar lashed to a pole in the shallows. Be told before you go that almost all of what you'll see along the coast road at Koggala is now posed for photographs rather than fishing, and a tip of LKR 1,000–2,000 is expected. That's not a reason to skip it — it's a genuinely good conversation to have with a twelve-year-old about what tourism does to a tradition — but if you want the real thing, look for solitary fishermen at quieter beaches near Ahangama around sunrise or dusk.

Where to eat

This coast has two food cultures stacked on top of each other: beach shacks laying out the day's catch on ice each evening for you to point at, and a wave of surf-town brunch cafés serving the kind of pancakes and bagels that buy you real goodwill from tired children. Leg seven

Galle Fort & the last night

The Portuguese fortified this rocky peninsula, the Dutch rebuilt it properly in the seventeenth century, and four hundred years later it is still a lived-in town rather than a monument — a grid of cobbled lanes inside coral-stone ramparts, with a working lighthouse, gem-merchants' mansions turned into hotels, and a mosque, a church and a Buddhist temple within a few streets of one another. It is also, and this matters after twelve days of tuk-tuks, the one place on the loop where you can let a ten- and twelve-year-old walk ahead of you without worrying about traffic.

Walk the walls at five o'clock

The single best thing to do in Galle costs nothing: a roughly two-kilometre loop of the ramparts, ideally started late afternoon, when the whole town comes up onto the walls — families out walking, boys playing cricket on the grass, couples on the bastions. Begin at the Clock Tower, pass the Dutch Reformed Church, round to the whitewashed lighthouse (1848, the oldest working light in the country, framed by palms and the most photographed thing in the south), and on to Flag Rock at Triton Bastion, where local men throw themselves backwards off the wall into the sea for tips as the sun goes down. Finish at Star Bastion. Kids love the whole thing, which is not always true of UNESCO sites.

Inside the walls, the Dutch Reformed Church — the Groote Kerk, finished in 1755 and the oldest Protestant church still in use in the country — is the one the children will remember: the floor and walls are paved with elaborate old grave slabs, several carved with skulls and crossbones. Ten minutes, free, and reliably described by families as spooky-fun. Just outside the walls, the fruit and spice market on Sea Street is a good cheap half-hour of mangosteen and rambutan and vendors pressing samples on you. And Galle International Stadium sits right against the ramparts — one of the prettiest Test grounds in the world, and visible free from the walls whether or not there's a match on.

Two things just outside the Fort

For the temple interest, Yatagala Raja Maha Viharaya is a fifteen-to-twenty-minute tuk-tuk away and a much older thing than anything inside the walls: a rock temple reckoned at around 2,300 years old, its main shrine carved into a hollowed boulder, with murals, a reclining Buddha and — the bit that lands with children — a small "hell cave" of vividly gruesome dioramas illustrating Buddhist moral tales. About 120 steps up.

And the Kosgoda turtle hatchery, 30–40 minutes north up the coast road, is a forty-year-old conservation project where collected eggs are protected and hatched and, timing permitting, children can help release hatchlings into the sea at dusk. Worth knowing that the ethics are debated even by sympathetic visitors — the practice of holding some females in tanks for years before release has real critics — which makes it a better stop, not a worse one, if you're willing to have that conversation on the drive home.

Getting home from here Galle to Bandaranaike airport is about two to two and a half hours on the Southern Expressway, which makes a final Galle night entirely workable even for a mid-morning flight. If your flight is very early, or you want a city afternoon, swap this last night for Colombo or Negombo instead.
💡 Galle Fort has no beach. That's the trade-off for the atmosphere — Unawatuna and Dalawella are 10–20 minutes away by tuk-tuk. And be aware the heritage mansion hotels inside the walls are stair-heavy with no lifts.

Four things this loop leaves out, in the order we'd add them back. The first one is arguably the most extraordinary experience available in Sri Lanka in February, and the only reason it isn't in the core route is that it costs two days and a night's sleep.

Worth rearranging the trip for

Adam's Peak — the night climb

Sleep in Nallathanniya · ~5,500 steps · 3–4 hr up, 1½–2 hr down · Add 2 days

February sits in the thick of the Sri Pada pilgrimage season (December full moon to May full moon), which transforms what would otherwise be a hard staircase in the dark into something closer to a festival. The entire route is lit. Tea stalls line it, selling roti and sweet tea at two in the morning. And at the top is one of the strangest sacred sites anywhere: a single footprint-shaped hollow in the rock, venerated simultaneously by Buddhists as the Buddha's, Hindus as Shiva's, and Muslims and some Christians as Adam's or St Thomas's. Pilgrims file past it, ring a bell once for each ascent they've completed, and wait in the cold while drumming and chanting come over a loudspeaker and the sun comes up. On a clear morning the peak throws a perfect triangular shadow across the plains below.

You climb overnight to be there for sunrise around 6:00–6:30. It is roughly 5,500 stone steps over about 7 km, entirely paved — not technical at all, just relentless, and hard on quads and knees on the way down. Is it doable at ten and twelve? Yes, with preparation: there are consistent first-hand accounts of seven-, ten- and eleven-year-olds completing it unaided during pilgrimage season, resting through the day beforehand and starting somewhere between 11 pm and 3 am. Some families arrange a local guide through their guesthouse as a safety net. Build in stops at the tea shops, dress in real layers — the summit is genuinely cold and windy — and know that vendors sell warm clothes on the steps if you've underpacked.

Two planning rules. Sleep in Nallathanniya (Dalhousie) itself, at the trailhead, not in Hatton — it removes the need to organise a 2:30 am tuk-tuk with children, and it is the single biggest lever you have on making this manageable. And do not climb on the Navam Poya weekend (expected around Saturday 20 February 2027): it draws the heaviest pilgrim crush of the entire season and the summit staircase becomes, in one climber's words, literally heaving. A weekday is a different mountain.

💡 Pair it with Kitulgala, two to three hours down the road towards the coast, as a decompression stop: grade II–III white-water rafting on the Kelani River (operators put the minimum age at around 7–10, so both your kids are comfortably inside it), the stretch of river where The Bridge on the River Kwai was filmed in 1957 — nothing of the set survives but the underwater footings, so treat it as a story rather than a sight — and the Belilena cave where 12,000-year-old remains of "Balangoda Man" were found. Sequence: arrive Dalhousie, sleep early, climb overnight, recover, drive to Kitulgala, raft, then on to Colombo.

The easiest upgrade

A second safari night, and Udawalawe properly

Add 1 night at Tissamaharama or Udawalawe · ~1½ hr between the parks

One night in the safari zone buys you an afternoon drive and a dawn drive in a single park. Two nights buys you Yala for the leopard and Udawalawe for the elephants, plus a mid-morning at the Elephant Transit Home feeding and an evening at Kataragama's puja — and it stops the whole leg feeling like a smash-and-grab on the way to the beach. If you're only adding one night to the entire trip, this is where the marginal night does the most work, because it converts a probability into a near-certainty.

If the surf sticks

Hiriketiya, an hour and a half east

Tuk-tuk or car · 1–1½ hr from Weligama · Add 1–2 nights, or a long day

A small horseshoe bay near Dikwella with a mellow left-hand break, water calmer than most of this coast (so genuinely good swimming with kids as well as surfing), and a café scene that has arrived in the last few years — including a vegan hopper kitchen doing beetroot-pink hoppers with dal and gotu kola sambol, and a bar that has made Asia's 50 Best list. It's too far to be a comfortable day trip from Weligama, so either give it a night or two on the way east, or skip it. On the way, Dondra Lighthouse marks the southernmost point of the island — free, five minutes, and a good "we stood at the bottom of Sri Lanka" photograph.

For the ruin-hungry

The other ancient city — and Mihintale

Add 1 night in the Cultural Triangle · Full day each

If you did Polonnaruwa, the fourth night in the Cultural Triangle buys you Anuradhapura — bigger, hotter, holier, still actively worshipped, and a completely different register (see the stop list in Leg two). Bolt on Mihintale, 16 km east of it, where a monk converted a king in 247 BC and Buddhism entered the island: a thousand-odd worn stone steps, climbed barefoot, to a gold-tipped white dagoba and a giant seated Buddha on the summit rock, with the whole dry-zone plain spread out below. Late afternoon, never midday — the stone is punishing and the steps are slippery in places.
