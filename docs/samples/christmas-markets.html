The Advent

Wien · Salzburg · München

Five days on the Christmas market circuit — where the sun sets at quarter past four and the whole trip happens by lamplight.

Advent in these three cities is not a daytime activity. The sun clears the rooftops late, hangs low and pale over the Danube and the Salzach and the Isar, and is gone by four in the afternoon — and that is precisely when the trip starts working. Stall bulbs come up against a sky still holding its last blue. Steam rolls off the Punsch cauldrons. Somewhere above you a brass quartet starts in on a chorale from a church balcony, and the smell — roasting chestnuts, cinnamon, hot wine, wet wool — arrives all at once. You will do your sightseeing in the grey hours and your living in the dark ones.

All three cities run their markets from mid-November to somewhere around Christmas, so almost any December week lands you in the thick of it. What differs is character. Wien runs more than a dozen markets at once, each with its own personality, wrapped around imperial architecture and 300 years of coffeehouse ritual. Salzburg compresses everything into one floodlit Baroque bowl beneath a fortress — smaller, older, more intense. München layers Advent directly onto its own beer-hall culture, and has the widest spread of oddball markets: a medieval one with flaming clay goblets, a pink one, one aboard a ship stranded on a railway bridge.

The four o'clock rule

This is the single fact that should shape every day of the trip. Mid-December gives you roughly eight and a half hours of light, and the markets are at their photographic and atmospheric best in the half-hour on either side of sunset — the blue hour, when the stall lights are already burning but the sky hasn't gone black. Every photographer in the research says the same thing independently: Munich's market-runners "treat [4:20 pm] as the golden hour for photos, when the stalls' lights start to dominate the fading sky"; in Salzburg it's "dusk through blue hour (roughly 4–6 pm)"; in Vienna the big squares only really work "after dark."

Which means: put the palaces, museums, tower climbs and coffeehouses in the flat light before three, be standing in a market square by half past three, and expect to be out in the cold and dark until the stalls close between eight and ten.

The shape of five days

Four nights, three cities, two train rides, moving west. Going Wien → Salzburg → München rather than the reverse means you start in the biggest, most spread-out city while you're freshest, and finish in Munich, which has the best onward flight connections. It also means each city gets smaller and more concentrated as you go, which is a pleasant way to end.

Getting the trains right

Both legs are simple, frequent and scenic enough to be worth a window seat. Wien Hauptbahnhof → Salzburg Hauptbahnhof runs roughly hourly and direct, about two and a half hours; the first stretch is flat Lower Austrian farmland, and somewhere around the Attnang-Puchheim junction the Salzkammergut foothills come up and stay. Salzburg → München Hauptbahnhof is about an hour and a half across the border, every couple of hours.

Book ahead, but only the trains

Austrian and German advance fares (ÖBB's Sparschiene, DB's Sparpreis) are dramatically cheaper than walk-up prices and December trains genuinely fill — reserve a seat on both legs. Nothing else on this trip needs booking except hotels (Advent weekends sell out early, especially Salzburg's small Altstadt inventory) and concerts. Markets are all free to walk into.

Six things that will catch you out

1 · Carry cash. Plenty of market food and drink stalls don't take cards, in all three cities.

2 · Your Glühwein comes with a deposit. Roughly €3–5 (Pfand) on top of the €5–6 drink, refunded when you return the mug — or keep it, that's the souvenir.

3 · Austria closes for Christmas, properly. Shops and most restaurants shut from 24–26 December, and eating out on the 24th is simply not a tradition; even restaurant staff go home. Market stalls are the most reliable option. Vienna's shops also close every Sunday by law.

4 · Markets don't all end on the same day. They finish anywhere between 20 December and 6 January — see the chart below before you fix dates.

5 · Lunch is the big meal. In both countries the midday meal is traditionally the main, warm one, often with a cheap set Mittagsmenü. Eat only at dinner and you miss the best value of the day.

6 · The 8th of December is a holiday in Austria. Expect the Salzburg and Vienna markets at their most crowded around 7–8 December.

The one thing all three cities share

The Advent

Punsch · Maroni · Christkind · Krampus

Before the cities divide into their own personalities, there is a common grammar. Every market in this circuit is a grazing operation, not a dining one: you walk, you hold a hot mug in both hands, you eat something out of paper, you move on. Viennese locals treat this as its own social occasion — the Punschstand-Runde, distinct from dinner — and Münchners as an after-work stop, the Weihnachtsmarkt-Bummel, on the way home rather than a weekend tourist outing. Copy them. Do not save the markets for one big evening; drop into three of them across a day.

The lineage is genuinely old and it is the same lineage in all three places. Vienna's tradition traces to a 13th-century "December Market" for winter provisions granted by Duke Albrecht I of Habsburg, which by the 14th century was a Nikolausmarkt selling gingerbread and toys. Munich's Marienplatz market was first recorded as a Nikolaimarkt in 1310 and renamed the Christmarkt in 1806. Salzburg's began as a 15th-century Tandlmarkt, later the Nikolaimarkt. What you are walking through is a provisioning fair that never stopped.

The mug is the whole ritual

You order a Glühwein and pay two prices: the drink, about €5–6, and a Pfand of €3–5 on the heavy ceramic mug. Hand the mug back at any stall of the same market and you get the deposit returned — or keep it, which is what the deposit is really for. Most markets print the year and the square on the glaze, so the mug is the souvenir locals actually collect.

The Feuerzangenbowle is the escalation: a rum-soaked sugar loaf set alight on iron tongs above the punch bowl, caramelising and dripping into the wine below. Munich has a whole tent built around one, behind the Schrannenhalle.

Two habits worth adopting. Come twice — browse the crafts in the morning or early afternoon when you can actually see them and nobody is pressed against you, then come back after dark for the lights and the drinking, which is a different event in the same square. And carry cash: card acceptance has improved on the big squares, but the small stalls and the smaller markets are still coins-and-notes, and the Pfand refund always is.

The one thing that ruins a tripMost markets close on or just before 24 December, and several shut days earlier — see the opening windows below before you book flights.

What's actually in the mug

  • Glühwein Mulled red — cinnamon, clove, citrus. The baseline; white (weißer) versions are increasingly common and lighter
  • Punsch Stronger and stranger: spirit, sugar, citrus, spice. The Austrian default — Vienna has gone modern with mulled Lillet and hot Aperol
  • Feuerzangenbowle A rum-soaked sugar cone set alight over the bowl, dripping flaming caramel into the wine. Munich's party trick
  • Jagatee "Hunters' tea" — black tea with rum. The move when you can't face more wine. Try it at Speck Willi in Salzburg
  • Eierlikör German egg liqueur, close to eggnog. The Sendlinger Tor speciality in Munich
  • Kinderpunsch Alcohol-free spiced punch. Order it unashamedly on mug number four

The dates trap

The markets do not all run to Christmas. Salzburg's fortress market shuts on 20 December, Munich's medieval market on the 23rd, and the main Marienplatz Christkindlmarkt at 2 pm on Christmas Eve — while Salzburg's flagship runs on to 1 January and Munich's Feuerzangenbowle tent to 6 January. If your dates are flexible, the first three weeks of December catch everything open at once. If they're fixed late, read down this chart.

Days worth building the trip around

If your dates aren't locked yet, a handful of specific afternoons are worth arranging the whole circuit around. One of them will genuinely startle you.

Sunday 13 December · 3–5 pm · München

Krampuslauf

Around 25 groups and more than 300 costumed figures — Krampus, Perchten and Klause — come rattling and stamping through the Munich Altstadt, chains dragging, carved wooden masks weighing up to 10kg, individual costumes running €1,800–2,500. It draws up to 50,000 spectators. It is a living Alpine tradition roughly 500 years old, revived at the Munich market in 2004, and the etiquette is worth knowing: they are play-acting menace, and they will pose for selfies rather than actually frighten anyone.

The practical cost is that streets around Viktualienmarkt close between roughly 3 and 5 pm and several bus lines divert. Get to a spot along the route early. If you're in Salzburg instead, the same Krampus and Perchten tradition parades through that city too from late November onward.

Leg one · 2 nights · Austria

Wien

Wiener Christkindlmarkt

Vienna does not do one central market. It runs more than a dozen simultaneously, which means the skill here is not seeing them all — it's picking the three that suit you. The flagship on Rathausplatz alone draws an estimated 2.8 million visitors across the season; the artisan lanes of Spittelberg, ten minutes away, feel like a village. In between: coffeehouses that have been engineered for lingering since 1683, three palaces that each grow their own market in the forecourt, and a December concert programme in the churches that doesn't exist in any other month.

The crowd-avoidance rule

Crowds build steadily through December and peak hard on weekends and especially 20–24 December. Go to the big markets right at opening (10 am) or well after dark on a weeknight. The tram stop at Rathausplatz/Burgtheater actually closes for safety at peak times — Friday to Sunday afternoons and evenings in the run-up to the 24th.

The big squares

Mark Brownlow, who has covered the Rathausplatz market for years, is blunt about the trade-off: go to Rathausplatz for the sheer scale and spectacle, but "look to the Spittelberg and Karlsplatz markets for locations with a stronger focus on artisan and unusual offerings." Both are true. Do the big ones once, at night, for the spectacle — and then spend your real time in the four small ones below.

The artisan four

These are the ones to linger in. All four sit within the 1st and 7th districts, three of them within ten minutes of each other on foot, and between them they cover glass ornaments, ceramics, felted crafts and Fairtrade organic food rather than another rack of identical baubles.

Three palaces, three markets

Vienna's particular trick is that its Habsburg palaces grow markets in their forecourts. This is where an afternoon of proper sightseeing and an evening of Glühwein collapse into the same address — go for the interiors in the flat early light, come back out into the courtyard as the lights come on.

Where to point the camera

The market squares themselves are covered above; these are the vantage points — the places to stand rather than the places to go. Note that most stalls are cash-only and the squares are far too dense for a tripod; save that for the quiet spots.

Also, without a detour

The Graben, the pedestrian street running off Stephansplatz, is hung with Vienna's signature illuminated "chandelier" light installations — the most photographed festive lighting in the city, and free, and on your way between two markets. Around the corner, the domed Michaelerkuppel entrance to the Hofburg on Michaelerplatz is the classic straight-through-the-archway shot; kissing underneath it is said to bring good luck. And the steps of Maria am Gestade, a narrow Gothic church wedged between old-town houses near Salvatorgasse, became a cinematic photo spot after Before Sunrise.

Wien · the indoor half of the day

The Kaffeehaus contract

Melange & Marmortisch

In a city with eight hours of daylight and a wind off the Danube, the coffeehouse is not a charming aside — it is infrastructure. And the thing to understand is that it works on a contract: for the price of one Melange you may occupy a marble table for hours, read the newspapers the house provides for free, write, argue, or simply be alone in company. The writer Alfred Polgar's line was that the Viennese need company in order to be alone.

The origin story credits beans abandoned by the retreating Ottoman army after the failed siege of 1683 — a better story than it is a settled fact — but a real coffeehouse existed within a few years of the siege, and by 1790 there were seventy. They became the era's public living rooms, hosting Freud, Trotsky and generations of writers who had nowhere else warm to think. UNESCO recognises the whole culture as intangible heritage.

Two practical notes. A glass of water always arrives unasked alongside the coffee — that's the house custom, not a special order. And do not order "a coffee": order by name.

How to order

  • MelangeEspresso, steamed milk, milk foam — Vienna's cappuccino. The default.
  • EinspännerDouble espresso in a glass under whipped cream. Named for the one-horse carriage drivers who needed a coffee that stayed hot in one free hand.
  • Kleiner / Großer BraunerSmall or large espresso "browned" with a splash of milk.
  • VerlängerterEspresso lengthened with hot water — the closest thing to an Americano.
  • FiakerBlack coffee laced with rum. Also named for the carriage drivers, who clearly had a system.
The cake war, settled out of court

The Sachertorte — chocolate sponge split with a single layer of apricot jam under a dark chocolate glaze — was created in 1832 by a 16-year-old apprentice, Franz Sacher, for Prince Metternich's court. His son Eduard refined it at the family hotel, which touched off a 20th-century legal battle with Demel over which shop could call its cake the "Original". Hotel Sacher won the name; Demel kept its own version, with one sponge layer instead of two. Order both, a day apart, and pick a side.

The Beisl, and the rest of the table

Beyond the coffeehouses, Vienna's real eating happens in the Beisl — wood-panelled, unhurried, and unapologetic about serving lung. The city's genius was never invention so much as absorption: schnitzel came by way of Milan, goulash and dumplings by way of Hungary and Bohemia, and Vienna's contribution was insisting on doing all of it properly, in its own rooms, at its own pace.

The classics to look for on a Beisl menu

  • Wiener SchnitzelVeal cutlet

    Pounded thin, breaded, fried in butter or lard until it billows away from the meat. Protected by name — a pork version must be labelled Schnitzel vom Schwein.

  • TafelspitzBoiled beef

    Slow-boiled in broth with root vegetables; the broth arrives first as soup, then the meat with apple-horseradish (Apfelkren), chive sauce and roast potatoes. Franz Joseph I's reputed favourite.

  • SalonbeuschelVeal lung & heart ragout

    Finely diced in a sour-cream sauce with a bread dumpling. A Jewish-influenced dish that entered mainstream Viennese cooking, declined for decades, and is now back on Beisl menus. Usually a daily special — ask.

  • BackhendlFried breaded chicken

    A whole deboned chicken, breaded and fried; in the Biedermeier era a symbol of bourgeois prosperity. The lighter counterpart to schnitzel.

  • ApfelstrudelApple strudel

    Hand-stretched pastry rolled around spiced apple, raisins and breadcrumbs, with vanilla sauce. The warm indoor answer to the market's Bratapfel.

  • PunschkrapfenPink rum-punch cake

    A lurid pink, rum-soaked petit four. Sluka's are the local benchmark, alongside their sugar model of the Stephansdom.

If you have a spare evening

On the vineyard hills at the city's edge — Grinzing, Nussdorf, Sievering, Stammersdorf — the Heuriger tradition dates to 1784, when Emperor Joseph II decreed that winemakers could sell their own wine and simple food without a licence. The format is deliberately informal: a cold buffet, rustic tables, and — Vienna's own rule — no recorded music, only live Schrammelmusik. It's a genuine cultural experience but a real trip out of the centre: over an hour by public transport, so plan a taxi. Order a Spritzer if a full glass feels heavy after a day of Glühwein.

Leg two · 1–2 nights · Austria

Salzburg

Salzburger Christkindlmarkt

This is the most concentrated stop of the three, and the most cinematic. The whole thing happens in one Baroque bowl: a hundred wooden stalls spread across Domplatz and Residenzplatz, joined by a lit passage under the cathedral arch, with the floodlit silhouette of Fortress Hohensalzburg hanging over the top of it. The modern market dates to 1974 and marked its 50th anniversary in 2024, but its roots run back to a 15th-century Tandlmarkt. It draws over 900,000 visitors a season into a space you can cross in four minutes.

The compression is the point. The Christkindlmarkt, the Mozartplatz ice rink, the Sternadvent courtyard, Getreidegasse and the funicular up to the fortress market are all within a 15-minute walk of one another — you can genuinely do all of them in one full day. Only Mirabellplatz (across the river) and Hellbrunn (a bus ride south) sit outside that circle.

Two Salzburg-specific catches

First: several of the smaller markets — the fortress and St. Leonhard — run Friday to Sunday only. Check your dates against that before you plan an evening around the fortress. Second: expect real snow and real cold. Daytime highs hover just above freezing and nights go well below, and the Altstadt is cobbled and pedestrianised, which also means no car can get near most hotel doors — arriving means a short wheeled-suitcase haul over stone.

The market in the bowl

Of roughly 100 stalls, only about ten sell food — which means each of those ten has carved out its own speciality instead of duplicating the neighbour's Glühwein. Locals use it as a genuine daily-life stop: office workers grabbing a Bosna on the walk home, couples queuing for oysters and prosecco as a first-date splurge.

From above, and from the bridges

Salzburg is a city built to be looked down on, and December is the best month to do it: the Old Town's slate roofs under snow, the fortress lit against a dark sky, the market squares glowing in two neat rectangles. Every viewpoint below is a short walk or one lift ride from the Altstadt, and all but one are free.

At street level

And three at ground level worth building the daylight hours around — a lane, a garden and a street on the far bank, each of which the local photographers name independently.

Two more, hidden along the way

Continuing along the Mönchsberg past the Bürgerwehr wall, Richterhöhe is the highest point on the hill, with a spectacular view across to the Festungsberg and Gaisberg behind — a local photographer's pick, described as "hidden along the way," best in morning light or with the sun behind you in the evening. And down at street level on Anton-Neumayr-Platz, just below the Museum der Moderne, a whole row of houses is built directly into the Mönchsberg rock face.

Salzburg · a Bulgarian sausage and a 1,200-year-old kitchen

Eating in the Durchhaus

Bosna · Nockerl · Mozartkugel

Salzburg's own street food was invented in 1949 by a Bulgarian immigrant, and it lives in a hole in the wall. The Bosna is two thin grilled pork bratwursts in a toasted white roll with onions, parsley and a secret curry-based spice blend — and it belongs to the city's distinctive Durchhäuser, the narrow covered passageways that cut through the buildings between Getreidegasse and the streets behind.

Zanko Todoroff started grilling a spiced sausage at the Müllner Bräustübl brewery, first calling it the "Nadanizer", then opened his own stall — the Balkan Grill — inside the Stockhamer Durchhaus. A local signwriter coined the new name by blending the Bulgarian word for snack, bosa, with "Bosnia". A Salzburg butcher known simply as Walter has run it since the 1980s using Todoroff's original 1950 recipe.

Ordering is its own small ritual. The menu is a numbered list, No. 1 through No. 5, toggling onions, mustard, ketchup and the spice blend, and regulars have strong opinions about which number is correct. No. 1 is the Original. Whether mustard (No. 2) or ketchup (No. 4) constitutes heresy depends entirely on who you ask.

Above the street food sits a much older formal culture: a restaurant documented since 803 AD, a coffeehouse tradition going back to the start of the 18th century — for nearly two centuries a male-only pleasure, with women barred from Café Tomaselli at its 1852 founding and only granted the right to enter Austrian coffeehouses in 1891 — and a princely 17th-century dessert invented, legend says, to please an archbishop.

The sought-after historic rooms — St. Peter Stiftskulinarium and Gasthof Goldgasse especially — book out around Christmas and New Year. Reserve prime evening slots at least a few days ahead, and check specifically for 24–26 December closures or fixed festive menus. St. Peter runs festive menus on the 25th and 26th and a New Year's Eve dinner.

Leg three · 2 nights · Germany

München

Münchner Christkindlmarkt

Munich has the widest spread of market types of the three cities — not just the 130-stall flagship on Marienplatz, but a whole typology: a medieval market where the mulled wine arrives in a flaming clay goblet, a queer market glowing pink in the Glockenbachviertel, a tiny one against a 14th-century city gate, another aboard a stranded passenger ship on a railway bridge. And it does all of this without abandoning its own Wirtshaus culture for a moment: a Münchner grazing a Christkindlmarkt at six may be at a regulars' table in a 400-year-old brewery tavern by eight, and the food barely changes character between the two.

With a day and a half, concentrate on the Old Town core — Marienplatz, the Residenz courtyard and the medieval market at Wittelsbacherplatz are all within a 10–15 minute walk — rather than chasing every outlying neighbourhood. Add one or two of the characterful smaller markets and a single indoor sight and the time fills comfortably. Munich's sunset falls around 4:20 pm; locals and market-runners alike treat it as the golden hour.

The Old Town cluster

Everything here is walkable from Marienplatz in under fifteen minutes, and the markets are quietest at opening and most crowded from 3 pm to close. The Marienplatz market has roots to the 14th century and has held an independent Nativity market on this spot since 1757 — it is still Germany's largest, everything from stable lanterns to hand-carved figures.

The characterful outliers

These are the reason to give Munich two nights instead of one. Each is a deliberate trip rather than a stumble, and each is a completely different evening.

Above the market

Munich gives you three tower climbs onto the same square and they are not equal. Alter Peter (St. Peter's) is the one photographers name: 306 steps, no elevator, last entry 5:30 pm — arrive before five and you catch the market lighting up from directly overhead, the Neues Rathaus roofline in the middle distance and the Frauenkirche's twin onion domes beyond. The Neues Rathaus tower has an elevator if the stairs are out. The Frauenkirche's south tower is 89 steps plus a lift, and on a clear winter day the Alps appear on the horizon.

München · the tavern under the market

The Wirtshaus order of things

Ist hier frei?

A Munich Wirtshaus isn't quite a restaurant. It's a tavern where you can as easily come for a single Maß of beer as for a meal, seated at long communal tables shared with strangers as a matter of course. The phrase to know is "Ist hier frei?" — "is this seat free?" — asked before you sit. The one thing not to do is take a Stammtisch: those small plaques mean the table is informally reserved for regulars.

The largest halls — Hofbräuhaus, Augustiner, Paulaner, Löwenbräukeller, Schneider Bräuhaus — are each tied to one of Munich's historic breweries and keep their own brass-band schedules. Note that a pretzel placed on your table unasked is not complimentary and will appear on the bill. And many places keep a weekly closing day, a Ruhetag, so check hours before setting out.

The most strictly policed food rule in Europe

"No Weißwurst should hear the noon church bells." The delicate veal-and-pork sausage — parsley, lemon, mace, gently poached, never smoked — was traditionally made fresh each morning because it spoiled quickly without refrigeration. Refrigeration arrived; the rule stayed. Most Munich restaurants keep a hard midday cutoff and locals genuinely observe it, so a visitor turning up at 1 pm expecting the classic Munich breakfast will simply be told it's over. It comes in its own poaching water in a covered bowl, with sweet mustard (Süßer Senf), a pretzel and a wheat beer — at breakfast. Bavaria has never treated beer with breakfast as unusual. Eating it properly is called zuzeln: bite or cut one end and suck the meat from the casing. Slitting it lengthwise and rolling the meat out with a fork is the discreet modern alternative.

A Sunday habit worth copying

Frühschoppen: meeting at the Stammtisch for a late-morning beer and an argument about politics, roughly 10 am to 1 pm, usually with a Volksmusik band in the corner. In Munich it merges with the Weißwurst breakfast — sausages and a brass band under the English Garden's chestnut trees at the Chinesischer Turm beer garden. A visitor who assumes Sunday late morning is "too early" for a beer is out of step with an entire institution.

If you can add a day — or swap one

Four detours

Nürnberg · Berchtesgaden · Oberndorf · Bratislava

None of these are in the five-day itinerary above, and the honest advice for a tight circuit is that every hour spent leaving a city is an hour not spent in it. But each of the four is a genuinely different experience from the three market cities — one is the most famous Christmas market in Germany, one is an alpine lake, one is where "Silent Night" was written, and one is a second European capital an hour from Vienna. If you add a sixth day, this is where to put it.

The big one · from München

Nürnberg's Christkindlesmarkt

ICE from München Hbf · 1h 10m–1h 30m · trains every ~15 min · allow a full day

This is the market everyone else is measured against, and the case for going is simple: around 180 wooden stalls with red-and-white striped canvas roofs fill the Hauptmarkt beneath the Gothic Frauenkirche — "the little city of wood and cloth" — and the whole medieval Altstadt around it sits inside a five-kilometre wall that still stands. You are never more than twenty-five minutes' walk from anything worth seeing. It is also the one detour that competes directly with your Munich time rather than complementing it, so weigh it honestly: this is a swap, not an addition, unless you have the extra day.

What Nuremberg has that Munich doesn't is a specific and slightly strange local repertoire. The stalls sell Rauschgold angels, straw stars, feather angels, and the city's own Zwetschgenmännle — small figures made of dried prunes. The food is protected by geography: Nürnberger Rostbratwurst, finger-length sausages grilled over a beechwood fire, a tradition documented since 1313 and eaten "drei im Weggla" (three in a bun); and Elisenlebkuchen, a protected-recipe gingerbread that is chewy and heavy with nuts rather than dry and biscuity. Nuremberg also claims the world's largest cauldron of Feuerzangenbowle — a separate, easy-to-spot installation, not one of the Hauptmarkt stalls.

The alpine one · from Salzburg

Berchtesgaden & the Königssee

Bus 840 from Salzburg Hbf · ~50 min · ~€11 day ticket · half to full day

This is the easiest big detour on the whole circuit — a public bus from outside Salzburg's station, no car, no booking, and you cross into Bavaria and into the Alps proper. Berchtesgaden itself is the alpine Christmas postcard made real: about fifty wooden huts spread over Schlossplatz and Weihnachtsschützenplatz beneath a walled royal castle and the onion-domed Stiftskirche, with the white bulk of the Watzmann massif behind. Woodcarvers, turners and crib-makers demonstrate regional craft between the stalls, and there's a "labyrinth" of 300-odd Christmas trees. Its own Laternenweg — a lantern trail of 70 lanterns over a 2.1 km circular walk from Weihnachtsschützenplatz up the Kalvarienberg — is the quieter, more romantic alternative to the market crowds, and takes 60–90 minutes.

Twenty minutes further on is the Königssee, Germany's cleanest and deepest lake, a fjord-like sheet of emerald water hemmed in by the sheer east face of the Watzmann. Because motorboats are banned to protect the water, the only way to see it is the electric passenger boat to St. Bartholomä — an 18th-century pilgrimage church with twin wine-red onion domes on the far shore, reachable no other way. A crew member traditionally plays a trumpet mid-lake to demonstrate the echo off the rock walls. In winter the crowds thin to almost nothing and red deer sometimes come down to the shore to feed; the operator's own description is "an almost fairy-tale-like tranquility."

Three winter closures to plan around

The Eagle's Nest (Kehlsteinhaus) is completely closed in winter — May to October only. The Königssee boats run only as far as St. Bartholomä; the Salet/Obersee route is late April to mid-October, so the far end of the lake and its waterfall are off-limits. And the Jennerbahn cable car shuts for maintenance from 9 November, reopening only 5–6 and 12–13 December and then daily from the 18th. Boats run year-round except 24 December.

Getting there: Bus 840 from Salzburg Hauptbahnhof (or Mirabellplatz, four minutes later) to Berchtesgaden, ~50 min; then bus 841 on to Schönau am Königssee, 10–15 min. Buy a ~€11 day ticket from the driver covering both legs both ways. Round-trip boat to St. Bartholomä €23.40. If you'd rather skip the boat, the Malerwinkel ("painter's corner") loop from the car park is a free, easy 45–75 minute circular walk to the most photographed vantage point on the lake.

If fog settles in the valley — which happens, and can linger for days — the salt mine is the reliable fallback: Germany's oldest working mine, cutting salt since 1517, with a mine train 650m into the mountain, two slides down into the workings and a raft crossing of an underground salt lake. A constant 12°C. €26.50; winter tours 11:00–15:00.

💡 The single most romantic bookable thing in the region: the royal castle's candlelit evening tours, run only a few dates each December. "Advent im Schloss" (€20) fills the state rooms with candlelight, Advent poems and a soprano; "Abend im Kerzenlicht" (€30) is a full tour by the light of 800 candles under dimmed crystal chandeliers, with Gregorian chant, a harpist and a glass of Sekt in the medieval hall to close. 2026 dates: 29 Nov and 6 Dec at 15:00 and 17:00; 11, 28 and 30 Dec at 17:00. No reservation needed — tickets go on sale at the museum counter 15 minutes before.

The half-day one · from Salzburg

Where "Silent Night" was written

S-Bahn S1/S11 from Salzburg Hbf · ~25 min · every 15–30 min · 2–3 hrs

Oberndorf bei Salzburg is a town of under 6,000 people on the Salzach that exists as a destination almost entirely because of one song. On Christmas Eve 1818, the assistant priest Joseph Mohr and the schoolteacher-organist Franz Xaver Gruber first performed "Stille Nacht! Heilige Nacht!" here, in the church of St. Nikola. That church was demolished after repeated flooding; a small octagonal memorial chapel, built on the exact spot in 1937 with stained-glass windows of the two men, now marks it.

It is genuinely tiny — one room, reached by a short flight of decorated steps, tucked behind a building off the main road, and one first-hand December visitor describes it as looking "a bit like an observatory." That smallness is the point. Standing in front of it in the cold and the dark, with a large Advent wreath on the door and a modest market selling mulled wine and wood carvings a few steps away, the story of the flood-damaged organ and the substitute carol two men wrote for Christmas Mass stops being a factoid. Visitors who find themselves inside together in December quite often start singing.

Next door, in the former parsonage where Mohr actually lived from 1817 to 1819, the Silent Night Museum covers the whole story across two floors — including a karaoke station where you can sing the carol in any of the 140-plus languages it has been translated into, and an exhibit debunking the popular legend that a mouse chewed through the organ bellows. The chapel is free and open daily 8 am–8 pm; the museum is €5, Tuesday–Sunday 10 am–6 pm in November and December, closed Mondays.

Two things pair naturally with it. The Salzachdamm embankment walk (20–30 min) looks across the river to the Alpine foothills — the same river whose 1897 and 1899 floods wrecked the original church. And because Oberndorf sits directly on the border, a five-minute walk over the Länderbrücke or the pedestrian Europasteg drops you into Laufen, Bavaria, which kept more of its pastel-facaded old town than Oberndorf did.

💡 Sunset here is around 4:15–4:30 pm and the chapel is at its best floodlit — so do the riverside walk at midday and save the chapel and market for the last of the light. If you happen to be here on 24 December, the 5 pm memorial service in front of the Gruber-Mohr monument is the reason people fly in.

A second capital · from Wien

Bratislava for the day

Train from Wien · ~1 hr each way · frequent · full day

An hour from Vienna, in a different country, with its own Advent season — and in December it's the more realistic escape than the obvious one. Vienna's best-known excursion, the Wachau Valley, largely shuts for winter: the Danube cruises don't run and many of the Heurige wine taverns close for the season. Bratislava doesn't. Note that tickets are cheaper bought at the station than online, and the high-speed Danube catamaran is a warm-weather proposition — take the train.

The city's Advent fills its two Old Town squares. Hlavné námestie (Main Square) takes the big lit tree, around 45 food stalls and 25 craft stalls, and a children's "Christmas Forest" of 40 decorated trees; the tree-lined promenade of Hviezdoslavovo námestie, running from the National Theatre past fountains and the historic Hotel Carlton (which once hosted Jules Verne and Einstein), gets its own tree, a wooden Nativity scene, a straw slide and a carousel. There are smaller markets at the castle and in the Old Town Hall courtyard. The eating here is different enough from Austria to be the point: look for lángoše (fried dough) and cheesy podpecník flatbread among the stalls, then halušky potato dumplings and garlic soup with a Zlatý Bažant at a proper Slovak pub rather than a tourist menu on the square.

Between the two squares, the compact Old Town takes about an hour: St. Michael's Gate, the last of the four medieval gateways (c. 1300), with a "Kilometer Zero" plate underneath showing distances to cities worldwide and reputedly Europe's narrowest house nearby at 160cm wide — though the interior was closed for renovation at research time, so don't plan a tower climb. Bratislava Castle rises 80m above the Danube; the courtyard and Baroque gardens are free and the view is the best in the city, with only the museum charging (€14). A few minutes off the loop, the Blue Church (Kostol sv. Alžbety) is an Art Nouveau confection painted pastel blue from roof to pews, built in 1913 as a tribute to Empress Elisabeth — the most photographed building in Slovakia, though the interior only opens for Mass.

Then finish high. The UFO observation deck, a saucer 95m up on the pylon of the SNP bridge, gives a 360° panorama over the Old Town, the castle and the Petržalka high-rises — worth timing for late afternoon, before the train back. Open daily 10:00–23:00, around €12.

💡 Bratislava runs a decorated Christmas tram — red-and-green interior, a painted fireplace, carols — on the regular city lines from around St. Nicholas Day (6 December) to near year's end, free with any transit ticket. The 2026/27 market dates weren't published at research time; the 2025/26 season ran 27 Nov–6 Jan, daily 10:00–22:00, closed 24–25 December. Confirm before you go.

Also possible, with reservations

Hallstatt (~1.5–2 hrs from Salzburg by train, changing at Attnang-Puchheim, then a short ferry across the lake) is spectacular but genuinely off-season in winter — its Christmas market is a single day, 8 December, not a multi-week affair. Come for the near-empty lakeside viewpoints and snow-dusted rooftops against the Dachstein, not for stalls, and check the Salzbergbahn funicular's winter hours before planning around the Skywalk. Rothenburg ob der Tauber's Reiterlesmarkt (20 Nov–23 Dec) is the most magical walled-town setting in Bavaria, but it's a transfer-heavy 1h10–1h45 from Nuremberg, so realistically it's a detour off a detour. Bamberg, a UNESCO old town 35–45 minutes from Nuremberg with a nativity-scene trail of some 350 cribs at 35 stations, is an easier hop but was the least-verified item in the research. And Neuschwanstein, however tempting: from Munich it's about 2.5–3 hours of transit each way in three legs, which does not combine with a serious market day. Save it.
