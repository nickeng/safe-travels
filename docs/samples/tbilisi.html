Four days · solo · the fifth season

Tbilisi in the
Amber Season ღვინო · ქვევრი · აივანი

Where the vine is carved into the church wall and buried under the cellar floor — a city of wooden balconies and steaming brick domes, at the one month of the year it turns the colour of its own wine.

Georgians will tell you their country did not discover wine so much as grow up inside it. The proof is not in a museum: it is carved into the walls. On the facade of the church at Ananuri, stonemasons in 1689 cut grapevines, tendrils and heavy bunches of stone grapes into the masonry alongside the angels. Saint Nino, who converted the country in the 4th century, is said to have bound her cross from grapevines and her own hair. And on the ridge above Tbilisi, the aluminium figure of კართლის დედა Kartlis Deda — Mother of Georgia — holds a sword in one hand for enemies and a bowl of wine in the other for guests. Here, the vine is the architecture.

Which makes October the month. Days settle at a shirtsleeves 15–20 °C and nights drop below ten; the light, as Emily Lush — an Australian writer who lives here — puts it, has "mellowed out into beautiful golden tones." The plane trees along Rustaveli start letting go. And an hour and a half east, in Kakheti, the rtveli is finishing: qvevri being filled and sealed, trucks sagging under Saperavi, the whole valley smelling of fermenting must. New-vintage wine starts reaching Tbilisi's bars within weeks. You could not time a wine-and-architecture trip better if you tried.

The city itself is small, vertical and walkable. Old Tbilisi (Kala) piles carved wooden balconies over brick bath-domes at the foot of a fortress hill; Sololaki next door is a 19th-century grid of Art Nouveau doorways and stained-glass stairwells; across the river, Avlabari holds the best sunset in the country. Everything below is within those few square kilometres, plus two day trips that are the actual reason to come in October.

Where in October to land

Three things move across the month and they don't peak together: the harvest is finishing, the city's leaves are starting, and two of the year's best days are fixed. Early October gets you cellars still working; the last ten days get you the gold.

The honest version: most Kakheti estates finish their main picking by late September, and each vineyard's harvest lasts only two or three days — the dates are set a week or two ahead, on sugar levels. Arrive in the first half of October and you'll likely catch cellars mid-work rather than pickers mid-row; if actually cutting grapes yourself is the point, book a harvest day (see Kakheti) or shift to late September. Arrive after the 20th and you trade the harvest for a city under full gold.

Four days, shaped around the wine

Day 1 — Old Tbilisi, ending underground. Be at Abanotubani before 8am, when the light is still low on the Persian tiles of Chreli-Abano and the streets are empty; the afternoon glare kills this shot. Drop into the Leghvtakhevi gorge, then cable car up from Rike Park to the Narikala ridge and Mother of Georgia, and walk down through the Sololaki balcony streets and the Betlemi stairs. Book a private room at a bathhouse for late afternoon — locals insist the sulphur baths are best when the air outside is cold — then eat khinkali at Racha and finish at Vino Underground, four minutes' walk away.

Day 2 — Kakheti. Leave by 8am with a driver. Sighnaghi's walls, Bodbe's gardens, and a family marani where the winemaker pours. Come back late, or better — stay the night in Telavi and give the Alaverdi–Ikalto–Tsinandali triangle a morning. It is the single best decision available in this itinerary.

Day 3 — the boulevards and the concrete. Walk Aghmashenebeli Avenue before the sweepers clear the leaves, then Rustaveli and the Moorish-Revival Opera House. Midday at Dezerter Bazaar for persimmon strings and churchkhela. Afternoon in Chugureti for Fabrika and the murals, or out to the Chronicle of Georgia. Sunset at King Parnavaz Garden. Dinner at Cafe Littera or Shavi Lomi.

Day 4 — Mtskheta, with a château on the way. Wine and 4th-century stone in one run: Château Mukhrani for a Kartli qvevri tasting, then Svetitskhoveli, Samtavro, and up to Jvari for the confluence at golden hour. Khinkali in a Mtskheta garage on the way home.

If you only do three things

  1. Taste amber wine poured from the qvevri it was made in, in Kakheti, by the person who made it.
  2. Soak in a private sulphur room in Abanotubani on a cold evening, and pay the extra 15–20 GEL for the კისი kisi scrub.
  3. Stand in Svetitskhoveli at 8am, before anyone else, and look up.

Booking windows. Family cellars: 1–2 days. Harvest experiences and Kakheti beds in early October: 3–4 weeks. Cafe Littera, Poliphonia, Shavi Lomi and Odlisi: a few days. Tbilisoba weekend accommodation: a month.

Two days worth planning around

The city tips its table out into the streetთბილისობა · სვეტიცხოვლობა

Tbilisoba თბილისობა

Held every year since 1979, and the closest thing Tbilisi has to a household festival. Producers come in from all twelve regions and set up along Rike Park and Orbeliani Square: wine, honey, wheels of cheese, mountains of fruit, churchkhela hung in bunches like candles. Folk ensembles and chidaoba wrestlers take outdoor stages. The moment everyone waits for is the satsnakheli — a wooden pressing trough dragged into the open, where people climb in and crush the grapes underfoot, singing, exactly as their grandparents did. It ends with a riverside concert that runs until dawn. Entry is free, all of it.

Timing it: the first weekend of October — expect 3–4 October in 2026, but treat that as provisional: dates are announced only three or four weeks ahead, and the 2025 edition was moved to 20–21 September because of elections. Check georgia.travel/tbilisoba in September. Come before noon if you want to browse the stalls in comfort — the Old Town genuinely fills from mid-afternoon — and stake a spot near the stage early for the grape-treading. Rooms tick up for the weekend; book a month out.

For a wine traveller specifically Tbilisoba is the one day you can taste from small producers who never export and will never have a tasting room — bottles brought in from the regions by the people who filled them, poured across a trestle table for a couple of lari.

Svetitskhovloba

A national public holiday centred entirely on Mtskheta, 20 km away. The Catholicos-Patriarch leads the liturgy inside Svetitskhoveli; a mass baptism follows at the river confluence below Jvari; the square outside fills with polyphonic choirs, dancers, craft stalls and food. Fireworks close it. The catch: the road in and out gridlocks and most tour operators simply cancel. If your trip covers the 14th, take the marshrutka, be in town by 9am, and plan to stay put all day — or go to Mtskheta on a different date entirely.

The reason to come · and the reason to come now

The 8,000-year cellarქვევრი · მარანი · ამბერი

Wine was not imported into Georgia. Grape domestication and fermentation are dated here to roughly 6,000 BC — some five thousand years before France — and the vessel that did it is still in use. A ქვევრი qvevri is an egg-shaped clay amphora, lined inside with beeswax and buried to its neck in the earthen floor of a მარანი marani, the cellar. Whole clusters of white grapes go in — skins, seeds, often stems — and stay for months. The earth holds the temperature steady. Nobody racks the wine off its skins.

What comes out is Georgia's contribution to the world: amber wine. Not "white," despite the grapes; a deep gold-to-copper liquid with real tannic grip, tasting of dried apricot, quince, walnut and beeswax, built to be drunk with food rather than admired. The method went onto UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2013. Georgia has preserved some 525 indigenous grape varieties — more than any country on earth — largely because Soviet planning optimised for volume and never bothered ripping the odd ones out.

The other half of the tradition is the table it's poured at. A სუფრა supra — the word means "tablecloth" — is not a dinner but a rite, called for a wedding, a funeral, a harvest, or simply a guest arriving. A tamada is elected and leads the toasts in fixed order: to Georgia, to peace, to the dead, to the guests, who are held to be heaven-sent. Nobody drinks between them. Dishes are not cleared, only added to, until there are plates on plates. Georgians have a word — shemomechama — for eating past the point of being able to stop.

A supra is a group format — here's the solo version You are unlikely to be invited to a private supra in four days, and restaurants only approximate it. What works instead: sit at the bar in a natural wine bar and let the person pouring choose (at Vino Underground they are the winemakers), or go to Kakheti, where a small family cellar routinely turns a tasting into lunch whether you asked for one or not. That is the closest a visitor honestly gets, and it is close enough.

Where to drink it in the city

Tbilisi's wine-bar culture is a decade old and completely unpretentious — grapevines grow up Old Town balconies and a neighbour's garage wine can appear on the same list as a labelled bottle. The natural cluster is Galaktion Tabidze Street in Sololaki, with more along Shardeni and Erekle II; several stay open until 2 or 3am. Two more worth knowing: Wine Factory N°1 in Vera, a converted brick factory now housing a dozen producers and restaurants, and the Warehouse bar inside Stamba Hotel, with 400-plus natural wines under a Brutalist concrete ceiling.

The four grapes to ask for by name: Rkatsiteli and Kisi as amber; Saperavi as the red; Mtsvane if you want the floral one. Then ask what the bar has that you've never heard of — with 525 varieties, that question always works.

Dzveli Tbilisi · the carved city

Balconies, brick domes, and a hillძველი თბილისი

Old Tbilisi is not a preserved quarter; it is a working, sagging, half-restored one. Carved wooden galleries lean out over shared courtyards on brackets that look one winter from giving up. An Orthodox church, an Armenian cathedral, a Persian-domed mosque and a Zoroastrian temple sit inside ten minutes of each other. In October, golden creepers climb the facades and blue balconies stand against cascades of red — the reason photographers pick this month over any other.

Three walks cover it, and they connect: the bath gorge at the bottom, the Sololaki grid behind it, and the cliff on the far bank.

Abanotubani — "the warm place"

The city's founding myth is a hunting accident: a 5th-century king's falcon fell into a steaming spring, and he named the place tbili — warm — and built a capital on it. Those springs still run. The brick and mosaic domes of the bathhouses push up out of a gorge below Narikala like half-buried kilns, and the whole district smells frankly of sulphur. Come at dawn for the architecture, come after dark for the water.

Sololaki & the balcony streets

Behind Freedom Square the streets tighten into a 19th-century grid built for merchants who had money and wanted it seen: Art Nouveau doorways, neo-Moorish entryways, wrought-iron balconies, and stairwells with coloured glass. Mornings here are almost residential — cats on the steps, mist over the terracotta. Walk Lado Gudiashvili Square, Asatiani Street, and take the Betlemi stairs up into the rooftops.

A note for the stained-glass hunters The famous "Kaleidoscope House" on Betlemi has moved and its original building is under construction — don't plan around it. The consolation is that Sololaki's stairwells are full of coloured glass; ring nothing, look up through open doorways as you pass, and be content with what you find.

Across the water — Avlabari and the Metekhi rock

Cross Metekhi Bridge and the city turns older and rawer: crumbling houses, cats on walls, and the single best set of views back at the Old Town. The steep cobbles of Wine Rise — ღვინის აღმართი, named for exactly what you'd expect — climb from Europe Square under Queen Darejan's cantilevered blue balcony toward Mother of Georgia framed in timber.

The other three centuries

Boulevards, glass, and concreteრუსთაველი · აღმაშენებელი

Tbilisi's architecture did not stop with the balcony. Under Tsarist rule the city built itself a European face; the Soviet century added mosaics, monuments and housing experiments; the 2010s bolted on glass. Walking all three in an afternoon is one of the real pleasures here — and October is when the plane trees do half the work for you.

The restored boulevards

Nineteenth-century merchants built palatial Art Nouveau and Neoclassical frontages along Rustaveli and, across the river, Aghmashenebeli — streetscapes that could pass for Vienna, except that carved wooden galleries hang above them and every sign is in Georgian. Go early. Emily Lush's advice for October is exact: get to Aghmashenebeli before the street sweepers, while the pavement is still "completely blanketed in syrup-coloured foliage."

The river and the glass

The Mtkvari is the city's other axis. Michele De Lucchi's Bridge of Peace arcs across it in glass and steel — a deliberate provocation against the medieval skyline behind, lit at night by thousands of LEDs. The best stretch on foot is the Left Embankment between Marjanishvili and Saarbrücken bridges, where the trees go fiery amber and you get the Public Service Hall (locals call it the Mushroom Building), the gold dome of Sameba, and the bridge, all framed in leaves. Walk it before 9am: soft light, no traffic, leaves still down.

Concrete and the Soviet century

The counterweight to all that plaster, and genuinely worth a half-day. Chugureti's Fabrika — a sewing factory turned courtyard of container bars and studios — is where the city's street-art scene began; the murals spread out across the surrounding walls, and October's soft light with fewer leaves in the way is the best time to shoot them. Further out, two monuments to ambition: the 35-metre carved pillars of the Chronicle of Georgia, and the Nutsubidze Skybridge, three 1970s housing towers strung together with iron walkways.

Where to stand, and when

The amber hourხედები

Tbilisi is a city in a gorge, which means it is a city of terraces — and in October the low sun does something to the terracotta and the plaster that it does at no other time of year. Two caveats shape the choices below: Narikala's walls are shut, and the celebrated Tabor plateau panorama — Emily Lush's "Tbilisi from her best angle" — now has a construction crane across the left of the frame. The workaround is better than the original anyway.

Go to King Parnavaz Garden in Avlabari, a scrap of hilltop square five minutes from the metro, where Metekhi Church, the fortress silhouette and the whole Old Town line up and glow. It is the best sunset in the city and it is almost always empty — "I almost always have it all to myself." Arrive 45 minutes before the sun goes.

Eating, and when

Walnuts, clay pots, dumplingsხინკალი · ხაჭაპური · ფხალი

Georgian food sits at a crossroads and admits it: Persian walnut pastes, Ottoman vine leaves, Russian dumplings, all absorbed and made local. Benjamin Kemper, who spent a month eating here for Saveur, put it well — "food ambushes you in the Georgian capital, in the best sense imaginable." Two structural things to know: portions are enormous and built for sharing, so as a solo diner order roughly half of what the menu implies; and a waiter will assume you want everything at once unless you say otherwise.

What to order, and where

  • Khinkali ხინკალი

    Hand-pleated dumplings holding spiced pork-and-beef and a mouthful of broth. Never use a fork: grip the topknot, bite a small hole, drink the broth first, then eat the rest and leave the knot on the plate — they're how you count. Best at Racha (watch them folded through the kitchen window) and Kakhelebi.

  • Khachapuri ხაჭაპური

    Cheese bread, in something like 47 regional forms. In Tbilisi you'll meet round imeruli and boat-shaped adjaruli, topped with a raw egg and butter you stir in at the table, then tear the crust off to dip. Retro has the city's most-praised adjaruli; Gunda uses endemic Georgian flours, including a wheat strain recovered from a Danish seed bank.

  • Pkhali ფხალი

    Spinach, beet, leek or green bean minced and bound with walnut, garlic and khmeli suneli, pressed into little rounds and crowned with a pomegranate seed. Arrives cold and first. Shavi Lomi serves a whole rainbow of them in a wooden bowl.

  • Badrijani nigvzit ბადრიჯანი ნიგვზით

    Fried aubergine rolled around spiced walnut paste, pomegranate on top. The natural partner for amber wine — the tannins want the walnuts. A good test of any kitchen.

  • Lobio ლობიო

    Kidney beans slow-cooked with coriander, marigold petals and chilli, served in a clay ketsi with a disc of mchadi cornbread as a lid. Salobie Bia is named for the dish and does the version everyone cites.

  • Mtsvadi მწვადი

    Meat grilled over embers — in Kakheti, often over vine cuttings, which gives it a faint resinous smoke. Onion, pomegranate, tkemali sour-plum sauce. Smaller than it looks; not a main course alone.

  • Shkmeruli შქმერული

    Chicken from the Racha highlands, pan-fried then finished in an entire head of garlic cooked in butter. Order bread you don't need.

  • Churchkhela ჩურჩხელა

    Walnuts on a string, dipped over and over in thickened grape must until they set into something between a candle and a nougat. Chewy, only just sweet, intensely of grape. Originally a 12th-century soldier's ration — it keeps two years.Freshest Oct–Nov

  • Chacha ჭაჭა

    Distilled from the skins and stems left after pressing — grappa's rougher cousin, 40–60%. Usually homemade, usually in an unlabelled plastic bottle behind the counter. Asking for it is a signal.New vintage from October

  • Chakapuli ჩაქაფული

    Lamb braised with tart unripe plums, white wine and a garden's worth of tarragon. Georgia's great spring dish — listed here so you know what you're missing, and don't order a limp off-season version.April–May only

The shape of a Tbilisi day

  • 08:00–10:30
    Sauzme საუზმე

    Barely a meal. Bread from a neighbourhood tone oven, sulguni cheese, honey, strong tea, eaten fast at home. There is no Georgian breakfast-restaurant tradition — cafés for visitors open 9–10am. Bakeries are baking from 6.

  • 13:00–15:00
    Sadili სადილი

    The main meal for most Georgians, and unhurried. Cold starters, cornbread, a stew, often wine even on a weekday. Dining rooms fill 1–3pm; there are essentially no lunch specials, so prices are the same as dinner.

  • 15:00–17:00
    The khinkali break

    A Tbilisi habit: a round of dumplings with cold beer or house wine, mid-afternoon, no occasion required. Dedicated sakhinkle stay busy right through.

  • 19:00–midnight
    Ghvino saaghamo ღვინის საღამო

    The wine evening — the city's real nightlife. A bottle of amber and some cheese, stretched over two hours. Bars fill by 8pm at weekends. In October the winemakers themselves come into town.

  • 18:00 onward
    Supra სუფრა

    Not a nightly habit but the occasion everything is organised around — two to five hours minimum. See the cellar.

Taverns, bakeries and one enormous bazaar

The old repertoire, done properly and cheaply. Nothing here needs booking, and most of it is better at lunch. Dezerter Bazaar deserves its own hour in October: strings of dried persimmons hanging from the awnings, pomegranates and chestnuts and ombré finger-grapes in the crates, and marigolds — as Emily Lush has it, "the colour of sunbeams" — filling the gaps between. Foragers bring down chanterelles and Caesar's mushrooms from the western forests. Go before 10am.

The newer table

A small group of kitchens taking Georgian ingredients somewhere else — fermentation, heritage grain, regional cooking treated as seriously as anyone treats French. All three want booking a few days ahead in October.

Day trip · or the overnight you should take

Kakheti, at harvestკახეთი · ალაზნის ველი

Private driver 1.5–2 hrs · ~70–100 GEL/person for the day · Full day, or 1 night

This is the day the trip is actually about. Kakheti makes roughly 70% of Georgia's wine and in October the Alazani Valley is in motion: Kamaz trucks sagging under grapes, qvevri being filled and sealed, woodsmoke and fermenting must in the air, and the vines turning amber and red against a Greater Caucasus finally clear of summer haze. Between the cellars stand some of the oldest churches in Christendom — an 11th-century cathedral marooned in open vineyard, a 6th-century academy with antique qvevri lying about in the grass. Wine and stone, in the same afternoon, within twenty kilometres of each other.

Getting there, and the one decision that matters

Hire a driver. Marshrutkas do run — Sighnaghi from Ortachala station, Telavi from the Isani area, both under two hours — but everything worth seeing is scattered between villages, the last vans back leave around 17:00–18:00, and Georgia's drink-drive limit is 0.03 BAC, which means self-driving and tasting are mutually exclusive. A car with a driver for the day (GoTrip, or arranged through your guesthouse) runs about 70–100 GEL per person and solves all three problems at once. For harvest specifically, Eat This! Tours runs both a real family harvest day and a "simulation" version with guaranteed daily departures through October — from around 340–410 GEL including transfers from Tbilisi.

One day or two? A long day (8am–8pm) gets you Sighnaghi, Bodbe and one or two cellars, and that is a good day. But an overnight in Telavi unlocks the Alaverdi–Ikalto–Tsinandali triangle, which is the densest concentration of medieval architecture in the region, and it means you can taste at three cellars instead of one without watching the clock. Take the night. Come back over the Gombori Pass — dense beech forest at 1,620 m in full October colour, honey sellers at the roadside, a marked lookout at the top, and the 3rd-century Ujarma Fortress at roughly the halfway point.

Booking, honestly

Each vineyard's harvest lasts two or three days and the dates are fixed only a week or two ahead, on sugar levels. Some cellars close to drop-ins during their own picking and take only people who pre-booked a harvest package.

Small family cellars: 1–2 days' notice, by phone or through your guesthouse.
Harvest experiences: weeks. Beds in Sighnaghi/Telavi in early October: 3–4 weeks; prices rise 20–40%.
Commercial wineries (Khareba, Shumi, Teliani) take walk-ins — but call in October, as shoulder-season hours shift.

Sighnaghi or Telavi? Sighnaghi is the beautiful one — walled, cobbled, balconied, and correspondingly touristed and pricier, with no wineries inside the walls. Telavi is the working regional capital: better restaurants, better transport, and everything within 20–30 minutes. For a solo wine-and-architecture traveller, Telavi wins on substance and Sighnaghi on atmosphere.

The cellars

The scale runs from a man ladling new wine out of an open qvevri into a clay bowl on his own veranda, to 7.7 kilometres of tunnel bored into a mountainside with the same machinery that dug the Tbilisi Metro. Do at least one of each. The small ones are the revelation; the big ones give you the context to understand what you just drank.

The stone

Kakheti's churches are older than most of Europe's and considerably emptier. Dress code is enforced at every single one — shoulders covered, long trousers or skirt, hair covered for women; wraps and scarves are lent at the gates. Note that Tuesday–Sunday is the rule for the museums and fortresses here: Batonis Tsikhe, Gremi, the Sighnaghi museum and Tsinandali all close Mondays.

The Telavi circuit & the north valley — what the overnight buys you

Alaverdi, Ikalto and Dzveli Shuamta sit within twenty kilometres of Telavi and make one satisfying morning; Gremi and Nekresi are further east and pair naturally with the Khareba tunnels.

Eating in the valley

Kakhetian cooking differs from Tbilisi's in specifics worth chasing: mtsvadi grilled over grape-vine cuttings; shoti შოთი, the sword-shaped bread slapped against the inside wall of a clay tone oven; and above all cold-pressed Kakhetian sunflower oil — deep gold, intensely nutty, poured raw over salads and bread, and the single flavour that tells you which region's table you're sitting at. Buy a bottle at Telavi Bazaar along with guda, a pungent sheep's cheese aged in a skin bag.

💡 Eat early. Lunch is the main meal out here — 13:00 to 15:00 — and a kitchen you reach at 14:30 may be winding down. Winery tasting rooms are also at their most alive at exactly that hour. Doli in Telavi doesn't open until 13:30; Odlisi is evenings only, from 18:30, and needs booking days ahead by Instagram message.

Staying the night

One night, base in Telavi. Two nights: Sighnaghi first — walk the ramparts at dusk and wake before the coaches — then move onto the valley floor at Tsinandali or Kisiskhevi and sleep amongst the vines.

Day trip · a château and the oldest stone in Georgia

Mtskheta, where it all startedმცხეთა

Marshrutka 2 GEL / 45–60 min from Didube · or driver ~50 USD · Half to full day

Mtskheta was the capital of the Kingdom of Iberia for the better part of a millennium, and it is where Georgia adopted Christianity in 337 AD — which makes it, for a country whose identity is stitched from faith and vines, the single most loaded 20 kilometres in the country. Three UNESCO-listed monuments sit within three kilometres of each other; a fourth, and the best of them according to people who live here, is eleven kilometres further west in a landscape of sandstone cliffs that looks nothing like the rest. October turns Svetitskhoveli's yellow stone to burnished gold, and the tour coaches have thinned since September.

Make it a wine day too

Mtskheta is usually sold as a churches half-day, which undersells it for anyone who came for the wine. Château Mukhrani sits on the same road, 25 minutes out of Tbilisi and 15 minutes short of Mtskheta — a 19th-century Bagrationi palace estate founded by a prince who imported the French château idea wholesale, ruined under the Soviets, restored from 2002, and now farming organically with twenty-odd vaulted underground cellar compartments. Crucially it pours Kartli varieties you won't meet in Kakheti: Shavkapito, an earthy, tart, garnet red; the bright citrus of Goruli Mtsvane; Tavkveri; and qvevri releases. The four-wine Tour & Classics is 70 GEL, the seven-wine Master Class with food pairings 193 GEL, and there's a restaurant on site. Open daily 10:00–18:00; pre-book, especially on October weekends.

Then drive fifteen minutes and stand in a cathedral built in 1010.

💡 Two timings do the heavy lifting. Be inside Svetitskhoveli's walled yard at 8am when the gates open — empty, quiet, best light of the day. Then save Armaztsikhe for two hours before sunset: cross the river, climb ten minutes of wooden staircase, and you get Svetitskhoveli and Jvari in one frame with the whole town glowing amber. There's no marked entrance or parking — look for the brown sign on the highway and walk up.

A full day, in order

  1. 09:30 — Château Mukhrani: cellars, tasting, lunch at the Orangery
  2. 13:30 — drive on to Mtskheta (15 min)
  3. 14:00 — Svetitskhoveli, then Samtavro five minutes north
  4. 16:00 — taxi up to Jvari (~15 GEL) for the confluence in late light
  5. 17:30 — Armaztsikhe for sunset, or the riverside confluence at water level
  6. 18:30 — khinkali in a garage at Angelina's

Doing it churches-only? Svetitskhoveli + Jvari + Samtavro on foot and by one taxi is 3–5 hours. Add Shiomgvime and it's a full day — but Shiomgvime needs a car, and opens late at weekends. The Mtskheta railway station is still closed for restoration.

The day’s stops

Dress code is strictly enforced at all four, and the Samtavro nuns in particular run a tight ship — be discreet with a camera inside. Svetitskhoveli is the exception to the general rule that Mtskheta's churches shut at dusk: its gates stay open until 10pm.

Where to eat here

Two institutions, neither of them refined, both worth the stop. In a residential backstreet a row of concrete carports attached to family houses has been converted into restaurants — the Garajebi, the garages. You walk in through a garage door past an open kitchen where the khinkali are folded to order, and eat upstairs on a concrete terrace under grapevines looking at suburban rooftops. No English menu; the grandson translates; bring cash.

Where to sleep in the city

Not in the four days — but hard to leave out

If you have another dayკიდევ ერთი დღე

Four days fills up fast, and these are what gets cut. Each is a full day out of Tbilisi; any one of them would justify a fifth. Take the first if you want the strangest landscape in the country, the second if you'd swap a day of wine for the Greater Caucasus, the third if you're here late in the month and want the leaves.

Half a desert, on the Azerbaijan border

David Gareja Cave Monastery

Shuttle from Pushkin Park 10:45am · 40 GEL return · 1.5–2 hrs each way · Full day

Georgia's most extraordinary monastic site, and utterly unlike anywhere else in the country: more than five thousand cells cut by hand into a lunar, mineral-striped semi-desert plateau on the Azerbaijani frontier, founded in the 6th century by one of the same 13 Assyrian Fathers who seeded Shiomgvime. Lavra, the lower monastery, is fully open as of 2026 and still inhabited by monks — you walk wooden balconies pinned to the cliff face past carved tombstones and into an active chapel. Above it, Udabno and its celebrated frescoes remain closed following a 2019 border dispute; do not attempt the ridge trail, as the border guards will simply turn you back. The compensation is the Rainbow Hills, a walk through vividly banded sedimentary rock around and above the complex — dry days only, it turns to mud.

Getting there: no marshrutka goes the whole way. The Gareji Line shuttle leaves Pushkin Park near Freedom Square at 10:45am and gives you about three hours on site, 40 GEL return — but it runs only to 31 October and needs a minimum of two passengers, so message ahead if you're alone. Otherwise a GoTrip car is around 160 GEL. Bring water and your passport; there are no shops past the highway, and border guards are present. October is the right month — summer here is brutally hot and snakier.

Where this guide's motif comes from

The Georgian Military Highway — Ananuri to Kazbegi

155 km on the E117 · 3–3.5 hrs direct, 7–8 with stops · Best as 1 night, not a day trip

Tolstoy and Dumas both wrote about this road, and the drive genuinely is the point: fortresses, Soviet mosaics, a 2,395-metre pass and the cleft of the Terek valley, ending under the 5,047-metre white bulk of Mount Kazbek. For an architecture traveller there is one unmissable stop, and it is the building this entire guide is designed after. At Ananuri, 66 km out, the Church of the Assumption (1689) has facades densely carved with grapevines — tendrils, leaves and heavy bunches of stone grapes, alongside almond-eyed angels, an archaic form of the Georgian script, and a great tree spreading across the rear wall. UNESCO describes "richly decorated facades with fine relief carvings featuring human, animal and floral images." Climb the tower for the Zhinvali Reservoir; shoot the complex from the bridge downstream. Free, opens 9am.

The October caveat is real. Aim for the first half of the month: hiking is still good and trails are empty, but from mid-October Juta and Truso close with snow, nights go to freezing, fog thickens, and the Gudauri–Kazbegi stretch can shut briefly after early snowfall (check georoad.ge). A day trip forces you to skip the Gergeti hike entirely and misses the early light on Kazbek, so give it a night in Stepantsminda. Bring cash and a Magti SIM bought in Tbilisi — there's no office up there. And note this is not a wine corridor: it's the trade you make against a day in Kakheti.

Best of all if you come after the 20th

Rkoni Valley — a monastery, a queen's bridge, and the leaves

75 km · 4WD or GoTrip from ~100 GEL return · Full day

Emily Lush's pick for the best autumn day trip out of Tbilisi, and the timing is almost made for a late-October visit: the valley "glows with golden hues from mid October into early November." A signposted, mostly flat 2–3 hour walk leads from Rkoni village through woodland to a 7th-century monastery where fallen leaves pile up between ancient tree roots, and on to Tamar Bridge — a slender 12th-century stone arch crossing the river in a single span, from Georgia's Golden Age. Nearby: Ikvi Church for its vivid blue frescoes, and Chapidon Winery for a Kartli tasting in a region far less visited than Kakheti. The road in is partly unpaved and there is no public transport, so this one needs a high-clearance car or a driver.

A cave city and a very strange museum

Uplistsikhe & Gori

Marshrutka to Gori 4 GEL / ~90 min from Didube · Full day · pairs with Mtskheta en route

Uplistsikhe — "the Lord's Fortress" — is a Bronze Age cave city carved into a riverside cliff, founded around 1000 BC and inhabited until the Mongols finally emptied it in the 13th century. Roughly 500 rock-cut structures survive: Queen Tamar's Hall, probably a pagan sun-goddess temple, with an ancient winemaking pit still visible outside it; a 1st-century Caisson Hall with a geometrically carved arched ceiling; and a 10th-century basilica that is the only structure the Mongols left standing. 15 GEL, open 10am–6pm through to 1 November, allow two hours, and come in the afternoon when the coaches have gone. Sturdy shoes — the surfaces are steep, uneven and almost entirely unshaded. Fifteen kilometres west in Gori, the Stalin Museum (15 GEL, closed Mondays) is compelling precisely for what it declines to say: six rooms of hagiography with the Gulag noted almost apologetically at the end, his armoured railway carriage parked in the garden, and the wooden hut he was born in enclosed inside a grandiose marble pavilion. Climb the medieval fortress above the town afterwards for air.
