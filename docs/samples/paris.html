A babymoon · five slow days · June 2027

Standing inside
the water

Paris arranged around benches, oval rooms and long gold evenings — Monet's Nymphéas, Van Gogh's last summers, and two villages where the paintings were made.

Monet spent the last decades of his life trying to paint a surface with no horizon — water, sky, willow and lily collapsed into one shimmering plane you were meant to stand inside rather than look at. In two oval rooms at the western tip of the Tuileries, he got his wish, and in June there is a particular pleasure in it: outside, Paris is 23°C and loud with terrace chatter; inside, it is cool, hushed, curved, and there is a bench in the middle of each room, because the panels were designed to be received sitting down. For a trip built around not being on your feet all day, that is close to a mission statement.

This is a five-day, art-led babymoon shaped around three things: the Impressionist collections that make Paris unrepeatable, a pace that assumes you will want to sit down every forty minutes, and the two villages — Giverny for Monet, Auvers-sur-Oise for Van Gogh — where the paintings you have just stood in front of were actually made. Everything below flags where the stairs are, where the benches are, and which of the classic French dishes you can order without a second thought.

The weather that shapes the trip

June is the best month to be pregnant in Paris. Daytime highs sit around 21–23°C with lows near 12–13°C, warm enough for terraces and cool enough that a morning in a gallery doesn't feel like an escape from the heat. There are typically 10–12 rainy days scattered across the month, usually brief — a compact umbrella and one light layer covers it. Museums have no crowd-pressure days off in June, but they also haven't yet hit the July–August crush, and restaurants are still fully open before the August shutters come down.

The real gift is the light. At the solstice the sun comes up before six and doesn't set until nearly ten, which changes how you can build a day: front-load the museums into cool, quiet mornings, take a genuine two-hour lunch and a lie-down, then go back out at seven when the light on the Seine is at its best and the city has stopped rushing. You will see more, on far fewer steps.

What to know before you book

  • Flying window. The second trimester (weeks 14–28) is widely described as the lowest-risk time to travel. Confirm with your own provider and your carrier before you book.
  • Skip the Métro where you can. Older stations are stair-heavy with no lifts. Lean on buses, the fully step-free line 14, the RER, and taxis for cross-town hops — the fare after a long museum day is money well spent.
  • Every museum here lends a folding seat. The Orsay, Orangerie and Marmottan all loan wheelchairs, folding stools and canes free at the cloakroom, no reservation, just ID. A folding stool means you are never stuck standing in a queue.
  • Book timed slots 1–2 days ahead for the Orsay and Orangerie, and further out for Giverny and Marmottan — the point is to never stand in a ticket line.
  • Time it around 21 June if you can. The Fête de la Musique turns the whole city into a free concert (see below).
  • Money. Euro; cards accepted essentially everywhere, including the Giverny shuttle bus.

Five days, paced for two and a half people

Three days in Paris, two out in the villages. The shape assumes one real sit-down meal and one lie-down every day, one museum per day rather than two, and nothing booked before 9:30am. If energy dips, Day 5 is the one to trade — Auvers involves hills, and a slow Paris day in its place costs you nothing.

  1. 1Sat

    Arrive · the Tuileries and the oval rooms

    Settle in, then walk the flat gravel of the Jardin des Tuileries to a late-afternoon slot at the Musée de l'Orangerie — 60–90 minutes, entirely seated if you want it to be. Dinner within walking distance of the hotel; don't be ambitious on night one.

  2. 2Sun

    Van Gogh at the Orsay, then the river

    Musée d'Orsay from opening — the redesigned Van Gogh rooms and Monet's Rouen cathedrals, with a long lunch in the museum's Belle Époque dining room so you never leave the building. Afternoon rest, then a seated Seine cruise and Pont Alexandre III at golden hour.

  3. 3Mon

    Giverny — the pond itself

    Out to Normandy on the 10am gates: the Clos Normand, the step-free tunnel to the water garden, the Japanese bridge with the lilies at their annual peak. A long seated lunch at the old artists' inn, the Impressionism museum, and back to Paris by early evening.

  4. 4Tue

    Marmottan, a garden, and a room made of light

    The quietest of the great Monet collections, Musée Marmottan Monet, in a residential mansion in Passy, with the Jardin du Ranelagh next door for a bench afterwards. Goûter at four. In the evening, the Atelier des Lumières — sit or stand wherever you like while the paintings move across the walls.

  5. 5Wed

    Auvers-sur-Oise — Van Gogh's last village · or a slow Paris day

    The flat-valley version of Auvers: his room at the Auberge Ravoux, Dr Gachet's garden, Daubigny's painted studio, with a taxi up to the graves instead of the climb. Or stay put: the Musée Rodin garden, the Jardin du Luxembourg, and Montmartre by funicular at sunset.

One booking that changes the trip Buy the Marmottan + Giverny combined ticket (€27) or the Giverny + Musée des Impressionnismes ticket (€25) — both are sold on site rather than online, and they turn two separate visits into one continuous Monet argument: the pond in paint, then the pond itself.
Salle I

The night the city plays

21 June 2027 · Fête de la Musique · free, everywhere, all evening

On the longest evening of the year, France stops charging for music. Since 1982, every 21 June, the whole country plays outdoors and free — string quartets in courtyards, a jazz trio on a pharmacy step, a choir on the cathedral forecourt, teenagers with amps in a square, all of it at once and none of it ticketed. In Paris it starts in the late afternoon and runs past midnight. There is no programme to follow; you simply walk and let the next thing find you, which is exactly the right way to experience a city on a night like this when you are seven months into something more important.

The move for this trip is to go early and leave early. Between roughly 6pm and 9pm the crowds are relaxed and the light is still gold — wander the Tuileries, your own arrondissement, or the quieter Left Bank squares, catch two or three sets, then go home. After 10pm the density rises sharply in the music-heavy quarters (Bastille, Canal Saint-Martin, the Marais), and that is the part to skip rather than push through.

Because 21 June 2027 falls on a Monday, it dovetails neatly with the itinerary above — many museums close Mondays anyway, so it makes a natural Giverny day with the festival waiting when you get back. Book dinner ahead if you want a table that night; restaurants fill and service runs late.

Programmes are published only weeks in advance — check the city's own listing once you have dates.

Also on in June Out at Versailles, the Grandes Eaux Musicales run the fountains to baroque music on weekends and most Tuesdays, with illuminated Grandes Eaux Nocturnes on Saturday evenings from early June. Exact 2027 dates are published roughly six to nine months out — worth a check if you add the Versailles day at the end of this guide.
Salle II · the reason you came

Three rooms, one obsession

Monet and Van Gogh in Paris — and all three collections are step-free, bench-lined and lend you a stool.

Paris holds the Impressionists in a way no other city can, because this is where they were argued over first. The name itself was coined as an insult in 1874 by a critic looking at a small canvas of a harbour at dawn — Impression, soleil levant — and that painting now hangs in a quiet mansion in the 16th, about twenty minutes from the room where Monet's final, wall-sized water lilies wrap around you. Between them sits the Orsay, a Belle Époque railway station converted into the greatest single hoard of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist painting anywhere, with Van Gogh given his own redesigned suite of rooms.

Three museums, three days, one per day. Together they run the whole arc: the word being invented, the movement at full strength, and one man's forty-year attempt to paint the same pond until it stopped being a pond.

How to visit these three without tiring Each is a 60–90 minute visit except the Orsay, which deserves a half-day — so pair the Orsay with lunch inside the building rather than trying to add a second sight. All three lend folding seats and wheelchairs free at the cloakroom (ID, no reservation). The Orangerie's oval rooms have a bench in the centre of each; the Orsay has benches through the galleries and lifts to every floor; the Marmottan's Monet collection sits in a level basement rotunda, with the first floor reachable by lift. Book timed entry for all three and you will not stand in a single line.

The one painting to sit down in front of

In the Orangerie's second oval room, walk to the far curve and sit. The Nymphéas panels were installed to Monet's own specification in 1927, months after his death: no frames, no horizon line, curved walls so the water closes around your peripheral vision. He wanted the viewer to have "the illusion of an endless whole, of a wave with no horizon and no shore." The trick only works from the bench, at a distance, without moving — the painting resolves and then dissolves as your eye travels, which is the entire point and is impossible to catch while walking past.

Downstairs, the Walter-Guillaume collection adds Renoir, Cézanne, Modigliani and Soutine in ordinary-sized rooms — a good, quick counterweight when you want to look at paintings that behave normally again.

  • Starry Night Over the RhôneArles, 1888. Gaslight on water — the one to see before Auvers.
  • La Salle de danse à ArlesFlat, crowded, almost a poster; the boldest colour in the room.
  • The Church at AuversPainted in June 1890, weeks before he died. You can stand where he stood on Day 5.
  • Rooms 36–37Van Gogh's redesigned space, opened 2024, tracing Paris → Arles → Auvers.

Salle III

Slow Paris

Everything else in the city, chosen for benches, shade, step-free access and a good view from a sitting position.

Paris is built for exactly this kind of trip and doesn't get enough credit for it. Half the great pleasures here are stationary: a chair dragged around a pond, an hour on a boat, a bridge at the moment the gold statues catch the light. What follows is organised not by district but by how much effort it asks of you — gardens where you sit, water where you're carried, and evenings where the city does the work.

Gardens, and the chairs in them

The Parisian garden is a public institution with a specific rule: the chairs are free and you may move them. All four below are flat, gravel-pathed, thick with benches and — crucially for June — properly shaded. Two of them sit within a few minutes of the museums on your list, which makes them the natural half of each museum day.

Seen from the water and the bridges

An hour on the Seine is the single most efficient sightseeing in Paris measured in monuments per step: the Eiffel Tower, Notre-Dame, the Louvre and the Orsay all arrive at your seat. Boarding at the river-level docks is step-free via installed ramps, and the boats are wheelchair-adapted (capped at four wheelchairs per boat). Do it in the late afternoon and step off in time for the bridges at golden hour — the gilded Fame statues on Pont Alexandre III were designed for exactly this light.

The Eiffel Tower without the climb You don't need to go up it, and the best photographs aren't taken from it. The frontal postcard view is from the Trocadéro terrace, which is step-free straight off the Métro — the grand staircase down to the gardens is optional. For the cinematic version, the iron double-decker arches of Pont de Bir-Hakeim are flat and a station's length away. Both are dramatically quieter at sunrise, and about 30% quieter on a Tuesday or Wednesday than a Saturday.

Evenings, and the indoor hours

June's late light means an "evening" outing can start at half past seven and still be in daylight. Two of these are indoor and weather-proof, which makes them the reserves to hold back for the day it rains.

Montmartre: take the funicular Montmartre is a genuinely steep hill and the direct route is a staircase of roughly 222 steps. The funicular runs 6am–12:45am on an ordinary Métro ticket and climbs it in under two minutes — use it in both directions. The separate dome climb inside Sacré-Cœur is about 300 winding steps up a narrow spiral: not recommended, and the view from the forecourt is the one on the postcards anyway.
Salle IV

The table, navigated

Good news: French cooking is mostly braised, baked and sauced — which means most of it is already safe.

Paris eats on ritual rather than convenience, and the ritual happens to work in your favour. The defining cuisine here is bistro cooking: tougher cuts cooked slowly in wine until they fall apart, gratins, roast chicken, tarts. Bœuf bourguignon — beef braised for hours in red Burgundy with lardons, pearl onions and mushrooms, first written down in the 1860s — is the emblem, and the same logic repeats through cassoulet, coq au vin and daube. Long, gentle cooking is exactly what makes bistro food land at the low-risk end of pregnancy dining. There is a short list of classics to sidestep, and one phrase to learn properly. That's the whole problem.

The other great Parisian format is the bouillon — late-1800s canteens for factory and market workers, serving a handful of dishes fast and cheap. Bouillon Chartier has traded since 1896 in its original Belle Époque room of brass hooks, mirrors and a century-old ticket system, and the 2010s revival (Bouillon Pigalle and others) brought back €10–15 mains of poireaux vinaigrette, œuf mayo and pot-au-feu. No reservation, fast service, and an old-fashioned menu that is easy to steer toward the safe end — a very good pre-museum lunch.

What to order, and what to skip

France's own food-safety agency (ANSES) and health portal (Santé.fr) are more specific than most English-language advice, and their guidance is what the verdicts below follow. The one genuine trap: "bien cuit" is not "well done." French kitchens routinely send out a bien cuit steak with a warm pink centre. The phrase that actually works is sans aucune partie rosée — no pink at all.

The cheese question, answered properly

This is the one place where the standard advice is wrong in a way that matters. ANSES's position: pregnant women should avoid raw-milk cheeses — "with the exception of hard pressed cheeses such as Gruyère and Comté." The highest-risk group is the soft bloomy-rind family (Camembert, Brie, crottin), then short-ripened uncooked pressed cheeses (Morbier, Reblochon, Saint-Nectaire), then washed-rind (Munster, Maroilles).

Cutting the rind off does not make a raw-milk cheese safe — the bacteria are throughout, not just on the surface. What does make it safe is cooking it through: a cheese baked into a gratin or a tart is no longer a risk. So the way to eat France's most famous cheeses while pregnant is hot, not cold. That's worth remembering in Normandy on the Giverny day, where the four great local cheeses are all raw-milk and bloomy — and where two restaurants serve them baked.

At the table, one question settles it: "Ce fromage, il est au lait pasteurisé ou cru ?"

Say this, get that

  • « Je suis enceinte, je voudrais la viande bien cuite, sans aucune partie rosée. »I'm pregnant — I'd like the meat well done, with no pink at all. The clause after the comma is the one that works.
  • « Ce fromage, il est au lait pasteurisé ou cru ? »Is this cheese pasteurised or raw-milk?
  • « Est-ce cuit à cœur ? »Is it cooked all the way through? — for any baked cheese dish.
  • « Je ne bois pas d'alcool — qu'est-ce que vous avez sans alcool ? »I don't drink — what do you have without alcohol? Draws zero social friction here.
  • « Un apéritif sans alcool, s'il vous plaît. »A non-alcoholic apéritif, please. Completely unremarkable to order.
  • « French Bloom »An alcohol-free sparkling wine from organic Chardonnay and Pinot Noir, now on serious wine lists — ask for it by name when there's a toast.

Where to eat

Weighted toward the museums on your list and toward rooms you can linger in — a century-old bistro with lace curtains, a revived workers' canteen, the last true steak-frites specialists. Prices are rough bands, not quotes; several of these need a reservation a day or more ahead.

The four o'clock pause

Goûter is the most useful institution in France for a pregnant traveller, because it makes a mid-afternoon sit-down with cake entirely normal. Two of the best versions are inside the museums themselves. Café Richelieu, tucked into the Louvre's Richelieu wing, is run by Angelina — the tearoom famous for its thick hot chocolate and the Mont-Blanc, a chestnut-and-meringue pastry with no raw egg in it. The Musée d'Orsay's restaurant is a listed Historic Monument in its own right: painted ceilings and chandeliers left over from the building's 1900 life as the Hôtel d'Orsay, with a lighter café alongside doing seasonal salads and ice-cream sundaes. Eating there means not leaving the building at all on Orsay day, which is the point.

Outside the museums, Du Pain et des Idées near the Canal Saint-Martin is worth the detour for its escargot pastries — pistachio-chocolate spirals, best in the morning before they sell out.

Salle V · day trip

Giverny — the pond itself

Train + shuttle · about 1h15 each way · a full, unhurried day · and June is the week the lilies open

Monet arrived in this one-street Norman village in 1883 and stayed 43 years, and the second half of that time he spent essentially painting his own garden — first the flower beds he laid out in front of the pink house, then the pond he dug behind it, diverting a stream and planting willows, bamboo and water lilies until the water became the only subject he needed. The gardens are not a museum of his work; they are the work, replanted each year to his scheme. Coming here after the Orangerie closes the loop with unusual force: you have stood inside the panels, and now here is the bridge, the willow, the exact green.

For this trip it is also the gentler of the two art pilgrimages. The core sights sit within an 800-metre stretch of the flat Rue Claude Monet; the paths are level gravel and packed earth; the two halves of the garden are joined by the pedestrian tunnel Monet built under the road in 1895, so there are no steps between them; and there are benches under the willows all round the pond loop. The one exception is the house interior, which has narrow rope-lined corridors and a staircase to the upper floor with no lift — see the ground floor's yellow dining room and blue kitchen and treat the rest as optional.

Accounts differ slightly on the lilies — one visitor reports "many blooming water lilies" in early June, while another dates the true peak to July and August. The consensus across sources is that they begin in late May, build through June with the strongest showing in the second and third weeks, and reach full mass in high summer. June buys you the flowers on the bridge and in the beds as well, plus cooler air and lighter crowds than July. It is the better week to be here pregnant.

Lunch in the village

Normandy is dairy-and-orchard country, which means cream and butter in almost everything and four raw-milk protected-origin cheeses within an hour's drive. Two of these kitchens serve the region's signature Camembert baked — a tart, or roasted whole — which is precisely the pregnancy-safe way to eat it. Cider and Calvados stand in for wine here, including the mid-meal trou normand; ask instead for jus de pomme pétillant, sparkling apple juice made by the same producers without the fermentation.

Built into the same journey

Vernon, the town you change trains in

No extra transport · 15–40 min · flat, and indoors when you want it

Every route to Giverny already stops here, which makes Vernon the easiest add-on in this guide — a half-timbered river town rather than a platform. Turn left out of the station and the Seine path takes you in a few minutes to the Vieux Moulin, the timbered mill hung improbably over a broken bridge arch that Monet painted as Houses on the Old Bridge at Vernon. If the weather turns or the legs have had enough, the Musée de Vernon is a small, cool, seated-friendly room holding Impressionist works including paintings by Blanche Hoschedé-Monet, Monet's step-daughter, and one of only four circular tondo water-lily canvases in the world — a startling thing to find in a town museum. The Collégiale Notre-Dame, 11th–17th century with good glass, is a flat stop near the station.

💡 Build it into the gap on the way back, not the way out — protect the 10am opening at the gardens.

Salle VI · day trip

Auvers-sur-Oise — seventy days

Train from Gare du Nord · about 1 hour · half to full day · flat village, steep plateau — this guide splits them

Van Gogh got off the train here on 20 May 1890, rented an attic room above a café for 3.50 francs a week, and in the seventy days before he died painted roughly eighty canvases — the church with its buckled roofline, the town hall with bunting up for 14 July, Dr Gachet's garden, and the wheat on the plateau above the village. He is buried at the top of that plateau beside his brother Theo, the two graves joined under a single sheet of ivy that Dr Gachet is said to have planted. If you have just stood in front of The Church at Auvers at the Orsay, this is a strange and moving hour: the building is still there, still leaning, on the same rise above the same lane.

In June the wheat is green-going-gold rather than the harvest colour of Wheatfield with Crows, which he painted in July — closer to his Green Wheat Fields. Sunset is near ten, so a late-afternoon arrival still gives hours of light.

Read this before you plan the day Auvers sits in the Oise valley and the sights are on two levels. The station, the town-hall square and Van Gogh's inn are low and flat — the village's own trail data puts the low point at 27 m. The church is partway up a slope; the cemetery, the wheatfields and the château sit at 105 m, a rise of roughly 75 m over 700 m–1 km of paved street. One first-hand account calls the cemetery climb simply "quite steep," and there are no confirmed benches or toilets at the church or the cemetery. There is no shuttle in the village.

The fix is cheap and obvious: Uber operates in Auvers, and cars reach the cemetery gate by a one-way loop. Take a taxi up from the town-hall square, spend as long as you want at the graves, and walk down. Downhill is the easy part. The château likewise has its own car park at the Chemin des Berthelées, so a taxi drops you at the entrance and skips the climb entirely.

The flat village — the whole day, if you want it

You can have a full, genuinely satisfying Auvers day without leaving the valley floor: his room, his doctor's house and garden, the painted studio of the painter who drew the whole colony here in the first place, and an absinthe museum with a garden and a bar to sit in. It's about 450 flat metres from the station to the town-hall square, and everything below is within a few minutes of that.

Up on the plateau — worth a taxi

Everything in this group sits at the top of the climb described above. Take them by car or taxi, in whatever combination you have energy for; the graves are the one most people would not want to miss. The "Racines" (Tree Roots) site behind the church — identified in 2020 as the subject of his last painting — can only be visited with a guide, and the ground is uneven; skip it unless it means a great deal to you.

Practicalities that actually matter here

Getting there: Transilien line H from Gare du Nord, roughly 45 minutes to just over an hour. On weekends and holidays from April to October a seasonal direct Train des Impressionnistes runs with no change at all — in a recent year departing Paris Nord 09:38, arriving Auvers 10:22, returning 18:15. On other days you change at Valmondois or Pontoise. A Navigo or Mobilis pass covering zone 5 includes the trip. Confirm the 2027 direct-service dates before you commit — it's the single biggest comfort difference of the day.

At the station: the platforms are quais non rehaussés — no awkward high step up into the train, which is better than many French stations. A pedestrian underpass links the two platforms and it is not confirmed step-free: ask the agent which platform serves your return before you cross. The ticket window is staffed weekdays 07:30–14:40 only, so on a weekend there's no help on site. There is a free toilet by the ticket hall.

Toilets and benches: confirmed at the station, on the town-hall square, and in the Musée de l'Absinthe's garden bar. Beyond that, use the paid attractions. Not confirmed at the church or cemetery.

The low-exertion route

Valley floor only, in order, all flat:

  • 1 · Station → town-hall square 450 m, paved, ~6 min at a slow pace. Benches on the square.
  • 2 · Maison de Van Gogh Room No. 5 and the film. Ground-floor restaurant and shop are step-free; the room itself is up a narrow period stair.
  • 3 · Maison du Docteur Gachet Recently restored, free, flat, with its garden.
  • 4 · Musée de l'Absinthe Belle Époque café interiors, an herb garden, and the À l'Heure Verte bar — the day's proper sit-down break, with non-alcoholic options.
  • 5 · Maison-Atelier Daubigny 200 m² of walls painted by Daubigny with Corot and Daumier. Weekends only. Van Gogh painted this garden.
  • + Taxi to the graves Then walk back down. The one splurge worth making.

The Musée Daubigny moved to temporary premises at the Maison de l'Isle, Rue Marcel Martin, for a two-year renovation from October 2025, so it may still be there in June 2027; the Maison-Atelier Daubigny opens weekends only. Book the Auberge Ravoux room slot ahead — it runs as small timed groups and photography inside is forbidden.

Salle VII

Where to rest

Two Left Bank quarters, chosen on one criterion: how little you have to walk to the paintings.

On a trip where the Métro is the enemy, the address does more work than the room does. Both quarters below put you on the Left Bank within a flat walk of the Musée d'Orsay, and both cross to the Orangerie and the Louvre by bridge rather than by train. Pick between them on what you want at the end of the day: café life on the doorstep, or genuine quiet.

Salle VIII · if you want more

Three extensions

None of these are in the five days above. Each is a swap, not an addition.

The obvious swap for Auvers on a low-energy day

Versailles, done sitting down

RER C · 35–40 min · 10-minute walk from the station · half to full day

Versailles is the least pregnancy-friendly great sight near Paris — 2,300 rooms, 800 hectares, and a "quick visit" that quietly becomes 8–12 km on foot — and it is also entirely manageable if you plan it around one fact. The estate's own little train, the standard way visitors cross the long distance to the Trianon palaces, carries an explicit official warning that pregnant women are advised not to ride it. Discover that at the kiosk and the day falls apart; plan around it and it doesn't.

The answer is the small electric vehicles hired from the north terrace of the Water Parterre — self-driven four-to-five seaters with GPS and an audio guide, €42 for the first hour, no such advisory because you set the pace and stop where you like. With one of those, do the art in the morning and drive the rest: the Hall of Mirrors, 73 metres and 357 mirrors, will be the most crowded room of your trip, so budget patience rather than lingering; then turn south to the Gallery of Great Battles, thirty monumental canvases running almost the length of the south wing, and the Coronation Room with David's Coronation of Napoleon — both far quieter than the royal apartments. Lifts serve every visitor level, and a step-free priority pedestrian walkway crosses the Honour Courtyard: ask the gate staff about it even without a mobility card. Then drive out to the Petit Trianon, which one visitor found "not filled to the brim with people, so we were able to peruse the palace at our own pace" — note its first floor isn't accessible, with a ground-floor multimedia room standing in — and stop for a long lunch on the shaded terrace of La Petite Venise on the Grand Canal. Free toilets, changing tables and water points are scattered across the estate.

Take RER C to Versailles Château Rive Gauche: not the fastest train but by far the shortest walk at the far end, 10 minutes against 17–20 from the other two stations. A Paris t+ ticket is not valid — buy singles or use a Navigo/Mobilis covering zones 1–4. On any weekend or most Tuesdays in June the gardens charge separately for the Grandes Eaux Musicales; a Passport ticket covers it.

💡 If the palace sounds like too much altogether, the Musée Lambinet in Versailles town is the antidote: an 18th-century mansion of paintings and decorative arts, five minutes from Rive Droite station, €7, and almost empty. Reserved mobility parking sits directly outside.

Add a day, or trade Auvers for it

Rouen — the cathedral, thirty times over

Direct from Paris Saint-Lazare · 1h10–1h30 · a full day

Monet rented rooms across the square from Rouen cathedral and painted its façade more than thirty times in different light, several of which you will have seen hanging together at the Orsay. The Gothic old town is flat and compact, and the Musée des Beaux-Arts holds a strong Impressionist collection of its own. It's a direct train — no transfers at all, which is more than Giverny or Auvers can offer. Don't try to bolt it onto the Giverny day: Rouen sits 65 km beyond Giverny in the wrong direction, and stringing them together means three train legs.

Only if you have a car

Château de La Roche-Guyon

5 minutes from Giverny by road · no public transport link · half day

A château built into a chalk cliff above the Seine, on the stretch of river Monet, Renoir and Pissarro all painted, with a kitchen garden along the water and a clifftop keep. It is physically the closest thing to Giverny in this guide and the hardest to reach without a car — and doing two ticketed sites in one day works against everything else here. Treat it as an alternative half-day rather than a same-day pairing. Open daily 1 April–1 November, 10am–6pm.
