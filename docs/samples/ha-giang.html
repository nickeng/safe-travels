Morning

Cao nguyên đá Đồng Văn

A limestone plateau on the Chinese border where twenty ethnic groups still measure the week not in days but in market mornings — and a road called Happiness clings to the cliff above it all.

It is four in the morning in Mèo Vạc and the town is already awake in the dark. Cattle come down the lane in ones and twos, hooves on wet concrete, a boy walking behind each one with a stick. Inside the market hall the wood fires are lit and the thắng cố cauldrons have been simmering since before midnight — horse bone, star anise, cardamom, cinnamon, twelve mountain spices going soft in the steam. By six the square is full: Hmong women in pleated indigo and magenta, Dao in red headscarves, Lô Lô, Giáy, Nùng, Tày, some of whom set out from their villages the previous afternoon. Corn wine is poured out of jerry cans into small glasses before anyone would call it breakfast. By eleven it is all over, and the plateau is empty again for another six or seven days.

That rhythm — chợ phiên — is the single most useful thing to understand about Hà Giang, and it is the thing you asked about. Every other decision on this trip (which direction you ride, where you sleep, which morning you set an alarm for) falls out of it. So this guide is built market-first: the calendar comes before the route, and the route is arranged to put you in a market town the night before, not the morning of.

One piece of housekeeping before anything else. Hà Giang province no longer legally exists. On 12 June 2025 it was merged into Tuyên Quang province in Vietnam's nationwide administrative reform, and Đồng Văn, Mèo Vạc, Yên Minh and Quản Bạ were reorganised into communes. Everyone — guides, homestay owners, road signs, and every guidebook including this one — still says Hà Giang, and the loop is unchanged. But if a mapping app or a booking site tells you your homestay is in Tuyên Quang, it is not a mistake.

Why October is the right call

You have picked the best month of the year, and not by a small margin. The rains that cause the landslides finish in September; the fog that swallows the passes doesn't arrive until December. In between sits a dry, clear, cool-mornings-warm-middays window that every rider's account agrees on. October also catches two things at once that don't overlap for long: the rice terraces finishing their golden harvest in the southern valleys around Mậu Duệ and Du Già, and the buckwheat (tam giác mạch) opening white in late September and turning pink across the Đồng Văn plateau through October and November.

The one date that matters

Nothing else in October forces your hand. There is no Vietnamese public holiday in October 2026 — Tết is 17 February, National Day is 2 September — so no closures and no domestic-holiday crowding. The Khâu Vai Love Market, Hà Giang's famous once-a-year gathering where former sweethearts reunite for a night of singing, falls on the 26th–27th day of the third lunar month, which is late April to mid‑May. It is the one great thing you will miss, and worth knowing about for a future spring.

Six days, and how to spend them

Most people ride this in three or four days, and most people regret it. Six is the right number for two reasons. It buys you the slow half-days that the loop actually rewards — Mã Pí Lèng ridden at 30 km/h with a dozen stops instead of blasted through in an hour, the Sky Path walked, the Nho Quế boat taken. And crucially, six days gives you a Sunday with a day of slack on either side, so you can build the whole route backward from whichever Sunday falls inside your window rather than hoping one lands conveniently.

Riders consistently describe 100 km as a full day here, not a morning. Our route below never asks for more than that, and two of the days ask for barely a third of it — deliberately, because those are the market mornings and the pass day.

Getting there: the overnight sleeper bus from Hanoi is the standard approach, roughly 260–300 km and 6–7 hours. Note the Bằng Phấn bus does not use Hà Giang's bus station (3 km south of the centre) — it drops at an office on 100 Trần Phú and stewards will often run you to your hotel door if you ask. Tell them the name in advance.

Before you leave home
  • Check your IDP convention. This one fact decides whether you can legally ride. See the next section — it is the most important paragraph in this guide.
  • Declare motorcycling to your travel insurer and get medical evacuation cover in writing.
  • Book Đồng Văn and Yên Minh a few days out. October is peak season and riders report towns fully booked.
  • Reserve your bike type, not just a bike — shops run out of semi-automatics and manuals and hand you an automatic.
  • Download offline maps. Viettel has the best coverage but do not rely on signal.
  • Bring cash. ATMs exist in Đồng Văn, Yên Minh and Mèo Vạc. There is none in Du Già.
Your first question

Ride It Yourselves?

Tự lái hay thuê xe ôm

Short answer: yes — if you can get a 1968-Convention International Driving Permit. If you cannot, hire a private Easy Rider and enjoy the trip from the pillion, because the alternative is riding uninsured on a road with sheer unguarded drops and a five-to-seven-hour ambulance ride to the nearest hospital that can actually help you. That single document, not your skill, is the deciding variable. Here is the whole picture.

The paperwork trap

Vietnam is a signatory to the 1968 Vienna Convention on road traffic, and only recognises International Driving Permits issued under it. It does not recognise the older 1949 Geneva Convention permit — which is the kind issued by the United States, Canada, Australia, Japan and Singapore. The UK and most of continental Europe issue 1968 permits. To ride legally you need a 1968 IDP with the motorcycle class stamped (A1 up to 125 cc, A above), carried alongside your home motorcycle licence, and you must obtain it at home before you fly — you cannot get one in Vietnam.

Be careful about permits sold online by private bodies such as the IAA, or by AAA: many are 1949-Convention documents or simply private translations dressed up to look official. They are marketed as if they solve this problem and they do not.

Rental shops mostly don't check. That is not the same as being legal, and it is emphatically not the same as being insured. The consequence that bites is not the fine — it is that an insurer can deny a medical claim on the grounds that you were riding unlicensed under Vietnamese law, at which point every cost is yours. Separately, standard travel insurance usually excludes motorcycling altogether unless you declare it and buy the add-on; confirm in writing that yours covers riding at your engine size and emergency medical evacuation and repatriation.

Police checks are concentrated right around Hà Giang City and have tightened noticeably: officers now specifically look for the bike-class stamp, and reported fines for an invalid licence have risen to 6–8 million VND, with bikes impounded and riders turned back. Breathalyser stops are routine in the mornings, and the legal limit is zero — which matters more than you'd think in a place where corn wine arrives before breakfast.

Be honest with yourselves about this

In April 2026 a 19-year-old British visitor, Orla Wates, died in a motorbike accident on this loop. She was riding pillion, with a local driver. The case drew international coverage, led to a criminal charge against the driver, and prompted a provincial directive requiring motorbike-tour operators to hold proper travel-agency licensing, keep contracts, passenger lists and insurance records, brief riders on safety, supply protective gear, and maintain their vehicles to standard. Vietnamese media has described crashes on this route as happening "almost daily" in busy periods. None of this is manufactured drama, and none of it should stop you going — but it is a very good reason to book with an established, licensed operator rather than the cheapest hostel-front shop, and to ride this at the pace of people who want to see it rather than survive it.

What your riding experience buys you — and what it doesn't

Transfers straight over

  • Machine control, clutch and gear feel, throttle discipline — you are not starting where a first-timer starts, and that is a genuine safety margin.
  • Hazard reading and lane discipline. Vietnam drives on the right, as most of the West does, so there is no side-of-the-road relearning.
  • Comfort with a long day in the saddle. Six days of 65–100 km legs will not break you the way it breaks people who learned to ride last Tuesday.

Does not transfer

  • Unguarded cliff roads shared with trucks and construction machinery that will not slow for you. This is a different discipline from mountain touring at home.
  • Engine braking on long steep descents — which is exactly why every rider's account recommends a semi-automatic or manual over an automatic here.
  • Livestock and children appearing without warning. Cows, buffalo and goats on the road are constant, not occasional. Slow down; do not swerve.
  • Surface unpredictability. Tarmac becomes gravel, mud or live roadworks with no warning, especially on the detours — Tà Làng and Sủng Là are named repeatedly.

The bike, and where to get it

Take a semi-automatic or manual. This is the one near-unanimous piece of technical advice in every first-hand account, and the reason is engine braking on the descents off Mã Pí Lèng and Chín Khoanh. The Honda XR150L (or XR190) is the purpose-built choice — taller, more ground clearance, better on the rough detour roads — and the natural pick for two experienced riders; expect from about US$18/day. The Honda Wave, Blade and Future semi-autos at 110 cc run 150–250k VND/day and are perfectly capable if lighter on the steepest grades. An automatic is not disqualifying — riders have done the whole loop two-up on a 125 cc Airblade — but it is second best.

In Hà Giang City, riders name Bồng Hostel (praised for bike condition, offers manual, semi-auto and automatic, gives free lessons and supplies gear), Hà Giang Hostel, Hồng Hào Hostel & Motorbikes, Style Motorbikes (Wave Alpha 110, XR150L, CB500X) and Hà Giang Amazing Tours. From Hanoi, Rentabike Vietnam has been going since 2004 and holds an International Tour Operator licence, with XR150, XR190, CRF300, CB500X and BMW GS-class bikes.

On deposits: many shops hold your passport instead of cash. That is normal in Vietnam but carries real exposure if the bike gets damaged and the shop is not reputable. Photograph the bike thoroughly before you leave, keep a copy of your passport, confirm the damage-waiver terms in writing, and buy the waiver — without it you are liable at "standard garage rates," or full replacement value on a write-off. Note that compulsory third-party insurance on the bike itself (about 100,000 VND) is a legal requirement and reputable shops include it.

If it goes wrong

Understand the evacuation chain before you need it, because it is longer than instinct suggests. On the remote stretches, the nearest care is a district hospital (bệnh viện đa khoa) for stabilisation only. From there it is onward transport to Hanoi — Vinmec, FV Hospital, or Family Medical Practice — and Hà Giang City to Hanoi is roughly 277 km, five to seven hours by road, with no airport in the province. Realistically, accident to international-standard care is many hours.

Do not plan on an ambulance. Rural response to the 115 service can take hours and rarely speaks English; flagging down a truck, taxi or private car is almost always faster.

After a crash
  • Don't move at once. Check finger and toe movement first. Keep your helmet on if a crowd gathers.
  • If a local person or animal is involved, stay calm and avoid confrontation. On-the-spot settlement of minor, clearly-your-fault incidents is the normal way here — but do it discreetly, not by flashing cash.
  • Call your insurer before agreeing to pay for anything significant.
  • You need a police report to claim, and police often won't attend a minor crash — you may have to go to the station. Note that if your IDP is invalid, reporting can itself trigger a fine or impoundment.
Border permits — genuinely unresolved

Quản Bạ, Yên Minh, Đồng Văn and Mèo Vạc all sit inside an official frontier zone, and foreigners are in principle required to hold a border travel permit (giấy phép vào khu vực biên giới). Enforcement is inconsistent: one 2025 rider was told by hostel staff it was unnecessary and skipped it; an operator describes a concrete process (passport plus plate number to a local office, 210,000 VND, issued on the spot); and a rumoured US$10 fee from 1 June 2026 does not appear to be backed by any official notice. Ask your rental shop or guesthouse in Hà Giang City on arrival whether it is currently being enforced and where to get one. This is a moving target, and "unclear" is the honest status — not "not required."

What to pack

Everything here earns its place by being non-obvious or hard to buy on the plateau. Reputable shops supply helmets and basic gear, and the 2026 provincial directive now explicitly requires them to — expect that to be enforced more visibly than in past years.

The Market Days

Chợ phiên · the periodic market

Hà Giang's markets run on two entirely different calendars, and almost every visitor gets caught out by the second one. Most of the famous names are fixed to a weekday — and, unhelpfully, nearly all of them to the same weekday. But a handful of the smaller, better ones aren't on the seven-day week at all: they run on a six-day lunar cycle, so they land on a different weekday every time. Understanding that second system is what turns a market trip from "we saw Đồng Văn on Sunday" into a week with three markets in it.

Calendar one · the fixed-day markets

The plateau's big markets almost all trade on Sunday, which is a planning problem disguised as a convenience. Mèo Vạc, Đồng Văn, Yên Minh and Tam Sơn all run the same morning, and they are hours apart. You get one of them properly per Sunday. A six-day trip normally contains one Sunday, occasionally two — so the very first thing to fix is which Sunday you have and which of those towns you want to wake up in.

Calendar two · the backward markets

Ask about chợ lùi and you will be told, depending on who you ask, either that the market "moves one day earlier every week" or that it "falls on Tiger and Monkey days." These sound like contradictory answers. They are the same answer.

Locals reckon by the traditional twelve-day can chi zodiac cycle, and each commune is assigned two animal days six apart — so its market comes round every six days. Because a Gregorian week is seven days, a six-day cycle lands one weekday earlier each time: Sunday, then Saturday, then Friday, walking backward through the week forever. Guides translate it into "one day earlier"; villagers just know it's a Tiger day.

These are the markets worth going out of your way for. They have almost no tourist traffic, minimal infrastructure, and a fair proportion of the people there walked for hours with goods or animals on their backs. And critically for a six-day trip: they can land on a Tuesday, which the Sunday markets never will.

How to pin down a date
  • Ask your homestay host the night before. Universally the most reliable method — locals track the zodiac day as a matter of course and can tell you instantly what trades tomorrow.
  • Ask your rental shop in Hà Giang City on day one and write the week's markets down. They do this daily.
  • Anchor from a known session. If someone confirms "Lũng Phìn was open Tuesday," count forward in sixes.
  • Use a Vietnamese lunar-calendar app (lịch âm) to get the can chi day for a date, then match it to the strip below.
  • Don't trust any printed calendar, including this one, for a specific date. No source publishes forward-looking dates; they all describe the mechanism.

The shape of a market morning

Whichever market you pick, the clock is the same everywhere on this plateau, and it is much earlier than instinct suggests. The single most common visitor mistake is treating a market like an attraction with opening hours and turning up at ten.

03:00–04:00
The thắng cố pots have been on since before midnight. Cattle start walking in; the first sellers claim ground in the dark. In Mèo Vạc, the town has been filling since Saturday evening — cafés busy, karaoke going.
05:00
Officially open. Stalls spilling out of the market hall into the lanes; families arriving on foot who set out the previous afternoon. Corn wine already being poured.
06:00–10:00
The market. This is the window — full colour, full crowd, the livestock pens at their busiest, and the food stalls actually cooking. If you see one hour of Hà Giang, see this one.
10:00–11:00
It thins fast. Food stalls sell out. This is the hour visitors who rode in that morning arrive to find they've missed it.
12:00–16:00
Packing up. Mèo Vạc's larger market can run to mid-afternoon; most are done by early afternoon and the town goes quiet for another week.
The rule that follows from all of this

Sleep in the market town the night before. Always. Every leg of the route below is arranged around this. Riding in on the morning means arriving at nine or ten, which means arriving after it is over. And bring small cash: nothing at any of these markets takes a card.

Being a guest at somebody's working week

These are not staged for visitors — they are how people buy pigs, get a haircut, sell a bolt of cloth and see their friends. Ask before photographing anyone up close, especially elders and children; move slowly and stay out of the way in the livestock pens, where animals move suddenly and the ground is churned mud. The meat and livestock sections are considerably more visceral than a food market at home. And at Hoàng Su Phì and Sà Phìn in particular, young people use market day to meet each other — a social function worth being tactful around.

The plateau's cooking

Eating At Six In The Morning

Ăn sáng chợ

There is no restaurant scene here, and that is the point. On thin limestone soil that won't grow much rice, more than twenty ethnic groups built a cuisine around corn, buckwheat, smoked meat and one communal cauldron — and they built it around market day, not around dinner service. Eating well on this loop means two things: showing up to a market before the sun, and eating whatever your homestay puts on the table at night. The calendar, not a menu, is what decides whether you get the good stuff.

Thắng cố, and why it can't be ordered in a restaurant

Thắng cố is the dish this plateau is known for, and its meaning has almost nothing to do with the recipe. It began as Hmong food: horse meat, bones and offal simmered for hours with about a dozen mountain spices — star anise, cardamom, cinnamon, lemon leaf — until the pungency mellows into something deep and savoury. But it was never restaurant food. It was cooked in one pot for the whole market, because market day was the one day scattered hamlets converged in a single place.

So the ritual is the dish. A low stool at the edge of the cauldron, a small bowl ladled to order rather than cooked to a menu, a glass of corn wine passed alongside, and conversation with people you may not see again for a week. Bite, sip, repeat, through the whole morning. One local guide calls eating from the communal pot "the most culturally authentic meal available anywhere on the loop" — and restaurant versions in Đồng Văn and Mèo Vạc, which do exist and are milder, are unanimously described as the lesser thing. A bowl at the market runs about 20,000 VND.

The other thing to know: corn is the rice here. Paddies are scarce on rock, so mèn mén — corn ground coarse and steamed twice into a crumbly porridge — is the Hmong household staple, almost never printed on a menu but very often set in front of a homestay guest. The same corn, fermented, becomes rượu ngô, poured from jerry cans at markets from breakfast onward.

On the corn wine

It appears within minutes of sitting down at any homestay dinner, and it is a welcome, not a challenge. Hosts pour generously; you are not expected to match them glass for glass. A smile and a hand over the glass declines a refill without giving offence.

Two practical notes. At 30–45% ABV and 1,000 m of altitude, it hits harder and faster than it would at sea level. And Vietnam's drink-drive limit is zero, with breathalyser checkpoints routine in the mornings — so the wine belongs to the evening you've already parked the bike, or to a market morning you're walking to.

The journey

Six Days, Anti‑Clockwise

Hà Giang → Quản Bạ → Yên Minh → Đồng Văn → Mèo Vạc → Du Già

Ride it anti-clockwise — Hà Giang City north to Quản Bạ, over the plateau to Đồng Văn, across Mã Pí Lèng to Mèo Vạc, then south through Du Già and back. This direction puts the pass in front of you rather than behind, keeps the two big Sunday market towns in the middle of the trip where a Sunday can be made to land on them, and saves the green, low, restful valley of Du Già for the end, when you'll want it.

Build the days backward from your Sunday. If you start the loop on a Thursday or Friday, natural pacing puts you in Đồng Văn on day three — which, with a day of slack in either direction, is exactly where you want the Sunday to fall. Then choose: Đồng Văn if you want the market and the Old Quarter and an easy roll onto Mã Pí Lèng afterwards, or Mèo Vạc if you want the biggest market in the province and its cattle pens, with the pass ridden the day before. Both are right; they are only one hour apart and you cannot do both mornings properly.

The one navigation warning

On the return leg from Du Già, do not follow the fastest route your phone offers. Riders have been sent onto a road they describe as "particularly horrendous" and technically passable but genuinely hazardous. Follow the DT181 to the QL4C instead. More generally, treat every road-condition report on this loop — including the ones below — as a snapshot: the Mèo Vạc–Du Già stretch has flipped between "the roughest section" and "the nicest" within a couple of years as roadworks progressed, and there are live construction zones sharing narrow cliff roads with heavy machinery.

Leg one · day 1 · ~50 km

Out Of The Valley

Hà Giang City → Tam Sơn, Quản Bạ

Keep this day short on purpose. Fifty kilometres and two hours of riding gets you over your first two passes and into the Tam Sơn valley by mid-afternoon — which is enough real gradient to find out whether your bike's brakes, chain and engine braking are what the shop promised, while you're still close enough to turn around. Most three-day itineraries skip Quản Bạ entirely and blast through to Yên Minh; you have the days, and this valley rewards them.

The road climbs out of Hà Giang City onto Bắc Sum Pass, hairpinning up through forested limestone with views back over the city, then crests at Heaven's Gate — a narrow gap between two peaks that was once a guarded gateway to the plateau, now the loop's first genuine oh moment, with a coffee shop parked right at the top. Halfway down the far side, pull into the small car park on the left and climb the steps to the tower: below you sit the Twin Mountains, two improbably symmetrical green domes rising from the paddy floor, which the Hmong explain as a fairy who was recalled to heaven and left her breasts behind to nurse her child. Her tears became the Miện river.

If it's a Thursday or Saturday, this is a market day and you should reroute your morning around it — Tráng Kim trades on Thursdays about 12 km from Tam Sơn and is done by noon; Quyết Tiến trades Saturdays about 7 km out, spread over roughly a thousand square metres of ground. Both are small, genuinely local, and utterly unvisited compared with Sunday's crowds. If you arrive on a Sunday, Tam Sơn's own central market is the district's biggest.

Where to sleep

Tam Sơn town puts you on top of the Sunday market. A few kilometres off the highway, Nậm Đăm is a Red Dao village of clay-walled houses that has built a genuine community-homestay economy — herbal baths, family dinners, a waterfall walk — and it is the more memorable first night if market access isn't the priority. Note the approach is a rough detour off the tarmac.

From Tam Sơn the highway climbs steadily north-east into pine forest, and the air cools. Somewhere past Quản Bạ the green stops and the rock starts: you are entering the Đồng Văn karst plateau, 2,300 km² of jagged limestone that became Vietnam's first UNESCO Global Geopark in 2010.

Leg two · day 2 · ~100 km

Onto The Rock

Tam Sơn → Yên Minh → Đồng Văn

The day the landscape changes character completely. You leave rice terraces and forest behind and climb into what one rider's account calls a "dramatic, prehistoric landscape of jagged limestone peaks and deep canyons" — corn and buckwheat grown in pockets of soil between bare rock, villages clinging to slopes at impossible angles. It is also the day of the loop's two most photographed pieces of road.

Out of Yên Minh the highway runs through a stand of pine that locals call the Đà Lạt of the North for its cool green contrast with everything around it — no trails, just a lovely stretch to ride slowly with a coffee stop, best in early or late light. Then comes Thẩm Mã Pass: five kilometres of tight S-bends at 1,500 m, the single most-photographed switchback on the loop, with a café at the viewpoint. Locals historically used this climb to test a horse's strength — the ones that failed, the story goes, became thắng cố. A little further on, Chín Khoanh — the Nine Ramps — coils down into a flat valley ringed with karst; go outside mid-morning if you'd rather not share it with the tour convoys.

Before Đồng Văn, turn into Sủng Là, the valley they call the flower garden of Hà Giang. In October its broad floor is under buckwheat — white shifting to pink — which spreads the colour out across the flat rather than confining it to a hillside, and the ~100-year-old Hmong houses of Lũng Cẩm village sit in the middle of it, including the clay-and-timber house used in the film Chuyện của Pao (The Story of Pao). It is popular with domestic tourists; go early. Five kilometres off the main road nearby, Phố Bảng is a faded old border town worth a detour on the right zodiac day.

See & do

Where to sleep · if you break the day at Yên Minh

Yên Minh is the practical alternative first night if you left Hà Giang late, and it's better supplied than most loop stops. One warning that matters for you: several of its best-known homestays lean hard into the group-tour scene — communal dinners, karaoke, rice wine, going late — which is a great night unless you have a 5am market alarm set. Choose deliberately.

The last climb before Đồng Văn tops out and the town appears below in a bowl of rock — dusty and sprawling at the edges, until you reach the U of century-old shophouses around the market square.

Leg three · day 3 · ~70 km

The Top Of The Country

Đồng Văn · Sà Phìn · Lũng Cú

A half-day of riding wrapped around whatever else this day needs to be. If your Sunday lands here, it belongs to the market and you should be in the square at first light, on foot, straight out of bed — this is the whole reason the route was built the way it was. Then head north on the spur to the flag tower at the top of Vietnam, and come back down through the Hmong king's opium palace.

Đồng Văn's Old Quarter is roughly forty houses more than a century old — one closer to two hundred — built from the early 1900s by Tày, Hmong, Hoa, Dao, Nùng and Kinh settler families in a U around the old market square. Yin-yang tiled roofs, carved wooden balconies, stone courtyards, red lanterns, French colonial and Chinese influence layered over Hmong and Tày tradition. It is at its best after dark when the tea houses and cafés light up, which pairs neatly with a dawn market on the same trip. Behind the town, a steep flight of steps climbs to Đồn Cao, a French fort built in 1927, for a 360° view over the town and the karst — go at sunset, when locals forage the hillside for herbs around you.

North, the road runs 25–30 km to Lũng Cú, where a 33 m tower flies a 54 m² flag — one square metre for each of Vietnam's fifty-four ethnic groups — on Dragon Mountain at about 1,700 m. It is 839 stone steps from the lower car park, fewer if you ride up to the higher one, and the view runs across the northern highlands into China. A kilometre and a half below sits Lô Lô Chải, a village of rammed-earth ochre houses belonging to the Lô Lô, one of Vietnam's smallest ethnic groups at around three thousand people; there are community homestays here and a café in an old minority house by the tower. On the way back, Sà Phìn — where a fortified 64-room mansion built between 1898 and 1903 for the Vương family, Hmong "kings" grown rich on the opium trade, mixes Chinese feng-shui planning with French colonial detail and Hmong craft. Look for the opium-poppy motifs carved into the pillars, and the stone bathtubs.

See & do

Coffee and cháo in the Old Quarter

Đồng Văn has a surprisingly good café scene for a town this remote, clustered around the market square, and it is where the evening happens.

Where to sleep

The choice here is genuinely between inside the Old Quarter and on the hillside above town. If your Sunday is here, sleep in the quarter: the market spills out of the hall into these exact lanes, and you wake up already in it. If it isn't, the hillside lodges trade a five-to-ten-minute ride for quiet, a view over the plateau, and considerably more character — but you'll be eating at the property, since there is nothing in walking distance.

Out of Đồng Văn the road starts climbing almost immediately, and within a few kilometres there is nothing on your right but air.

Leg four · day 4 · 23 km, all day

Mã Pí Lèng

Con đường Hạnh Phúc · the Happiness Road

Twenty-three kilometres. Give it the whole day. This is the stretch the loop exists for — National Road 4C carved directly into a wall of limestone at 1,500 m, the emerald Nho Quế river a vertical half-kilometre below, and Việt Nam's deepest canyon opening up beneath you. They call it the King of Vietnamese passes, and the road itself is called Happiness Road, which is a considerable piece of understatement given how it was built.

It took eleven years in the 1960s, and it was made largely by hand — hammers, chisels and explosives — by thousands of workers, many of them young ethnic-minority volunteers, including a crew remembered as the "suicide squad" who were lowered on ropes over the cliff face to chip the ledge out inch by inch. There's a monument and small museum to them partway along at the Happiness Road Museum, and it's worth the stop before you ride the rest of it.

Three things to get off the bike for. From the museum, the Sky Path (Vách Đá Trắng) is a paved, walkers-only trail that climbs above the road and traces its line from higher up — two to three hours at an easy-to-moderate grade, past the Great White Cliff and a natural rock window called the Eye of Mã Pí Lèng, and dramatically emptier than the road below. Do not ride it; the path narrows dangerously and it's signposted against. Second, drop down road 193A past the Pả Vì Hmong village to the Tu Sản canyon boat: 120,000₫ a head, 60–90 minutes on the water between near-vertical walls, with a stop at a riverside café on elevated platforms that one guide reckons the highlight of the whole trip. Boats leave when full, tickets are on-the-day only (office 7am–6.30pm, be there by 4pm), private kayaks and paddleboards are banned, and there are karaoke speakers on board — bring earplugs. Third, just stop at the pull-offs. There are many, they are unmarked as often as not, and the road has stretches without guardrail, so the real hazard here is not difficulty but distraction mid-corner.

Rider's note on the descents

The pass road itself is well paved and, ridden slowly, well within your ability. The two side roads are a different matter. Road 193A down to the boat marina is steep, narrow and potholed, with hydro-plant trucks coming up it. The Tà Làng Pass descent to the river — a favourite of riders who've found it — is described as "treacherous but spectacular" and only one vehicle wide. Both are firmly within reach of an experienced rider on a manual or semi-auto with working brakes, and firmly outside it in the wet. Judge on the day.

See & do

Eating in Mèo Vạc

Mèo Vạc is a working market town rather than a pretty one, and its food follows: a couple of good sit-down places around the square, and — on Sunday — the best breakfast on the plateau in the market hall itself.

Where to sleep

Sleep by the square. There is no scenic-lodge argument to make here — the entire point of a Mèo Vạc night is walking out of the door into the market at first light, and two of the options below are literally on it. Rooms facing the market street pick up noise the night before market day, which cuts both ways if you wanted waking anyway. One property to note and avoid for this purpose: the purpose-built Pả Vì Hmong village cluster on the main road is striking and popular, but a community karaoke house runs most evenings and carries through the village.

South out of Mèo Vạc on the DT176 the rock desert finally releases you. Over a few hours the limestone softens into green — and by the time you drop into Du Già you are in what feels like a different province.

Leg five · day 5 · ~72–92 km

Down Into The Green

Mèo Vạc → Sủng Trái → Du Già

Riders describe this as the most beautiful and the most demanding day on the loop, and both halves of that are earned. Seventy to ninety kilometres of narrow, winding, sometimes pothole-scarred mountain road that trades Đồng Văn's rock desert for lush emerald valleys — and which has, within the space of a couple of years, been described by riders as both the roughest section of the whole circuit and the nicest, as roadworks came and went. Check its current state before you set off.

The payoff comes in stages. Lũng Hồ viewpoint, above the road, opens onto a valley of conical karst peaks with a snack stall usually parked at it — widely rated one of the loop's best panoramas and requiring no walking at all. The M-bend is exactly what it sounds like: a section where the road below folds into the letter M as it hairpins down the slope. Around Mậu Duệ, terraced fields are visible straight from the tarmac; their peak gold is late July to early September so by October the best colour has generally gone, but it remains a good stop. And somewhere on this stretch stands the ruin of a French colonial watchtower on a hilltop, with no plaque and no explanation, which one writer fairly calls Tolkienesque.

Du Già itself is a genuine one-street rural commune — fourteen villages, around eight thousand people, Tày, Hmong and Dao, living in wooden stilt houses among paddies, ponds and roaming buffalo. It fills with motorbike groups converging from both directions in the late afternoon and goes properly quiet after dark. Du Già waterfall, a few kilometres out, is a multi-tiered cascade into a natural swimming pool with rocks to jump from; October water is cool but swimmable, and going in the morning rather than late afternoon avoids the tour groups. If it's a Friday you have a market — small, entirely local, no English, Tày and Hmong and Dao families in their best clothes trading rice, potatoes, corn, pork and chicken alongside silver jewellery and embroidered bags. (Sources genuinely disagree between Friday and Saturday here; ask your homestay host, who will know.)

See & do

Eating in Du Già

Eat at your homestay. This is near-unanimous advice and the food genuinely justifies it — Du Già's lower elevation and river valley mean fresh river fish, lighter broths and stir-fried mountain greens, a marked and welcome change after several days of plateau stew and smoked meat. There are a couple of places in the village for a drink afterwards.

Where to sleep

This is the loop's budget stop and its most atmospheric one — traditional Tày stilt houses with family dinners, homemade wine and mosquito nets, versus a newer wave of en-suite "modern" homestays. Choose on the axis that actually matters here: social versus quiet. Places on the main road catch arriving groups; ones set back up a hillock or across the river trade a short walk for silence and a sunrise over the valley. There is no ATM in Du Già — arrive with cash.

The last morning is the quiet one: a waterfall before breakfast, then a long descent west out of the mountains, following a river down until the karst flattens and Hà Giang City reappears on the Lô.

Leg six · day 6 · ~73–81 km

Back Down The Lô

Du Già → Hà Giang City

Take the DT181 to the QL4C — not whatever shortcut your phone suggests. It's the scenic way and, per riders who took the other one, the sane way. The road passes A Boong waterfall and a river viewpoint on the descent, and there's no need to rush: you have all day for a ride that takes half of it.

Then give Hà Giang City the afternoon that almost every rider skips. Two things are worth it. Ride up to Café Núi Cấm — part of which is inside and on top of a gutted old bus — for the best view in the city, then climb the stone steps another 45 minutes to the ruins of a French fort on the summit, being slowly reclaimed by jungle in a way one writer describes as faintly Angkorian. And go to the Provincial Museum, renovated in 2020, which devotes itself to the province's nineteen ethnolinguistic groups — more than a third of Vietnam's total — with a sound-and-light show and fifteen minutes of drone footage of rural life projected onto a concave screen. It also holds, delightfully, a map of the market days and locations across the whole province: the thing this guide has spent several thousand words approximating, on a wall, kept current. Go on day one if you can; go on day six if you can't.

See & do

Where to sleep · first and last nights

Hà Giang City is a logistics town, not a destination — the atmospheric places to stay are all out on the loop. What matters here is whether you land near the bus drop, whether the bike shop is a walk away, and whether they'll let you wheel a bike in at midnight. All the hostels below confirm free bike parking and free luggage storage while you ride, which is what you want for the main bag. Two specifics: NoMadders closes its gates 11pm–7am, so it fails for a late sleeper bus or a pre-dawn start however restful it is; and Four Points is the city's only international-brand hotel and the obvious treat for the final night after six days on a bike.

If you have more days · or want a different week

Three Ways To Extend

Không nằm trên tuyến chính

The classic loop is not the only thing here, and two of these are arguably better suited to what you're after than the loop itself — quieter markets, almost no other visitors. None of them fits inside six days; all three are worth knowing before you book your flights, because if your dates are flexible at all, one of them changes the trip.

The one that most rewards your interest

Hoàng Su Phì · two markets in one weekend

Add 3 days (2 minimum, 4 ideal) · ~100 km each way from Hà Giang City, 3 hrs · not reachable from the loop

Nearly 3,000 hectares of hand-cut terraces, a Vietnamese National Heritage Site since 2012, spread across six communes in the far west — and in October they are gold. The harvest runs late September into early-to-mid October, with the highest and most remote commune, Bản Phùng, ripening last, so arriving even in the second week of October gives you a good chance of catching colour somewhere. If you see only one commune, sources are unanimous that it should be Bản Phùng: the highest terraces in the district, ninety minutes of winding road from the district centre, right up against the Chinese border.

But the reason to put this first is the market weekend. Cốc Pài in Xín Mần trades on Saturday; Vinh Quang in Hoàng Su Phì trades on Sunday — and if you come in or out via Xín Mần you can take them back to back. Vinh Quang's is the district's oldest market, reckoned over two hundred years old, running two to three kilometres along the main street, drawing Dao, Tày, Nùng, H'Mông and La Chí families who wake at three or four in the morning and walk in. It sells brocade, embroidered scarves, silver, bamboo shoots and Shan Tuyết snow tea from centuries-old trees. Almost no loop traffic reaches either.

Thông Nguyên, where three rivers converge, is the practical base with the widest choice of lodging; Nậm Ty's Red Dao terraces are visible straight from the main road; Hồ Thầu leaves small patches of forest around each field to stop landslides, a visible piece of traditional land management. For a summit, Chiêu Lầu Thi rises to 2,402 m with cloud-sea views and the district's snow-tea slopes, a full day's outing.

💡 The catch is the road, not the distance. The DT177/DT178 connector has been under intermittent construction for years and rider reports from 2023 to 2026 describe the same stretch as everything from fresh pavement to boot-deep mud ruts depending on the season. Build in a buffer day and ask in Hà Giang City before committing to a route. Riding time isn't your constraint; road uncertainty is.
Ride out east instead of looping back

Bảo Lạc & Cao Bằng · a different landscape

Add 3 days minimum (4–5 for the full loop) · Mèo Vạc → Bảo Lạc ~80 km, 4–5 hrs · exits toward Hanoi

Rather than turning south to Du Già, keep going east out of Mèo Vạc and you leave the karst plateau for something else entirely: broader valleys, lower traffic, easier and faster riding than the loop, and a great deal fewer visitors. The centrepiece is Bản Giốc, the widest waterfall in Southeast Asia, on the Chinese border; alongside it the Ngườm Ngao cave system, the Phong Nậm valley, the fourteen stacked switchbacks of the Mẻ Pia pass, the Thang Hen lakes and Angel Eye Mountain.

For your purposes there is a neat piece of sequencing here: because Mèo Vạc's market is Sunday, a Sunday departure east lets you take the cattle market at dawn and then ride out, likely reaching Pác Miầu or Bảo Lâm's market the same day. Bảo Lạc itself has a daily market plus a big one every five days on the lunar calendar — a different cycle again from the plateau's six-day one, and equally unprojectable from home. Confirm locally a few days ahead.

Two routes east out of Mèo Vạc. The QL4C/QL34 via Bảo Lâm is about 80 km and moderate. The Khâu Vai river-ferry backdoor — crossing the Nho Quế by hand-poled bamboo raft with your bike aboard, then dirt through Đức Hạnh — is genuinely challenging and weather-dependent, with no ferry from June to August; October should be past the worst of the mud. Before you plan any of this, check whether your rental company allows the bike into Cao Bằng province and offers a drop-off there or in Hanoi — that single answer decides whether this is a one-way exit or an out-and-back.

A half-day detour, no extra nights

Khâu Vai · the canyon from the other side

Half a day from Mèo Vạc · ~30 km, close to an hour each way · fits inside the six days

The only one of these three you can slot into the trip as it stands. Khâu Vai is famous for a market that isn't running while you're there — the once-a-year Chợ Tình Love Market falls in spring — but the commune is worth the ride regardless. The Nho Quế cuts a canyon here just as dramatic as the one below Mã Pí Lèng but reached from the quiet side, with a boat trip through the gorge, a stone "maze" of jagged limestone formations to walk through, and villages that see almost no visitors. The memorable part is the crossing: a manually poled bamboo ferry takes you and your motorbike over the river and lands you in Cao Bằng province. There is a bridge; take the raft.

Base in Mèo Vạc — homestays in Khâu Vai itself are very basic and some are known only locally. Half a day covers the canyon, the ferry and the maze; a full day if you add a boat ride.
