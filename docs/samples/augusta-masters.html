Georgia · Four days · A group of friends

Augustathe one week a year the whole town wears green

Pines, pimento cheese, and 61 magnolias down a private driveway. A guide to the 2027 Masters — and to the river city that hosts it.

For fifty-one weeks a year Augusta is a working river city of about 200,000 people — brick cotton warehouses on the widest main street in the South, a canal from 1845 still turning turbines, James Brown in bronze downtown. Then, for one week in April, the population swells with golf pilgrims from every continent and the whole place tilts toward a private club on Washington Road that nobody can get into. Restaurants extend their hours. Homeowners hand over their keys and leave town. Azaleas and dogwoods detonate pink and white along every street, not as landscaping for the cameras but because that is simply what Augusta does in April.

Your window is Monday 5 April to Sunday 11 April 2027 — practice rounds Monday through Wednesday, the Par 3 Contest on Wednesday afternoon, and the tournament proper Thursday to Sunday. Four days is a good length: one full day inside the ropes if your badges came through, a half-day for the Riverwalk and Broad Street, an evening or two of rooftops and barbecue, and a day for horse country across the state line in Aiken, South Carolina.

What follows assumes you are here for the golf but not only for the golf — because the odds are good that at least one of your four days will not have a badge attached to it, and Augusta in bloom is a genuinely lovely place to be turned loose in.

01Getting here, and getting around

Augusta Regional Airport (AGS) sits eight miles south of downtown and runs a genuinely reduced schedule most of the year — during Masters week it fills to bursting and fares behave accordingly. The pragmatic alternative for a group is to fly into Atlanta (ATL) and drive the 2½ hours east on I-20, or Columbia, SC (CAE) and drive 90 minutes west. Either way, rent a car. Nothing here is walkable between neighbourhoods, and the two best things you'll do off-course — Aiken and the canal — are both drives.

Once you're in town, plan around the tournament's tidal traffic. Washington Road becomes a managed corridor with shuttle stops and $40–100 parking lots; the bridges into North Augusta clog at gate-open and gate-close. Lean on rideshare for evenings on Broad Street rather than hunting downtown parking, and if you're staying across the river in South Carolina, leave a real buffer for morning rounds.

02The uncomfortable truth about badges

Augusta National sells tournament tickets through its own lottery and nowhere else. Applications for 2027 opened 1 June 2026 and closed on 20 June; winners were notified by late July 2026, and badges are mailed out in March 2027. You could request up to four tickets for each practice day, four for the Par 3 Contest, and two for each tournament round — but you only ever win one day. Tournament-round names are drawn first; everyone unsuccessful rolls into the practice-round draw.

Resale is prohibited outright, and third-party badges can be — and are — refused at the gate. If your group's lottery came up empty, the honest answer is that Augusta throws a very good party outside the fence, and the section below is built for exactly that scenario.

Four days, sketched

A shape that works

  • Day 1 · Sunday or Monday. Land, collect the car, and ease in: the Riverwalk and Broad Street on foot, the green jacket at the Augusta Museum of History, an Azalea cocktail at Craft & Vine, supper at Luigi's if you can get in.
  • Day 2 · Practice round (Mon–Wed). Gates at 7. This is the day cameras are allowed — Founders Circle, Amen Corner, ball-skipping on 16. Nap after. Rooftop at Six South for sunset.
  • Day 3 · Aiken. Out by 7:30 for the training track and the oak tunnel on South Boundary, breakfast at the Track Kitchen, Hopelands Gardens mid-morning, lunch and a wander in The Alley. Sunday polo at Whitney Field if the day lines up.
  • Day 4 · Tournament round, or the free version. Badge in hand: leave the phone in the car and walk the whole property. No badge: Savannah Rapids at sunrise, Phinizy Swamp, Sconyers for barbecue, then find a bar with a patio and a television.

If your dates are still soft, arrive by Saturday 3 April: the Augusta National Women's Amateur final round is that day and the Drive, Chip and Putt finals are Sunday the 4th — two much cheaper ways onto the same grass.

1
The reason you're here

Seven days in April

The first men's major of the year, played on the same course every year since 1934, and the only one with a permanent home. What surprises first-timers is not the golf but the quiet — no corporate signage anywhere on the property, no phones ringing, concession prices frozen in 2002, and a crowd that has been taught to call itself something other than a crowd. The roars, when they come from down in Amen Corner, arrive through the pines before you can see what caused them.

WeekHow the seven days actually run

Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday are practice rounds — cheaper, looser, and the only days a camera gets through the gate. Wednesday afternoon brings the Par 3 Contest on the club's short course, an institution since 1960 when co-founder Clifford Roberts went looking for ways to "give spectators value." Players bring their children out to caddie; the running superstition is that nobody has ever won the Par 3 and the Masters in the same year. Competition begins Thursday and runs to Sunday, when the gates close thirty minutes after the green jacket ceremony.

The unofficial practice-round tradition worth turning up for: golfers skipping balls across the pond in front of the par-3 16th green, a stunt dating back to at least the late 1980s and now so expected that the gallery groans if a player declines.

A photographer's problem

You will get almost no pictures

This is the single planning fact most groups miss. If your badge is for a tournament day, you will leave Augusta National with zero personal photographs — no phone, no camera, no exceptions. Plan the golf imagery elsewhere:

  • The Washington Road gates. The Magnolia Lane entrance is shootable from the public road outside the property. It's the shot everyone actually goes home with.
  • Founders Circle — but only if one of your days is a practice round, and only with a stills camera. Free, and they walk you over.
  • Amen Corner and the azaleas at 12 — practice days only. Bring the camera Monday even if your "real" day is Sunday.
  • Everywhere else in town. Pendleton King's Azalea Walk, the headgates at Savannah Rapids, the Freedom Bridge at blue hour, the 75-foot James Brown mural — all in the pages below, all with your phone in your hand.

Practical group move: send one person on the practice-round day with a proper camera and treat that as the trip's photo day. Everyone else's Thursday-to-Sunday memories will be verbal — which, honestly, is part of the point.

2
For the days without a badge

Golf, outside the fence

The club runs no public tours and never has — the gates on Washington Road are as close as an unbadged visitor gets. But Augusta has spent ninety years building a secondary golf culture around the week, and it is genuinely good: a green jacket on display a mile from the ones that aren't, a rooftop putting green with cocktails, and a public course that Bobby Jones played on his way to the 1930 Grand Slam.

Eat the tournament without a ticket Visit Augusta publishes a free downloadable "Taste of the Tournament" passport each spring, sending you round downtown restaurants that put their own spin on the club's pimento cheese and other golf-inspired plates, collecting stamps as you go. The 2027 participant list won't post until closer to the week — check visitaugusta.com in March.
3
Beyond the gates

The city on the Savannah River

Augusta is more than a golf backdrop. It's James Brown's hometown, an 1845 canal city of textile mills, a walkable downtown of murals and rooftop bars strung along a mile of riverside boardwalk. Save the non-tournament hours for it — most of this is free, and much of it is best at golden hour when the crowds have gone back to Washington Road.

3aDowntown, on foot

Start at the water. The Riverwalk is the city's green spine — a landscaped mile along the Savannah with an amphitheatre, gardens and the Augusta Sculpture Trail threaded along it — and everything below is within a walk of it. Two blocks up, Broad Street carries the James Brown bronze and the downtown mural trail; on Saturdays the market takes over the 8th Street plaza from 8 am to 2 pm, which means it will be running on Saturday 10 April, mid-tournament. When the sun gets high, the indoor half is just as good: a former cathedral with 94 stained-glass windows, the first museum in the country devoted to Southern art, and the house a future president grew up in.

3bThe canal, and the water

The other Augusta is the one dug in 1845 — still the oldest continuously operating industrial canal in the United States, and the reason the mills are here at all. You can ride it on an open-air Petersburg boat, walk its towpath from the headgates ten miles upriver, or trade it for 1,100 acres of floodplain marsh on the southern edge of town. All of it sits fifteen to twenty minutes from downtown, which makes a sunrise walk before a 7 am gate entirely realistic.

4
Eating in Augusta

The most famous sandwich in American sport

The Washington Post once wrote, flatly, that "Augusta is not a culinary destination." That was probably fair fifty-one weeks a year and is not really the point. For one week Augusta gets a stage almost no city its size ever gets, and what it puts on that stage costs $1.50 — a cold spread of sharp cheese, mayonnaise and diced pimento on plain white bread, handed over in a fairway-green bag. Everything else here orbits that: a Greek-Italian dining room that has fed four generations of Masters champions, barbecue pits that have run since 1956, a 145-year-old grocery-turned-deli, and a chef-driven scene that has quietly got good enough for food writers to detour off I-20.

4aPimento cheese, and the small war over its recipe

Pimento cheese is Georgia's official state sandwich, but nowhere is it ritualised like this. The story starts with a husband and wife, Hodges and Ola Herndon, making sandwiches in their home kitchen on Kissingbower Road and selling them to patrons for a quarter apiece; their son drove them to the course in a delivery truck. As crowds grew, a South Carolina caterer named Nick Rangos took the contract in the 1950s and guarded the recipe until he died. The Augusta chain Wife Saver took over in 1998. Then, in 2013, Augusta National brought catering in-house — and longtime patrons swore the sandwich changed. Fans nicknamed it PimentoGate. Wife Saver's owner, who had spent years reverse-engineering Rangos's original, never revealed the missing ingredient.

Its quieter twin, the egg salad sandwich, has also been $1.50 since 2002. Between them they anchor a concessions list — peach ice cream sandwich $3, sweet tea, Georgia pecan caramel popcorn — cheap enough to be an annual news story in its own right.

4bBooking, and the week the rules change

Masters week bears no resemblance to a normal Augusta week. Restaurants extend hours, reservations move from optional to essential, and a few institutions do the opposite of what you'd expect: Luigi's closes on the tournament's final Sunday after opening every other night of the week, and the Sports Center is cash-only and shut Sunday and Monday year-round. Sources agree that bookings are required at most sit-down rooms but none of them will tell you how far ahead — so the practical advice is to phone directly as soon as your dates are fixed, and to confirm hours again in the week itself.

5
Where to sleep

The strangest lodging market in American sport

A city of roughly 200,000 absorbs six-figure single-night demand for four days. Chain hotels within three miles of the club run four to eight times their normal rate. And the default way a group of friends does Masters week is not a hotel at all — it is a rented house, because the maths is not close.

If you take one piece of booking advice Split a three- or four-bedroom house. For a group of four or more it beats multiple hotel rooms on cost per head and gets you a kitchen and a living room with a television, which is where you'll actually watch the rounds you don't have badges for. The catch: the best walkable-to-a-gate properties re-book within days of the previous year's tournament ending — twelve months out is normal for that tier — and four-to-five-night minimums are standard. Aiken's rental market is cheaper and far less competitive; three to six months out is realistic there.

5bOn the Georgia side

The Partridge Inn is the only property here with real character — a century-old hilltop hotel built around a restored 1836 house, with a quarter-mile of connected verandas and the city's best rooftop bar on top of it.

5cAcross the river, and down the road in South Carolina

Everything below sits in South Carolina, and everything below is cheaper than its Georgia equivalent for the same nights. The Willcox in Aiken — an 1898 Colonial Revival hotel at the centre of the old Winter Colony — is the one to book if you'd rather your base be a destination than a bed.

The day trip · 20 miles · car required

Aiken, South Carolina

Twenty miles east and across the state line sits the sport Augusta isn't about. In the 1880s wealthy Northern families — Vanderbilts, Astors, Whitneys — came south for the "pine vapors," decided the air was good for their lungs, and brought their racehorses with them. The Winter Colony they built never really left: 2,100 acres of privately-owned urban forest inside the city limits, dirt roads deliberately left unpaved so horses have right of way, a street sign at the corner of Whiskey Road and Easy Street, and a polo field that has been in continuous use since 1882. The City of Aiken runs an entire tourism campaign aimed at Masters visitors — Tournament Days, Aiken Nights — which tells you how well-worn this day trip is.

Drive · 25–35 min via US-1 or I-20 · half day, or a full day with polo · no transit link

6aHow to spend the day

Go early. Aiken's best hours are before nine, when the light is under the oaks and the horses are working. Start on South Boundary Avenue — a live-oak tunnel so photogenic it made the cover of Southern Living's "South's Best" issue — then five minutes on to the Aiken Training Track for the 7–11 am workout window, watched free from the Cot Campbell Clockers' Stand. Eat at the Track Kitchen, a breakfast-only room that has fed exercise riders and grooms since 1957 and functions more like a company canteen than a restaurant — the same people, the same seats, table-hopping through the meal.

Mid-morning belongs to Hopelands Gardens: fourteen acres of former Winter Colony estate behind a serpentine brick wall, free, with century-old live oaks, deodar cedars, a reflecting pool, and camellias, roses and azaleas all at once in April. The Thoroughbred Racing Hall of Fame is inside the grounds in a converted carriage house, also free, and includes an exhibit on the Black community's role in the local equine industry that most visitors don't expect. Then downtown — The Alley, a car-free pedestrian district off Laurens Street with a self-pour taproom, a whiskey bar, a smokehouse and an ice cream parlour — for lunch and a wander.

If your Aiken day lands on a Sunday, that changes the plan: the Aiken Polo Club's spring season runs April through June with double-header Sunday matches at Whitney Field, $10 general admission and free parking, on ground where polo has been played continuously since 1882. Call the polo hotline before you drive out — matches move for weather.

One seasonal caveat Aiken's headline horse events are March events. The Aiken Triple Crown — the Trials, the Steeplechase, Pacers and Polo — runs on three consecutive March Saturdays and will be over before you arrive. By April most winter-season trainers have shipped their horses on to Keeneland, Churchill Downs and Belmont, so morning activity at the training track is noticeably lighter than a February visit. Treat it as an atmospheric detour, not the day's main event. What is running in April: Sunday polo, the Hopelands Monday-evening concert series, and FEI-level eventing at Stable View.
Also in April

Three things worth checking dates for

  • Hopelands Concert Series — free Monday-evening lawn concerts under the oaks, April through June, opening in the first week of April. Bring chairs and a picnic; non-alcoholic drinks only. Park at the Green Boundary Club on Whiskey Road.
  • Stable View Spring International — FEI cross-country, dressage and show jumping at a 1,500-acre venue, and free for all spectators, no ticket. The 2026 edition ran 3–5 April; the spring dates land in the first week most years, so it may overlap your practice rounds.

6cWhere to eat in horse country

Aiken eats like two towns folded together: a Gilded Age winter colony, and a Midlands South Carolina barbecue town sitting in the belt of counties where hash on rice — a thick pork stew ladled over plain white rice, documented at community barbecues here since the 1850s — is not optional on a plate. Masters week is locally nicknamed "a second Christmas": hotels near full, restaurant and beverage tax receipts up about 9%. Sit-down rooms like The Willcox and Prime Steakhouse want reservations well ahead; counter-service spots and the market run close to normal.

If you want a second South Carolina stop on the same day, Redcliffe Plantation State Historic Site in Beech Island sits roughly between Augusta and Aiken — an 1859 Greek Revival house whose interpretation now centres the enslaved people who lived and worked there, with tours of the mansion and surviving slave quarters Thursday to Monday at 11, 1 and 3 ($10 adults; grounds free, 9 am–6 pm). Reservations aren't required but are strongly encouraged. Stable View is 20 minutes north of downtown Aiken.

If you have more time, or a spare afternoon

Three detours

None of these is in the four-day plan above. Each is here because it's genuinely good and because a Masters trip has more dead hours than people expect — the afternoon after a 7 am gate, or the day your badge didn't cover.

The half-day across the bridge

North Augusta, South Carolina

Drive · 5–10 min over the 13th Street bridge · half day or an evening · free

Discover South Carolina titles its own article on the place "North Augusta: A Little North of the Masters," which is about right. It is five minutes over the river and a complete change of gear: a paved seven-mile rail-trail along the water, a restored wetland with alligators in it, a colonial village staffed by volunteers, and a riverfront district built around one of the best minor-league ballparks in the country, with the Augusta skyline behind the outfield. The obvious play is a late-afternoon walk followed by dinner in Riverside Village and a sunset drink at Jackson's Bluff, the rooftop bar on top of the Crowne Plaza, where downtown Augusta catches the last light across the water.

  1. 1

    Brick Pond Park

    Forty acres of restored wetland with boardwalks past four ponds, a waterfall, a fishing dock and a wildlife-viewing pavilion. Around 110 bird species recorded, plus river otters and — repeat visitors insist — alligators up to seven or eight feet. Locals call it a miniature Okefenokee.

    Free · 30–60 min · park by the municipal building
  2. 2

    The North Augusta Greeneway

    A designated National Recreation Trail on an old Norfolk Southern rail bed, paved and fully accessible, tracing the Savannah River for several of its seven-plus miles. Includes a 180-foot bridge over Highway 230 and a repurposed 90-foot flatbed railcar serving as a bridge over Crystal Creek.

    Free · 20 min to a half day · links Brick Pond and Riverview Park
  3. 3

    Living History Park

    Seven and a half acres of recreated colonial village representing 1735–1785, built on the site of the old waterworks and run entirely by volunteers. Fifteen structures including a blacksmith's forge, a grist mill and the Spring House Tavern — the one surviving original waterworks building. Self-guided, or take the phone audio tour.

    Free · 45–90 min · check the calendar; it closes for private events
  4. 4

    Riverside Village & SRP Park

    Home of the Augusta GreenJackets, the Atlanta Braves' Single-A affiliate — voted Ballpark Digest's Ballpark of the Year in its opening season, sitting right on the river with the Augusta skyline as its backdrop. Around it: a walkable strip of restaurants and bars, a boardwalk, and the Crowne Plaza's rooftop.

The outdoor day, and a round of your own

Clarks Hill Lake & Hickory Knob

Drive · 50–75 min northwest · full day · park pass / green fee

Forty-five minutes to an hour and a quarter out of Augusta the Piedmont opens into a 71,000-acre reservoir with more than 1,200 miles of largely undeveloped shoreline straddling the state line — pine and hardwood, red-clay bluffs, quiet coves, and none of the resort-town gloss you might expect. Mistletoe State Park is the Georgia-side anchor and the easiest version of the day: 15.5 miles of trail, a sandy swim beach, kayak rentals on site, and a reputation as one of the best bass lakes in the country. April is prime for crappie, bluegill and shellcracker, and prime for the native Piedmont azalea blooming through the woods.

The other reason to come is that some of your group will want to actually hit a ball. Hickory Knob State Resort Park, over the line in McCormick, South Carolina, is the only full-service resort park in the South Carolina system: an eighteen-hole championship course where Lake Thurmond is visible or in play on every hole, plus archery, skeet, lakeside cabins and an on-site restaurant. It's about 65–75 minutes from Augusta via US-25 — a long way to drive for golf, unless you've spent three days watching other people play it.

Two things to verify before you commit. Hurricane Helene badly damaged the Corps of Engineers' recreation areas on this lake in September 2024; the last campground reopened in May 2026, so it should be fully back by your visit — check the Savannah District's site anyway. And Hickory Knob's course was closed May–September 2026 for bunker and green renovations, scheduled to reopen well before April 2027. Confirm tee times directly.

Add a night, before or after

Savannah

Drive · ~2½ hrs southeast · 1–2 nights · book lodging early

The one genuinely different trip on this page, and the one that needs a decision up front: because it's two and a half hours away, Savannah works as a pre-tournament arrival or a post-tournament decompression, but not as a day trip. Either way, book the beds as early as you book Augusta's — April is Savannah's peak month independently of the Masters, and the whole region's inventory compresses in tournament week.

What you get for the drive is a compact, ferociously walkable two square miles built on James Oglethorpe's 1733 grid of 22 public squares — live oaks, Spanish moss, monuments, and grand houses framing each one. Monterey Square is the loveliest, centred on the Pulaski Monument with the Mercer-Williams House on its edge; Chippewa Square is the Forrest Gump bench square, though the bench itself now lives in the history museum. Forsyth Park's 1858 fountain anchors the southern end, with a farmers market on the lawns on Saturday mornings. Down at the water, River Street's cobblestones and converted cotton warehouses hold seventy-odd shops and bars while container ships slide past at eye level.

For a group, the differentiator is the law: Savannah's Historic District permits open containers — one drink at a time, in a plastic cup no larger than 16 ounces, anywhere from the river to Jones Street. Most bars will pour yours to go on request. String together the rooftops (Peregrin atop the Perry Lane, Bar Julian on the 13th floor of the Thompson, Rocks on the Roof, Electric Moon in the Plant Riverside district), and finish at a dueling-piano saloon near River Street. Book a table: Mrs. Wilkes Dining Room is the essential one and it's weekdays only, 11 am–2 pm, walk-up, with a queue before opening — build the day around lunch rather than dinner. For evenings, The Grey in a restored 1938 Greyhound terminal, Husk, or the pink 18th-century mansion that is The Olde Pink House. Twenty minutes east, Bonaventure Cemetery under moss-draped oaks is the most photogenic hour in Georgia; thirty minutes east, Tybee Island has three miles of beach, a lighthouse you can climb all 178 steps of, and a crab shack under the live oaks.

The Savannah Music Festival — seventeen days of jazz, bluegrass, classical and roots across downtown venues — very likely overlaps. A third-party listing puts 2027 at 2–17 April but the festival hasn't posted its own dates; check savannahmusicfestival.org. SCAD's Sidewalk Arts Festival, which turns Forsyth Park into a chalk-art competition, falls on the last Saturday of April and so will almost certainly miss you.

Savannah's stops are deliberately left off the map below — pinning a city 130 miles away would zoom the CSRA map out to uselessness.
